Ten years ago, a three-day industry conference in another city was nothing. Fly in, stand at the stall or booth all day, network over dinner, maybe a drink or two, up early the next morning to do it again. Now you're counting down to the return flight by lunch on day two, your feet ache from standing, and the client dinner that used to energise you just feels like one more thing to get through before you can finally sit down alone.

This isn't about losing your edge at work. It's a real, physical shift in stamina that shows up specifically in the demanding, compressed pace of business travel and conferences, and testosterone decline is one genuine contributor most men never connect to it.

## Why do work trips drain you more than they used to?

**Standing, presenting, and staying socially "on" for long stretches is more physically demanding than a normal desk day, and your recovery capacity has changed.** Testosterone supports muscle endurance and the efficiency of overnight physical recovery. When levels decline, a day spent on your feet at a trade booth or in back-to-back meeting rooms leaves a deeper physical debt than it used to, one that doesn't fully clear overnight in an unfamiliar hotel bed.

**Sleep quality on the road is usually worse than at home, and that compounds everything else.** A different mattress, an unfamiliar room, a late client dinner, an early flight, these all cut into sleep even before you factor in that testosterone and sleep quality already influence each other in both directions. Skip even one night of decent sleep on a trip and the next day's stamina takes a visible hit.

### Is this the same thing as jet lag?

**No, and it's worth separating the two.** Jet lag is specifically about your body's internal clock being out of sync with a new time zone, something we've covered in detail separately for men who travel across time zones regularly. What's being described here is different: general stamina and recovery capacity for the physical and social demands of a busy work trip, even a trip within India with no time zone change at all. Both can show up on the same trip and make each other worse, but they have different causes.

## What's actually happening during a high-pressure trip?

**Cortisol rises with performance pressure, and chronically elevated cortisol works against testosterone.** Presenting to clients, closing deals, representing your company in front of senior colleagues, all of this raises stress hormones. In short bursts that's normal and even useful. Across three or four back-to-back days with poor sleep and irregular meals, sustained cortisol elevation can suppress testosterone further, making the fatigue feel worse than the schedule alone would explain.

**Client dinners add alcohol and heavy, unfamiliar food on top of an already taxed system.** A trip that involves nightly client dinners, often with alcohol expected as part of the relationship-building, adds a real physiological cost: alcohol disrupts sleep architecture even when it feels like it helps you fall asleep faster, and it has a documented, if usually temporary, suppressive effect on testosterone production.

## Planning a demanding trip: what to prioritise and when

| Preparation step | Why it matters | Best timed |
|---|---|---|
| Book the earliest reasonable check-in, not the cheapest flight | Reduces the first-night sleep debt before the trip even starts | Before you book travel |
| Pack for one solid workout or walk, even a short one | Movement genuinely offsets some of the stamina hit from standing and sitting all day | Packing stage |
| Decide in advance which dinners you'll have one drink at, not all of them | Limits cumulative alcohol and sleep disruption across a multi-day trip | Before the trip |
| Block 20-30 minutes alone each day, even in a packed schedule | Basic recovery window that lowers cumulative cortisol load | During the trip |
| Flag a genuinely rough return to your doctor if it happens repeatedly | Rules out something beyond normal travel fatigue | After the trip, if needed |

## How do you protect your energy without looking like you can't keep up?

**Frame recovery choices as professionalism, not weakness.** Skipping a nightcap, stepping out for fifteen minutes between sessions, or asking for an earlier flight home isn't a sign you can't handle the pace anymore. Senior colleagues who've been doing this for decades often make exactly these choices quietly, having learned the hard way what protects their performance over a multi-day stretch.

**Hydrate and eat on a schedule, not opportunistically.** Conference food and client dinners tend to be heavy, salty, and irregular in timing. Carrying water and eating something modest at consistent times, even if it means turning down a second plate at the buffet, keeps blood sugar and hydration more stable than grabbing whatever's available whenever there's a gap.

**Watch for a pattern, not just one bad trip.** One exhausting conference after a long stretch without travel is normal. If every work trip now leaves you disproportionately wiped out, with symptoms lingering well after you're home, that pattern is worth raising with your doctor alongside your general energy levels, rather than assuming it's simply "how travel is now."

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to feel far more drained by work trips than I used to?**
Yes, for many men in their 40s and 50s this is a genuine, common shift tied partly to declining testosterone's effect on stamina and recovery, compounded by poorer sleep quality on the road. It doesn't mean you're falling behind professionally.

**Is this the same as jet lag?**
No. Jet lag specifically concerns your body's internal clock being disrupted by crossing time zones. This is about general physical and social stamina for demanding travel, and it can happen even on a domestic trip with no time zone change at all.

**Does alcohol at client dinners really make a difference?**
It can, more than most men expect. Alcohol disrupts sleep quality even when it seems to help you fall asleep, and it has a temporary suppressive effect on testosterone, so several nights of it in a row on a trip adds up.

**When should travel fatigue prompt a doctor's visit rather than just better trip planning?**
If exhaustion after work trips is severe, doesn't improve with a few days of rest at home, or comes with other symptoms like low mood, low libido, or unexplained weight change, it's worth a broader conversation with your doctor rather than assuming it's just the travel.

Work travel isn't going away, and neither is the pressure to perform well on it. But protecting your energy deliberately, rather than white-knuckling through every trip the way you did at 30, is what actually keeps you performing well on the ones that matter most.
