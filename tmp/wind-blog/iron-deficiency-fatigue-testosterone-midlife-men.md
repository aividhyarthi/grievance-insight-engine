A man in his late forties mentions to his doctor that he is tired all the time, struggling to get through an afternoon without wanting to lie down, and assumes it must be his testosterone finally catching up with him. It is a reasonable guess, andropause and fatigue are genuinely linked. But his blood work comes back with normal testosterone and a haemoglobin count on the low side. Iron deficiency, not low testosterone, turns out to be driving most of what he is feeling, and he had never once considered it because iron deficiency gets framed in India almost entirely as a women's health issue.

## Why does iron deficiency get overlooked in men?

**Iron deficiency anaemia is widely, and understandably, associated with menstruating women, which means it rarely enters a midlife man's or his doctor's first list of suspects for persistent tiredness.** Men do not lose iron through menstruation, so the assumption is often that they simply will not run low. In practice, iron deficiency in men is less common than in women but far from rare, and when a man in his forties or fifties does become iron deficient, the cause is usually more worth investigating, not less, since there is no obvious monthly explanation covering it.

**This is exactly why unexplained iron deficiency in a midlife man deserves a proper medical workup rather than a quiet supplement fix.** New-onset low iron in a man who was not previously deficient can occasionally point toward slow internal blood loss, such as from the gastrointestinal tract, which is something a doctor needs to actively rule out rather than something to self-manage at home.

### How much overlap is there with andropause symptoms?

The symptom overlap is substantial and is the main reason this confusion happens so often. Both low testosterone and iron deficiency can independently cause persistent fatigue, difficulty concentrating, low mood, reduced exercise tolerance, and a general sense of being "flat." A man experiencing all of these can reasonably assume it is his hormones, when a basic blood count might reveal a far simpler and more fixable cause sitting alongside, or instead of, any hormonal change.

## What causes iron deficiency in midlife Indian men?

**Diet is one major factor.** Iron from plant sources (non-heme iron, found in lentils, spinach, and other greens common in Indian vegetarian diets) is absorbed by the body considerably less efficiently than iron from meat and fish (heme iron). A largely vegetarian diet is not automatically iron-deficient, but it does mean the margin for error is thinner, particularly if intake of vitamin C, which helps iron absorption, is also low, or if tea or coffee, which can inhibit iron absorption, is consumed with meals.

**Medication is another, often-missed factor.** Long-term use of proton pump inhibitors or other acid-reducing medication, commonly taken for acidity and reflux, can reduce the stomach acid needed to absorb dietary iron efficiently over months or years of continuous use. This is a particularly relevant blind spot for midlife men, since acidity medication use tends to climb in this age group alongside everything else layered into a busy, stressful life.

### Could both be happening at once?

Yes, and this is actually common. A man can have both borderline-low testosterone and iron deficiency simultaneously, with each contributing separately to how tired and low he feels. Treating only one, based on a guess rather than test results, often leaves a meaningful part of the fatigue unaddressed. This is the core argument for testing both rather than assuming either.

## What does the testing and treatment actually look like?

**A full blood count along with ferritin (the body's iron storage marker) is a simple, inexpensive blood test** that most Indian diagnostic labs offer, often alongside a testosterone panel if your doctor orders both together when you describe fatigue. Ferritin specifically tends to be a more sensitive early marker than haemoglobin alone, since haemoglobin can stay in a normal range even as the body's iron stores are being depleted.

| Finding | What it suggests | Typical next step |
|---|---|---|
| Low ferritin, normal haemoglobin | Early or mild iron deficiency | Dietary changes, possible oral iron supplement |
| Low ferritin and low haemoglobin | Iron deficiency anaemia | Oral iron supplement, source investigation if unexplained |
| Normal iron markers, low testosterone | Andropause-related fatigue more likely | Discuss testosterone management with your doctor |
| Both low | Combined contributors to fatigue | Both addressed, iron deficiency source investigated |

**If iron deficiency is confirmed, your doctor will typically recommend an oral iron supplement at a specific dose and duration**, not an indefinite open-ended habit, along with dietary guidance such as pairing iron-rich foods with vitamin C sources like citrus or amla, and avoiding tea or coffee immediately around meals. Self-starting an iron supplement without a confirmed blood test is not advisable, since excess iron intake over time can itself cause harm, and masking a genuine deficiency's underlying cause by supplementing blindly can delay finding something that needed proper investigation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can iron deficiency cause the same fatigue as low testosterone?**
Yes, the fatigue, brain fog, and low mood from each can feel very similar, which is exactly why relying on symptoms alone to guess the cause is unreliable. A blood test covering both ferritin and testosterone is the only way to tell them apart with confidence.

**Should I just start taking an iron supplement if I feel tired all the time?**
No. Taking iron without a confirmed deficiency does not help fatigue from other causes and can cause its own problems with prolonged excess intake. Ask your doctor for a blood test first.

**Why would a man suddenly become iron deficient with no obvious cause?**
This deserves a proper medical evaluation, since new iron deficiency in a man can occasionally signal slow internal blood loss that needs to be identified. Your doctor may recommend further investigation beyond a routine blood test depending on your history and other symptoms.

**Does a vegetarian diet mean I am automatically iron deficient?**
Not automatically, but the margin is thinner than with a diet that includes meat and fish. Pairing iron-rich foods like lentils, spinach, and jaggery with vitamin C sources, and limiting tea or coffee with meals, can meaningfully improve absorption.

Fatigue in midlife is rarely explained by a single cause, and testosterone, while a genuine and important piece of the picture, is far from the only one. A simple blood count alongside your testosterone test can rule iron deficiency in or out quickly, and treating the actual cause, rather than the first one that comes to mind, is what actually gets your energy back.
