For years, Diwali at Anand's house meant his mother ran the kitchen and his father supervised the guest list. Now his parents are older and live with him, and somewhere in the last few years, without anyone formally deciding it, the hosting duty quietly became his: the sweets order, the puja arrangements, standing over a stove for hours the night before, greeting forty relatives the next day while running on four hours of sleep. He used to enjoy festivals. Now he mostly wants them to be over.

This shift, where a man who grew up watching his parents host now finds himself doing the hosting, is common in Indian joint families as the older generation ages or a man becomes the head of household. What's less talked about is that this handover often lands right in the decade when a man's own energy, sleep, and recovery capacity are already changing.

## Why does hosting duty land on men specifically, and why now?

**In many Indian households, festival hosting responsibility follows a fairly predictable generational handover.** As parents age, become less physically able to manage a full day of cooking and guests, or pass away, the responsibility for organising and executing the festival often moves to the eldest son or the son living with them, sometimes shared with a wife, sometimes not. This transition tends to happen in a man's 40s or 50s, the same period when testosterone is gradually declining and recovery from physical exertion and sleep disruption takes longer than it used to.

**The mismatch is timing, not fairness.** The load itself, cooking for extended hours, standing for a full day, managing guests, isn't inherently harder than it would have been at 30. What's changed is the body doing it.

## What actually makes the physical load harder in midlife?

A few specific factors compound during a heavy hosting stretch.

**Standing and cooking for extended hours strains a body that's already losing some muscular endurance.** Muscle mass typically declines gradually from the 40s onward, partly tracking with testosterone. Long stretches on your feet in a hot kitchen, common during Diwali sweet-making or a Navratri feast, tax joints and lower-back muscles more than they did a decade ago.

**Sleep disruption compounds fatigue rather than sitting separately from it.** Late nights prepping, early mornings for puja, and houseguests staying over all cut into sleep during exactly the window when your body needs consistent rest to manage testosterone production, since a large share of daily testosterone release happens during deep sleep.

**Irregular eating during a hosting stretch adds a third layer.** Skipped or rushed meals while cooking for others, combined with festival sweets and rich food eaten standing up between tasks, disrupts the blood sugar stability that also supports steady energy and hormonal function.

## Is there a way to keep the role without the exhaustion?

You don't have to hand the whole responsibility off to protect your energy, but a few targeted changes reduce the toll significantly.

**Delegate specific tasks, not the whole event.** Most hosting duty is actually a bundle of separable jobs: shopping, cooking, cleaning, guest management, puja logistics. Assigning even two or three of these to a spouse, sibling, adult child, or hired help on the day frees up meaningful energy without you stepping back from the role entirely.

**Protect one or two sleep windows deliberately.** You may not get a full normal sleep schedule during a multi-day festival, but protecting even one full night's sleep in the run-up, rather than sacrificing all of them to preparation, meaningfully changes how you feel by the main event day.

**Eat on a schedule even while cooking for others.** Keep a plate aside and actually sit down to eat it at a normal mealtime, rather than grazing on sweets between tasks and calling that dinner.

**Build in a recovery day.** Treat the day after a major hosting event the way you'd treat the day after a hard physical effort: lighter obligations, more sleep, and not immediately diving into the next round of errands.

| Task | Traditional expectation | A more sustainable approach |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet-making and heavy cooking | Done solo, often overnight | Split across two people, or order part of it in |
| Guest hosting on the day | One person manages everything | Assign a specific person to guest logistics, greeting, seating |
| Puja arrangements | Managed personally start to finish | Delegate shopping and setup, keep only the parts that matter most to you |
| Sleep during the festival window | Sacrificed entirely for preparation | Protect at least one full night before the main event |
| Recovery after the event | Straight back into normal routine | One lighter day built in deliberately |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to dread festivals I used to enjoy?**
Yes, this is a common experience when hosting responsibility shifts onto you without your enjoyment of the occasion being renegotiated at the same time. It's worth naming this to your family directly, most relatives don't realise the load has changed hands, or how much it costs you now compared to a decade ago.

**Could low testosterone be making festival exhaustion worse?**
It's possible. Declining testosterone is associated with lower energy, slower physical recovery, and reduced tolerance for sleep disruption, all of which are stressed during a heavy hosting stretch. If festival fatigue feels disproportionate to the effort, or persists well after the event ends, it's worth mentioning to your doctor alongside your broader symptom picture.

**How do I ask other family members to share the load without conflict?**
Frame it around specific, separable tasks rather than the whole event: "Can you handle the sweets order this year" is easier for people to say yes to than "I need help with Diwali." Most resistance comes from vagueness, not unwillingness.

**Should I skip hosting duties some years if I'm not up to it?**
That's a personal and family decision, but it's a legitimate option to raise, particularly if fatigue or other symptoms have become significant. Scaling a celebration down, or rotating hosting duty to another family member for a year, is not a failure, it's pacing yourself for the years you'll still want to host.

The quiet handover of hosting duty is often as much an emotional transition, stepping into a parental role in the family, as it is a physical one. Noticing that the load has landed on a body that's also changing is the first step to carrying it sustainably rather than resentfully.
