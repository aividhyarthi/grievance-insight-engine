Many men in their 40s return to exercise using the same routine that worked in their 20s: high volume, minimal warm-up, little attention to recovery, and an assumption that soreness or a nagging niggle will resolve itself the way it always used to. It often does not, at least not as quickly, and the result is frequently a cycle of enthusiastic starts followed by injury or burnout within a few months. The issue is rarely a lack of effort. It is usually a mismatch between the routine and a body that recovers differently now, partly because of changing hormones and partly because of ordinary ageing in joints, tendons, and connective tissue.

## Why doesn't my old routine work anymore?

Recovery capacity genuinely changes with age. **Declining testosterone, part of the gradual process known as andropause, or clinically, late-onset hypogonadism (LOH), affects how efficiently the body repairs muscle tissue after training**, meaning the same workout that once required a day of recovery may now need two or three. Joint cartilage and tendon elasticity also decrease gradually over time, making high-impact or poorly warmed-up movements more likely to cause a nagging injury rather than the tolerable soreness they used to produce. None of this means training should stop or become dramatically gentler. It means the structure around training, warm-up, recovery, and progression, needs to change even if the underlying exercises stay largely the same.

## What should a sustainable weekly routine actually include?

### The four pillars

**Resistance training, at least twice a week**, remains the single most valuable form of exercise for men in midlife, directly counteracting age-related muscle loss and supporting bone density, insulin sensitivity, and the body's ability to use the testosterone it has. Compound movements, squats, presses, rows, and hinges, remain the most efficient use of training time.

**Cardiovascular work, kept moderate and consistent rather than sporadic and extreme**, supports heart health and metabolic function without adding excessive recovery demand on top of resistance training. This does not need to mean running long distances if your joints do not tolerate it well. Brisk walking, cycling, and swimming are all genuinely effective.

**Mobility and warm-up work, treated as part of training rather than optional extras.** Ten minutes of deliberate joint mobility and dynamic stretching before a session measurably reduces injury risk in this age group, and is one of the most commonly skipped elements by men used to a younger body that did not seem to need it.

**Deliberate recovery days, built into the week rather than taken only when forced by injury.** This includes actual rest days, adequate sleep, and enough protein intake to support muscle repair, all of which matter more, not less, as recovery capacity changes with age.

| Element | How often | Common mistake to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Resistance training | 2 to 3 sessions a week | Skipping warm-up and jumping straight into heavy loads |
| Moderate cardio | 2 to 3 sessions a week | Treating every session as maximum effort |
| Mobility and warm-up | Before every session, 10 minutes | Treating it as optional when short on time |
| Full rest days | 2 full days a week | Only resting once forced by injury or pain |

## Why is recovery treated as part of the program, not an afterthought?

The most common mistake among men restarting fitness after 40 is treating recovery as something that happens automatically in the background while the "real" training is what counts. **In practice, muscle and strength gains happen during recovery, not during the workout itself**, and under-recovering is one of the more common reasons men in this age group plateau or get injured despite training hard. Two full rest days a week, at least seven hours of sleep, and spacing out sessions that target the same muscle groups are not signs of insufficient commitment. They are part of what makes the training effective in the first place.

## When should I involve my doctor before starting?

If you have been sedentary for a long stretch, have existing joint issues, or have any cardiovascular risk factors, a general health check before starting a new routine is a reasonable precaution rather than an overcautious one. It is also worth asking your doctor for a testosterone blood test if you notice persistent fatigue, unusually slow recovery, or a marked drop in strength despite consistent training, since these can point to a hormonal contribution worth addressing directly rather than training around indefinitely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many days a week should a man over 40 exercise?**
A common, sustainable structure is 2 to 3 resistance sessions, 2 to 3 moderate cardio sessions, and at least 2 full rest days each week, adjusted to your starting fitness level and any existing joint or health issues.

**Why do I get injured more easily than I did in my 20s?**
Joint cartilage and tendon elasticity decline gradually with age, and recovery between sessions slows, partly due to declining testosterone. Skipping warm-up and progressing load too quickly are the most common preventable causes of injury in this age group.

**Is cardio or strength training more important after 40?**
Both matter, but resistance training has the strongest evidence for counteracting age-related muscle loss and supporting bone density and metabolic health in midlife, which is why it should not be dropped in favour of cardio alone.

**Should I get a testosterone test before starting a new exercise routine?**
Not automatically, but if you notice persistent fatigue, unusually slow recovery, or a marked strength drop that does not improve with consistent training, it is reasonable to ask your doctor for a testosterone blood test alongside a general health check.

Exercise after 40 is not about training less out of resignation. It is about training smarter, with recovery, mobility, and consistency given the same weight as effort itself. Done this way, a sustainable routine in your 40s and 50s can build real strength and health for the decades ahead, rather than the short bursts of intensity followed by setback that a copy-pasted younger routine tends to produce.
