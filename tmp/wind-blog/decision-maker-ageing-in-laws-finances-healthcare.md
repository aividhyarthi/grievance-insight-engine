When Arvind's father-in-law had a stroke, the family naturally turned to Arvind, the son-in-law with the steady job and the calm head, to sort out the hospital bills, the insurance claims, and eventually the property paperwork that hadn't been touched in a decade. His wife was too distraught to manage it. Her brother lived abroad. Arvind, at 51, became the de facto decision-maker for a household that wasn't technically his, on top of managing his own parents and his own declining energy.

This is an increasingly common but rarely named role for midlife Indian men: becoming the practical authority over an in-law's finances and healthcare, often by default rather than by any formal arrangement. It carries real weight, real stress, and often no acknowledgment at all, and it lands at exactly the life stage when a man's own hormonal and physical resilience is already declining.

## Why does this responsibility so often land on the son-in-law?

**In many Indian families, the son-in-law is seen as the "outsider with the steady head": less emotionally entangled than the biological children, often financially literate, and available.** When an ageing in-law's health crisis or financial complexity hits, it's frequently easier for the family to lean on him than to have a harder conversation about which sibling should take the lead, particularly if the wife's siblings live in another city or abroad.

This isn't inherently unfair, and many men take on the role willingly out of genuine care for their in-laws. **What makes it heavier is the lack of formal acknowledgment.** There's rarely an explicit conversation establishing that this is now his job, what its boundaries are, or how long it's expected to last. He simply becomes the person everyone calls, indefinitely, without the authority or recognition that a formally appointed decision-maker would have.

### The stress of navigating a family you didn't grow up in

**Managing your own parents' care is hard enough. Managing your in-laws' care adds a layer of complexity**: you often don't know the full financial picture, the family history, or the old grievances between siblings that can surface during a health crisis or an inheritance conversation. You're making significant decisions, sometimes about property or major medical procedures, within a family system whose unwritten rules you're still learning, even after years of marriage.

This can create a particular kind of stress: high responsibility paired with limited authority or context, which is a well-documented driver of chronic workplace stress and applies just as much within a family setting.

## How does this connect to testosterone and midlife health?

**Sustained responsibility-driven stress keeps cortisol elevated over weeks and months**, particularly when the stress involves ongoing uncertainty, like an in-law's fluctuating health condition or a drawn-out property dispute. Elevated cortisol over time suppresses testosterone production through a well-established hormonal pathway, on top of whatever age-related decline is already underway in your 40s and 50s.

**Men in this role commonly report the same cluster of symptoms as other chronic caregivers**: disrupted sleep from managing calls and hospital visits at odd hours, reduced time for exercise, irritability at home that seems disconnected from anything your own family did, and a general sense of being stretched too thin. These are consistent with both chronic stress and declining testosterone, and the two often reinforce each other.

| Aspect of the role | Common impact | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Financial decision-making | Anxiety, decision fatigue | Involve a financial advisor or CA where possible, don't carry it all mentally |
| Medical decision-making | Stress from uncertainty, guilt over choices | Get a second medical opinion when decisions are major, share the decision with your spouse explicitly |
| Family dynamics | Tension with in-laws or their siblings | Set clear boundaries early on what you will and won't take on |
| Your own health | Deprioritised, skipped check-ups | Schedule your own annual health check the same week as any major in-law health event |

## How do you share the load without causing family conflict?

**The clearest way to reduce the strain is an explicit conversation, not an assumed arrangement.** Sitting down with your spouse and, where possible, her siblings to agree who handles what, whether that's finances, medical appointments, or day-to-day check-ins, prevents the quiet resentment that builds when one person carries everything by default.

**If siblings live abroad or in another city, their contribution doesn't have to be physical presence.** Splitting financial contributions, handling specific paperwork remotely, or taking turns with phone check-ins are all legitimate ways to share the load that don't require relocating. Naming this explicitly, rather than letting distance become an unspoken excuse, matters.

**It's also worth being honest with your spouse about your own capacity.** Loving your in-laws and being willing to help doesn't mean you have unlimited bandwidth, especially if you're also managing your own parents, your job, and your own health. Saying "I can handle the finances but I need your brother to handle the day-to-day hospital visits" is a reasonable boundary, not a betrayal.

## What about your own health in the middle of all this?

**This is the piece that gets dropped first, almost always.** Your own annual health check, your own testosterone or general blood work if you've been having symptoms, your own exercise routine and sleep, all tend to slide when you're managing someone else's crisis. The irony is that this is exactly the period when your body most needs that consistency.

**A practical habit**: whenever you're scheduling an in-law's medical appointment or health check, book your own for the same week. It's a small structural trick, but it prevents your own health from becoming the thing that's always "next month."

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to feel resentful about taking on this role, even though I care about my in-laws?**
Yes, this is a very common and understandable reaction, and it doesn't mean you don't care about them. Resentment usually builds not from the caregiving itself but from the lack of acknowledgment, shared responsibility, or clear boundaries around the role. Naming the resentment honestly, ideally with your spouse, is more productive than suppressing it.

**How do I raise the topic of sharing responsibility without seeming like I'm complaining?**
Frame it around practical logistics rather than emotion: "I think we should map out who handles what for your parents, so nothing falls through the cracks" lands very differently from an accusation. Most family members respond better to a structured proposal than to an emotional confrontation, even if the underlying feeling is the same.

**Can this level of stress actually affect my testosterone levels?**
It can contribute to it, yes. Chronic stress from ongoing high-stakes responsibility keeps cortisol elevated, and sustained high cortisol is linked to lower testosterone production. Combined with disrupted sleep and less time for exercise that often comes with this role, it's a reasonable contributor to fatigue, low mood, or reduced libido on top of any age-related hormonal decline.

**Should I get a formal power of attorney or legal authorization for my in-laws' affairs?**
For financial and property matters especially, formal documentation protects everyone, including you, from disputes or ambiguity later. It's worth discussing with a lawyer or financial advisor what level of formal authorization makes sense given your specific family situation, rather than operating purely on informal trust indefinitely.

Being the quiet backbone of your in-laws' care is a real and demanding role, even when nobody calls it that. Recognising the toll it takes, and actively protecting your own health and boundaries within it, isn't selfish. It's what makes the role sustainable for as long as your family needs you in it.
