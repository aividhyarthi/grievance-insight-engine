Deepak, 48, used to read the morning paper over chai and move on with his day. Now he finds himself lying in bed at midnight, scrolling through one grim headline after another, a market crash, a conflict overseas, a local crime story, unable to stop and unable to shake the low, heavy feeling it leaves behind. He isn't more politically engaged than he was a decade ago. If anything, he reads less analysis and more raw headlines, and somehow feels worse for it. If bad news hits harder and lingers longer than it used to, there's a real physiological reason, not just a worse news cycle.

## Why does bad news feel so much worse now than it used to?

**The news itself, on average, isn't meaningfully worse than it was ten or fifteen years ago, what's changed for many midlife men is their capacity to absorb distressing information without it leaving a residue.** Falling testosterone is associated with lower overall mood resilience and a reduced buffer against low-grade, repeated stress for many men, which means the same disturbing headline that once prompted a shrug now sits with you for hours.

### The mechanism behind doomscrolling specifically

**Each distressing headline triggers a small stress response, and scrolling to the next one before the first has resolved means the body never gets the chance to come back down, so the stress accumulates across a session rather than peaking and fading.** This is true for anyone at any age, but a body already running a higher baseline of cortisol from work pressure, poor sleep, or family responsibilities has less room to absorb yet another layer of unresolved stress signals before it tips into genuine low mood or irritability the next day.

## Is this actually connected to andropause, or just a worse habit?

Both, and they reinforce each other in a way that's worth understanding.

**Reduced patience, lower mood resilience, and a smaller buffer against repeated stress are recognized features of andropause for many men, and doomscrolling is a near-perfect trigger for all three, since it delivers exactly the kind of repeated, unresolved, low-control stimulus that a depleted system handles worst.** A man in his 30s with a higher hormonal and stress reserve might read the same headlines and move on; the same man a decade later, with less reserve, finds the same content sitting much heavier.

## Does doomscrolling actually affect testosterone, or just mood?

**There's no direct evidence that scrolling news itself lowers testosterone, but the chronic stress and poor sleep that heavy late-night scrolling produces are both independently linked to lower testosterone over time, which makes the habit worth addressing on its own terms.** The relationship runs in a loop: falling testosterone lowers your tolerance for stress and bad news, scrolling bad news late at night raises cortisol and disrupts sleep, and disrupted sleep and chronic stress further suppress testosterone. Breaking the loop anywhere helps the whole cycle.

## What actually helps, without going cold turkey on the news?

**Setting two or three specific windows a day for checking news, rather than allowing it to be an always-available background activity, dramatically reduces the cumulative stress load without requiring you to disengage from current events.** A man who checks news for fifteen minutes at breakfast and fifteen minutes in the early evening stays informed just as well as one who scrolls for two hours spread across the day, with a fraction of the accumulated stress.

**Keeping the phone outside the bedroom, or at minimum ending news consumption at least an hour before sleep, protects both the stress-resolution window and sleep quality, two things that compound each other if both are left unprotected.** This single change is frequently the one that makes the biggest difference, since night-time scrolling combines the worst of both problems, unresolved stress and disrupted sleep, in the same half hour.

| Habit | Effect on stress load | Effect on sleep |
|---|---|---|
| Checking news in short set windows | Lower, bounded exposure | Minimal impact |
| Scrolling continuously through the day | Higher, cumulative exposure | Moderate fatigue from fragmented attention |
| Scrolling in bed before sleep | Highest, unresolved stress carried into the night | Significant delay in sleep onset |
| Reading a single curated summary once daily | Lowest exposure, most control | No meaningful impact |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can doomscrolling actually lower testosterone?**
Not directly, but the chronic stress and disrupted sleep it produces are both independently linked to lower testosterone over time, so the habit is worth addressing even without a direct causal link.

**Why does bad news bother me more now than it did ten years ago?**
Falling testosterone in midlife is linked to reduced mood resilience and a smaller buffer against repeated stress for many men, so the same volume of distressing content lands harder than it once did. It's a real physiological shift, not a sign of becoming thin-skinned.

**Is it better to quit reading the news entirely?**
Not necessarily. Bounded, scheduled news checking, two or three short windows a day, keeps you informed with far less cumulative stress than open-ended scrolling, without the social or practical downsides of disengaging from current events completely.

**When does news-related anxiety need professional support?**
If low mood, dread, or irritability from news consumption persists for days, spreads into unrelated parts of your life, or feels disproportionate to what you're reading, it's worth discussing with a doctor or a mental health professional rather than treating it as a phone habit alone.

Deepak moved his phone charger to the kitchen instead of the nightstand. He still reads the news, just not at midnight, and not in an endless loop. He says the headlines haven't gotten any kinder, he's just stopped handing them his last waking hour of the day.
