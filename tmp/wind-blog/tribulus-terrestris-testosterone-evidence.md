It has a long traditional-medicine history and a prominent place in modern bodybuilding and "testosterone booster" marketing alike, which makes it worth separating what it is traditionally used for from what current clinical research actually supports.

## What is tribulus terrestris actually used for traditionally?

**Tribulus terrestris, known as gokhru in Hindi and used across both Ayurvedic practice and other traditional medicine systems, has historically been used for general vitality, urinary tract support, and as a tonic herb, rather than specifically as a hormone-altering agent in its traditional context.** Its modern reputation as a testosterone booster is a comparatively recent development, driven largely by supplement marketing from the 1990s onward rather than by longstanding traditional claims specifically about testosterone.

## Does it actually raise testosterone?

**This is the specific claim worth examining most carefully, because it is the one driving most tribulus supplement sales, and the honest answer is that most rigorous clinical trials in men have not found a significant testosterone-raising effect.** Several placebo-controlled studies measuring testosterone levels before and after tribulus supplementation in healthy or mildly symptomatic men found no meaningful difference compared to placebo. **A smaller number of studies, often with methodological limitations like small sample sizes or lack of blinding, have reported more favourable results, which is part of why the topic occasionally resurfaces in marketing material despite the weight of better-quality evidence pointing the other way.**

### Where the evidence is slightly more interesting

**Separate from testosterone itself, a handful of studies have reported improvements in libido or self-reported sexual satisfaction with tribulus use, in both men and women, even without a corresponding change in testosterone levels.** This suggests that if tribulus has any genuine effect, it may work through a different mechanism, possibly related to nitric oxide pathways or other factors affecting sexual response, rather than through raising testosterone as the marketing implies. This remains an area of limited, inconclusive research rather than an established finding.

| Athletic performance / strength | Little to no consistent benefit shown | Low |

## Is it safe to try, even without strong testosterone evidence?

**Tribulus terrestris is generally considered reasonably well tolerated in short-term use for most healthy adults, but "reasonably well tolerated" is not the same as "risk-free for everyone."** It has documented mild diuretic effects, which matters for anyone with kidney conditions or who is on other medications affecting fluid balance. **There are also case reports, though rare, of liver effects with certain tribulus products, and as with most herbal supplements sold in India, quality, standardisation, and actual tribulus content can vary meaningfully between brands, since India's supplement market does not regulate herbal products with the same rigour as prescription medication.** Independent testing of herbal supplements in the Indian market has periodically found gaps between labelled and actual ingredient content, which is a reason for realistic caution rather than blanket avoidance.

**If you are on testosterone replacement therapy or being evaluated for low testosterone, tell your doctor before adding tribulus or any other supplement.** This matters particularly here, since a man might reasonably but mistakenly attribute a change in how he feels to tribulus rather than to his actual prescribed treatment or another factor entirely, complicating how his care is monitored and interpreted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is tribulus terrestris the same as gokhru used in Ayurveda?**
Yes, tribulus terrestris is the botanical name for the plant known as gokhru in Hindi and used across Ayurvedic and other traditional medicine systems, primarily for vitality and urinary health rather than specifically for raising testosterone.

**Why is it marketed so heavily as a testosterone booster if the evidence doesn't support it?**
Supplement marketing often outpaces the underlying clinical evidence, and tribulus became closely associated with testosterone-boosting claims largely through bodybuilding and sports supplement marketing from the 1990s onward, a reputation that persisted even as more rigorous research generally failed to confirm the claim.

**Could tribulus help with libido even if it doesn't raise testosterone?**
Some limited studies suggest a modest effect on libido or sexual satisfaction independent of testosterone, though the evidence remains inconclusive and the mechanism isn't well understood. It should not be relied upon as a treatment for a significant, persistent change in libido, which deserves proper medical evaluation.

**Is it safe to combine with testosterone replacement therapy?**
There's no established evidence it meaningfully interacts with TRT, but you should still tell your doctor before combining them, both for general safety screening and so any change in how you feel can be attributed accurately.

Tribulus terrestris has a genuine traditional history, but its modern reputation as a testosterone booster runs well ahead of what current clinical research actually supports. If declining testosterone is a real concern, a proper blood test and a conversation with your doctor remain the more reliable path than a supplement bottle promising a shortcut.
