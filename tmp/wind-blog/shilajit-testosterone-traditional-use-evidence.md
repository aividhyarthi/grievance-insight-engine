Shilajit has become one of the most aggressively marketed substances in the men's vitality supplement space in India, often sold in thick jars with dramatic before-and-after claims about strength, stamina, and testosterone. Strip away the marketing language, though, and shilajit is actually a well-documented substance in classical Ayurvedic texts, used for a very long time for reasons that only partly overlap with what today's supplement bottles promise. Understanding both the traditional context and the current research helps separate what is genuinely known from what is simply sold hard.

## What is shilajit, actually, and what was it traditionally used for?

**Shilajit is a sticky, tar-like substance that forms over centuries from the slow decomposition of plant material compressed within Himalayan and other mountain rock layers, and it has been used in Ayurvedic medicine for well over a thousand years.** Classical texts describe it as a rasayana, the same broad category ashwagandha falls into, used traditionally for general strength, vitality, healthy ageing, and as an adjunct to other treatments rather than as a single-purpose remedy. It contains a complex mixture of fulvic acid, minerals, and organic compounds, and traditional Ayurvedic practice has always emphasised that its quality and effect depend heavily on its source and purification process, something the modern loosely regulated market often glosses over.

**The narrow modern framing of shilajit specifically as a "testosterone booster" is largely a product of contemporary supplement marketing rather than how it was traditionally understood or prescribed.** In classical use, it was combined with other herbs, adjusted for an individual's constitution, and prescribed by a practitioner as part of a broader treatment approach, not sold as a single standardised capsule for a single hormonal outcome.

## What does the actual research say about shilajit and testosterone?

**A small number of clinical studies, most involving purified, standardised shilajit extract rather than raw or commercially variable products, have found modest increases in testosterone in healthy adult men over study periods of around 90 days.** These studies are genuinely interesting but limited: sample sizes have generally been small, and most existing research does not specifically focus on men with clinically diagnosed low testosterone or andropause, which limits how directly the findings apply to that population.

**Some research has also looked at shilajit's effects on general markers of fatigue, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial function, areas where the traditional "vitality" framing arguably has more research support than the testosterone-specific claim does.** This mirrors a pattern seen with other Ayurvedic herbs like ashwagandha, where the modern marketing narrows a broad traditional use down to a single hormonal claim that the evidence supports only partially.

### Purity is the real practical concern

**Because shilajit is a naturally sourced substance, contamination with heavy metals, particularly lead, arsenic, and mercury, has been documented in some unregulated or improperly purified commercial products, which is a genuine safety issue distinct from the question of whether it works.** Studies using purified, pharmaceutical-grade shilajit go through processing steps specifically designed to remove these contaminants, and this is not something a typical commercial jar sold in an Indian pharmacy or online marketplace can be assumed to have undergone. This is one of the more serious, specific quality concerns in the supplement pillar of andropause care.

## How should a man approach this practically?

**Anyone considering shilajit should talk to their doctor first, particularly if they are on any prescription medication or being evaluated or treated for low testosterone, since interactions and quality concerns both need to be weighed alongside any potential benefit.** A doctor cannot necessarily vouch for a specific commercial product's purity, but they can flag interaction risks with existing medications and ensure shilajit use does not delay or complicate a proper diagnostic workup for symptoms that might actually need medical treatment.

**Consulting a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner, rather than choosing a product based on marketing claims or price alone, matters more with shilajit than with many other supplements because of the documented purity variability in the unregulated market.** A practitioner can advise on sourcing, appropriate traditional preparation, and whether shilajit fits a person's individual constitution and health picture, which is a meaningfully different approach from picking a jar off a pharmacy shelf because an advertisement promised results.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is shilajit safe to take alongside testosterone replacement therapy (TRT)?**
This should only be decided in conversation with your doctor. There is limited research on interactions between shilajit and prescribed testosterone treatment, and self-combining supplements with hormonal medication without medical guidance carries real risk, including complicating how your treatment response is monitored.

**How is purified shilajit different from what's sold in most Indian shops?**
Purified, pharmaceutical-grade shilajit used in clinical research goes through specific processing to standardise its composition and remove contaminants like heavy metals. Many commercially available products have not been through equivalent testing or purification, which is why sourcing and product quality genuinely matter here.

**Does shilajit work faster than other Ayurvedic herbs like ashwagandha for testosterone?**
There isn't good comparative research directly answering this, and both should be understood as showing modest effects over sustained use, typically studied over around 90 days, rather than fast-acting remedies. Neither should be expected to produce quick, dramatic results.

**Can I just try shilajit myself without telling my doctor if I feel generally low energy?**
It is safer and more responsible to mention it to your doctor first, especially given documented contamination risks in some products and the possibility that persistent low energy has a medical cause worth properly diagnosing rather than self-treating around.

Shilajit sits in a similar place to several other Ayurvedic substances covered here: a genuinely old, broadly used traditional remedy with a narrower, still-developing body of modern research behind the specific claim most heavily marketed today. Purity and medical guidance matter more with shilajit than with many alternatives, given the documented contamination risk in India's unregulated supplement market.
