A man struggling with brain fog, forgetting names mid-conversation or losing his train of thought in meetings, often ends up searching for memory supplements, and ginkgo biloba is usually one of the first names that comes up. It has decades of use as a memory and circulation aid, sold everywhere from pharmacies to general stores. Some men, having found it for brain fog, then wonder whether it might also help the fatigue and low drive that come with andropause.

This article looks at what ginkgo biloba is actually studied for, what the evidence shows for memory and blood flow, and why the leap to testosterone does not hold up.

## What is ginkgo biloba actually studied for?

**Ginkgo biloba comes from one of the oldest living tree species, and its leaf extract has been used in traditional Chinese medicine for centuries before becoming a mainstream supplement in the West and in India.** Modern research has focused mainly on two areas: cognitive function, particularly memory and processing speed, and blood flow, since ginkgo appears to have a mild effect on dilating blood vessels and reducing blood stickiness.

**The strongest evidence sits specifically in people with existing cognitive impairment, not in healthy adults looking for a general brain boost.** Several studies and reviews have found modest benefits for memory and daily functioning in people with mild cognitive impairment or early dementia when taking standardised ginkgo extract over several months. The picture is far less convincing for healthy, non-impaired adults, where multiple well-designed trials have found no meaningful difference in memory or cognitive performance compared to placebo.

## Does ginkgo biloba help with the brain fog of andropause?

**This is where the evidence gets genuinely murky, because andropause-related brain fog hasn't itself been studied with ginkgo as a specific treatment.** The research base is either in healthy young adults (where ginkgo mostly doesn't show a clear benefit) or in people with diagnosed cognitive impairment (where it shows modest benefit). Midlife men with hormone-related brain fog fall into neither group cleanly, so any benefit is speculative rather than demonstrated.

**What is more clearly supported is ginkgo's mild effect on blood flow, which has a plausible, if indirect, connection to how alert and clear-headed a man feels.** Better circulation to the brain could theoretically support concentration and mental clarity, independent of any specific hormonal mechanism. This is a reasonable, modest expectation, not a guarantee of sharper thinking.

| "Ginkgo helps erectile function" | Some small, older studies suggest modest benefit via blood flow, not hormones, results are inconsistent |

## Does ginkgo biloba affect testosterone?

**No controlled human study has found that ginkgo biloba raises or meaningfully affects testosterone levels.** The claim appears to be another case of a supplement's genuine, narrow reputation (memory and circulation) getting stretched into a much broader "hormone support" claim once it is marketed alongside testosterone boosters on the same shelf or the same website.

**This is worth being specific about, because it is easy to confuse with the different, Ayurvedic herb Brahmi (Bacopa monnieri), which is also marketed for memory in midlife men but comes from an entirely different botanical and research tradition.** Ginkgo and Brahmi are sometimes used almost interchangeably in casual conversation about "brain herbs," but they are different plants with different evidence bases, and neither has meaningful human research linking it to testosterone.

## The bleeding risk that matters more than most people realise

**Ginkgo's effect on blood flow comes with a real downside: it can increase bleeding risk, particularly when combined with blood thinners, aspirin, or other medications that affect clotting.** This is one of the more well-documented interaction risks among commonly used herbal supplements, and it is not theoretical. Case reports exist of excessive bleeding in people combining ginkgo with anticoagulant medication, and it is generally recommended to stop ginkgo at least a week or two before any planned surgery.

**For midlife men, this matters more than it might for a younger person, simply because more men in their 40s, 50s, and 60s are on blood thinners or aspirin for heart health, or are more likely to need a surgical procedure in any given year.** If either applies to you, this is a supplement to specifically mention to your doctor, not assume is harmless because it is sold without a prescription.

### India's supplement market caution

As with most herbal supplements sold in India, ginkgo biloba products vary in concentration, standardisation, and purity across brands, since India's supplement market is loosely regulated compared to prescription medicine. A product that doesn't clearly state its standardised extract concentration is harder to trust for consistent dosing, which matters more for a supplement with a real interaction risk like ginkgo than for a simple vitamin.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I take ginkgo biloba alongside testosterone replacement therapy (TRT)?**
There is no documented direct interaction between ginkgo and testosterone medication, but you should still tell your doctor you are taking it, so your full medication and supplement list is on record, particularly given ginkgo's bleeding risk profile if you are also on other medications.

**How long does it take to notice any effect from ginkgo?**
Studies that found benefit in people with cognitive impairment generally used it consistently for several months, often 12 to 24 weeks, before seeing measurable change. It is not a same-day or same-week effect, and expecting a quick result is likely to lead to disappointment.

**Is ginkgo biloba the same as Brahmi?**
No, they are different plants from entirely different traditional medicine systems, Ginkgo biloba from East Asian tradition and Brahmi (Bacopa monnieri) from Ayurveda. Both are marketed for memory, but they have separate evidence bases and neither has established testosterone effects.

**Should I try ginkgo for andropause-related brain fog?**
Discuss it with your doctor first, especially if you take any blood-thinning medication or have a bleeding disorder. If your doctor is comfortable with you trying it, treat it as a possible, modest aid for mental clarity, not a treatment for the underlying hormonal changes, which still deserve proper evaluation.

Ginkgo biloba has a genuine, if modest, place in the research on memory and blood flow, particularly for people with diagnosable cognitive decline. It has no meaningful evidence behind it for testosterone, and its bleeding risk is real enough to deserve a conversation with your doctor before you add it to your routine.
