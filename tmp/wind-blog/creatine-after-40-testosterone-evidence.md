A man in his late 40s notices he is lifting less than he did five years ago, and recovers slower after the gym than his 25-year-old training partner. Somewhere along the way, he hears that creatine, the supplement he associates with college gym bros and bodybuilding forums, might actually be one of the better-studied options for men his age. That sounds suspicious, and it is worth asking whether it holds up.

It largely does, though not in the way the marketing implies. Creatine is one of the most extensively researched supplements in sports science, and a meaningful part of that research base now includes older adults specifically, not just young athletes. But its connection to testosterone itself is much thinner than its connection to muscle and strength.

## Is creatine actually a "testosterone booster"?

**No, not based on the current clinical evidence, despite how it is sometimes marketed alongside testosterone-support products.** A small, frequently cited 2006 study in young rugby players found a rise in dihydrotestosterone (DHT, a more potent derivative of testosterone) after creatine loading, and that single result has been repeated in marketing copy for nearly two decades. Subsequent, larger studies, including several in older men, have generally failed to replicate a consistent, meaningful testosterone or DHT increase from creatine supplementation. The honest summary is that creatine's hormonal effects, if any exist at all, are small and unreliable, not a dependable lever for raising testosterone.

## What does the evidence actually support, then?

**Where creatine is genuinely well-supported is muscle strength, power output, and lean mass, particularly in adults over 50 who combine it with resistance training.** Multiple randomised controlled trials and several meta-analyses specifically in older adults show that creatine supplementation, alongside a structured strength-training programme, produces greater gains in muscle mass and strength than training alone. This matters directly for andropause-related muscle loss (sarcopenia), which is driven by declining testosterone, reduced protein synthesis, and often less consistent training as life gets busier in your 40s and 50s.

**There is also a growing, though newer and less settled, body of research on creatine and brain function in ageing adults**, including some signal for cognitive performance under fatigue or sleep deprivation. This is a genuinely interesting area, but it is still early, and it should not be oversold as an established brain-health benefit the way the strength research can be described with more confidence.

### What creatine doesn't do

Creatine will not meaningfully move a man's testosterone blood test result. It will not substitute for resistance training, protein intake, or sleep, all of which matter more for muscle preservation in midlife. And it is not a treatment for diagnosed low testosterone or late-onset hypogonadism (LOH), which requires proper medical evaluation.

## Who should be cautious, and what should an Indian man check before buying?

**Creatine monohydrate is considered one of the more thoroughly safety-tested supplements for healthy adults, but "healthy" is doing real work in that sentence.** Men with existing kidney disease, or those on medications that affect kidney function, should not start creatine without their doctor's clearance, since creatine supplementation increases the workload the kidneys process, even though it does not appear to damage healthy kidneys in the research to date. Adequate water intake also matters more when taking it.

**India's supplement market is loosely regulated compared to pharmaceuticals, and creatine products vary meaningfully in purity and quality between brands.** Independent testing of sports supplements sold in India has periodically found products with fillers, inconsistent actual creatine content, or contamination, none of which is reliably visible from the packaging alone. This is not a reason to assume every product is compromised, but it is a reason not to treat all brands as interchangeable, and to be skeptical of unusually cheap products or those making exaggerated hormonal claims.

**Before starting creatine, or any supplement, tell your doctor, especially if you are on testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) or other regular medication.** This is not just a formality: your doctor needs a complete picture of what you are taking to interpret blood work correctly and to catch any interaction risk, even one that seems unlikely on paper.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does creatine cause hair loss or affect testosterone negatively?**
The concern usually traces back to the DHT finding in that one older rugby-player study. It has not been consistently replicated, and there is no strong clinical evidence that creatine meaningfully worsens hair loss or lowers testosterone in most men. If you have a family history of hair loss and remain concerned, raise it with your doctor rather than avoiding creatine on that basis alone.

**Is creatine safe for men over 50?**
The research specifically in older adults is reassuring for healthy individuals, with strength and muscle benefits when paired with resistance training. The main caveats are kidney disease, certain medications, and the general rule of checking with your doctor first, particularly if you have any chronic condition.

**Will creatine help with andropause symptoms like fatigue or low libido?**
Not directly. Its evidence base is about muscle strength and mass, not fatigue or libido specifically. If those are your main concerns, they are worth raising directly with your doctor rather than expecting a supplement marketed for gym performance to address them.

**Do I need to cycle creatine or take a "loading phase"?**
Loading protocols and cycling are common gym folklore, but the research does not show that either is necessary for creatine to work; consistent daily use is what the evidence actually supports. Specific dosing decisions are best made with medical or qualified sports-nutrition guidance rather than following generic advice off a product label or a gym trainer's personal routine.

Creatine's real story is less dramatic than the marketing, and arguably more useful: a well-studied supplement for muscle strength in ageing bodies, not a hormone booster. For a midlife man losing strength, that is still a meaningful thing to understand clearly, and to discuss with a doctor before adding it to a routine that may already include other medication or treatment.
