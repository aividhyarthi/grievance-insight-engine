A disagreement comes up over something small, perhaps money, or a decision about the children, or an in-law visit, and instead of raising his voice the way he might have in his thirties, a man in his late 40s simply stops talking. He answers in short sentences for the rest of the evening, finds a reason to be in another room, and the matter is never actually resolved, just quietly buried. His wife has started calling it "going into his cave." He would call it keeping the peace. Both descriptions are capturing the same pattern, and it is worth understanding why it tends to show up, or intensify, in midlife.

## Is going quiet during an argument actually a problem, or is it normal?

**It depends almost entirely on what happens next.** Taking a brief, clearly communicated pause to calm down before continuing a difficult conversation is a well-supported, healthy strategy, even recommended by relationship researchers, since arguing while physiologically flooded with stress hormones rarely produces a useful outcome. The trouble is a different, more corrosive pattern: consistently going silent, refusing to re-engage, giving one-word answers for hours or days, and leaving your partner to wonder whether the issue was ever actually heard. Relationship researcher John Gottman's long-running studies on marriage identified this pattern, which he termed stonewalling, as one of the clearest predictors of a marriage's long-term unhappiness when it becomes a man's default response to conflict rather than an occasional, bounded pause.

## Why might this pattern show up or get worse in midlife specifically?

**Several things tend to converge in a man's 40s and 50s that make withdrawal a more likely response than it was earlier in a marriage.** Work stress is often at its peak, financial responsibility for the household (and sometimes ageing parents) is heaviest, and physical fatigue is more noticeable than it used to be, all of which reduces the emotional bandwidth available for a difficult conversation after a long day. **Lower testosterone has been associated in research with increased irritability, reduced frustration tolerance, and a flatter emotional range for some men**, which can make a genuine attempt at conversation feel disproportionately draining, and silence can start to feel like the only manageable option rather than a deliberate choice to punish or ignore a partner.

### The upbringing factor many men don't name out loud

**Many Indian men were raised in households where showing frustration, sadness, or vulnerability, especially to elders, was treated as inappropriate or weak, and where a father's silence during conflict was simply the model available to copy.** That upbringing does not disappear with age; if anything, under stress, the oldest, most practiced coping pattern tends to resurface first. Recognising that the habit was learned, often before you had any say in it, can make it easier to actively choose something different now rather than treating it as simply your fixed personality.

## What does withdrawal actually cost a marriage over time?

**The research is fairly consistent: a partner on the receiving end of repeated stonewalling tends to report feeling dismissed, unimportant, and increasingly lonely within the relationship, even when the withdrawing partner intends the silence as a way to avoid saying something hurtful.** Unresolved conflict does not disappear when it goes unspoken, it tends to resurface in the same argument weeks later, or to quietly build into a broader sense of disconnection that is harder to repair than the original disagreement would have been.

| Pattern | What it looks like | Effect on the marriage over time |
|---|---|---|
| A brief, named pause | "I need twenty minutes, then let's talk" | Healthy, reduces the chance of saying something regretted |
| Consistent silent withdrawal | Short answers, leaving the room, days of distance | Partner feels dismissed, issue resurfaces unresolved |
| Silence used to punish | Deliberately prolonged coldness after a disagreement | Erodes trust, often the most damaging version |
| Re-engaging after cooling down | Returning to finish the conversation calmly | Builds trust that conflict can be resolved safely |

## What actually helps a man break this pattern?

**Naming the pattern to your spouse, outside the heat of an actual argument, is often the single most useful first step.** A calm conversation along the lines of "I notice I go quiet when things get tense, and I know that's hard on you, I want to work on it" tends to land very differently than the silence itself does, and it opens the door to agreeing on a practical system, such as a specific phrase either partner can use to request a short, time-bound pause rather than an open-ended retreat. **If fatigue, irritability, or a flattened mood are part of what is driving the withdrawal, and especially if those symptoms have been building for months alongside other andropause symptoms, a conversation with your doctor about your testosterone and overall health is a reasonable, practical step, not an overreaction.** If the pattern is tangled up with ongoing low mood, anxiety, or a sense of hopelessness about the marriage, a counsellor, whether individually or as a couple, can help in a way that a single good conversation at home sometimes cannot. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it bad to need time alone after an argument with my spouse?**
Not inherently. Taking a short, clearly communicated pause to calm down before continuing a conversation is a healthy, well-supported strategy. The concern is a consistent pattern of indefinite silence or refusing to re-engage at all, which relationship research links to long-term marital dissatisfaction.

**Could low testosterone actually be making me more likely to shut down during conflict?**
It is plausible as a contributing factor. Lower testosterone has been associated with increased irritability, reduced stress tolerance, and flatter mood in some men, which can make emotionally difficult conversations feel more draining than they used to. It does not fully explain the pattern on its own, since upbringing and habit play a large role too, but it is worth mentioning to your doctor if withdrawal has intensified alongside other symptoms.

**How do I ask for space without my spouse feeling shut out?**
Say it explicitly and attach a time frame: "I need twenty minutes to calm down, then I want to finish talking about this" communicates very differently than simply going silent and leaving the room. The specificity is what reassures a partner that the issue has not been dismissed.

**When should a couple consider counselling for this kind of pattern?**
If withdrawal has become the default response to most disagreements, if issues are consistently left unresolved, or if either partner feels persistently lonely or dismissed within the marriage, a counsellor can help build a healthier communication pattern. This is especially worth considering if the withdrawal is tangled up with low mood or anxiety that one-on-one conversation at home has not been able to shift.
