## Why do video calls feel more tiring than in-person meetings?

**Video calls demand more sustained cognitive effort than face-to-face conversation, largely because the brain has to work harder to read normal social cues through a flattened, sometimes delayed, medium.** Small lags in audio, a narrower field of view that cuts off body language below the shoulders, and the unnatural stillness required to stay in frame all add up to a kind of low-grade cognitive strain across a full day of back-to-back calls. This isn't a new observation specific to midlife men. Researchers studying "Zoom fatigue" during and after the pandemic found it affects people broadly, but the effect can be more noticeable for a man whose baseline energy reserves are already lower than they were at 30.

**For men experiencing andropause-related fatigue, brain fog, or disrupted sleep, the extra cognitive tax of video calls can compound an already-thin energy budget, making a heavy meeting day feel disproportionately depleting compared to a similarly busy day of calls or in-person conversation.** This isn't imagined or exaggerated. It's a real interaction between a documented cognitive load and a genuinely reduced capacity to absorb it.

## Why does seeing yourself on screen bother you more now?

### The mirror that never used to be there

**Continuous self-viewing during video calls is a genuinely unusual experience that almost no previous generation of working men dealt with for hours a day, and it can sharpen self-consciousness about physical changes a man might otherwise not dwell on.** A slightly worse camera angle, poor lighting, or simply spending forty minutes looking at your own tired face while trying to concentrate on what a colleague is saying is a specific, modern source of low-grade discomfort that has nothing to do with vanity and everything to do with an unfamiliar cognitive demand.

**This can intersect with broader midlife body-image shifts, noticing greying hair, a fuller face, tired-looking eyes, in a way that feels more pointed than catching a glimpse in a mirror, because the exposure is prolonged, repeated daily, and often visible to colleagues at the same time.** For a man already managing some self-consciousness about ageing, this can add a layer of workplace-specific discomfort that's rarely discussed openly.

### It's not just you

**Surveys of remote and hybrid workers have found self-consciousness about appearance on video calls is common across age groups, but midlife men report it less often out loud, partly because it can feel like a trivial thing to admit to compared to more "legitimate" work stress.** Naming it plainly, rather than assuming it's an unusual personal quirk, is often the first useful step.

## Is this connected to testosterone, or just modern work life?

**Video call fatigue itself is largely a cognitive and technological phenomenon that affects people of all ages and hormone levels, so it isn't caused by declining testosterone.** What andropause-related changes can do is reduce the energy reserve a man has to absorb that fatigue without it showing, meaning the same meeting-heavy day that was manageable at 32 can leave a man in his late 40s noticeably more drained, irritable, or foggy by evening. Similarly, mood and confidence changes that sometimes accompany andropause, lower motivation, flatter affect, more self-critical thinking, can make ordinary self-viewing on a screen feel more uncomfortable than it would with a steadier baseline mood.

**If video call exhaustion is part of a broader pattern that includes persistent fatigue, low mood, reduced motivation, or noticeably lower confidence across other areas of life, it's worth mentioning to your doctor alongside other andropause symptoms rather than dismissing it as purely a "work thing."** The two are often intertwined rather than separate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Zoom fatigue a real, studied phenomenon?**
Yes. Researchers, including a well-known Stanford study, have identified specific mechanisms behind video call fatigue, including excessive close-up eye contact, cognitive load from limited mobility on camera, and the unusual demand of constant self-viewing. It's a recognised effect, not a personal weakness.

**Why do I feel more self-conscious on camera than I used to?**
Continuous self-viewing during calls is a genuinely new experience for working professionals, and it can sharpen awareness of physical changes like greying hair or a fuller face that you might not otherwise dwell on. This is common and not a sign of vanity or a deeper problem on its own.

**Can I just turn off my camera for most meetings?**
For many meetings, yes, and it's increasingly normal to do so, particularly for larger or less interactive calls. Reserve video for conversations where visual presence genuinely adds value, and don't feel obligated to default to video for everything.

**Should I mention video call fatigue to my doctor?**
If it's an isolated annoyance, probably not necessary on its own. If it's part of a broader pattern of fatigue, brain fog, low mood, or reduced confidence, it's worth raising alongside those symptoms so your doctor has the full picture.

Video calls aren't going away from working life, but the fatigue and self-consciousness they bring aren't fixed facts either. A few practical adjustments, combined with an honest look at your broader energy and mood, can make a meeting-heavy day feel considerably more manageable.
