A sudden, gripping pain under the right ribs after a heavy, ghee-laden meal, one that comes in waves and sometimes radiates to the shoulder blade, sends a fair number of midlife Indian men to the emergency room every year. More often than not, the diagnosis is gallstones: small, hardened deposits that form in the gallbladder and occasionally block its outlet.

Gallstones aren't traditionally framed as a hormone issue, and in most cases, the hormone connection is modest at best. But the same midlife changes that affect testosterone, weight gain, insulin resistance, and shifting fat distribution, also raise gallstone risk, which is why the two conditions show up together often enough to be worth untangling properly.

## Why do gallstones become more common after 40?

**Age itself is one of the clearest risk factors for gallstones**, and the reasons are mostly mechanical and metabolic rather than hormonal in the testosterone sense. As men age, the gallbladder empties less efficiently, cholesterol concentration in bile tends to rise, and years of dietary habits accumulate their effect. Add in the weight gain that's common in midlife, particularly visceral fat around the abdomen, and the risk climbs further, since excess cholesterol is one of the main raw materials gallstones form from.

**Rapid weight loss is a less obvious but genuine risk factor too.** Crash dieting or very low-calorie diets, sometimes adopted abruptly by midlife men alarmed by a health scare, can actually increase gallstone risk in the short term, because the liver releases extra cholesterol into bile during fast fat breakdown. This is one reason doctors generally recommend gradual, sustained weight loss over aggressive short-term diets.

## What's the actual connection to testosterone and weight?

**There isn't strong direct evidence that low testosterone itself causes gallstones.** What exists instead is a shared-risk-factor relationship: the visceral fat gain and insulin resistance that commonly accompany declining testosterone in midlife men are the same conditions that independently raise gallstone risk. Metabolic syndrome, the cluster of high blood sugar, high blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol, and abdominal obesity that's closely linked to low testosterone, is also a well-established gallstone risk factor in its own right.

**In practical terms, this means a man dealing with both declining testosterone and gallstones is often looking at the same underlying driver, excess weight and metabolic dysfunction, from two different angles**, rather than one condition causing the other directly. Addressing the shared cause, through diet, activity, and weight management, tends to help both pictures, which is a more useful frame than searching for a single hormonal culprit.

## What are the warning signs you shouldn't ignore?

Many gallstones cause no symptoms at all and are discovered incidentally during an unrelated ultrasound. When symptoms do occur, the classic pattern is a steady or cramping pain in the upper right abdomen, often triggered by a fatty meal, that can last from thirty minutes to several hours.

**A different, more serious pattern needs urgent attention.** Severe, unrelenting abdominal pain combined with fever, chills, vomiting, or yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice) can signal a blocked bile duct or gallbladder infection, both of which are medical emergencies. In this situation, don't wait for a routine appointment or try home remedies; call 112 or get to the nearest emergency room immediately.

## How are gallstones treated, and does losing weight help or hurt?

**Diagnosis is usually straightforward**, through an abdominal ultrasound, and treatment depends on whether the stones are causing symptoms. Silent, symptom-free gallstones are often simply monitored. Symptomatic gallstones, or ones causing complications, are typically treated with laparoscopic surgery to remove the gallbladder (cholecystectomy), a common and generally well-tolerated procedure. In India, the cost of laparoscopic gallbladder removal at a private hospital typically ranges from roughly ₹60,000 to ₹1,50,000 depending on the city and hospital, with government hospitals and insurance coverage often reducing this significantly; checking your health insurance policy's coverage for laparoscopic procedures before scheduling is worth doing.

On the weight question, the honest answer is both: **being overweight raises gallstone risk, but losing weight too quickly also raises it.** The safest approach, and the one most doctors recommend, is gradual, steady weight loss (roughly half a kilogram to a kilogram per week) rather than aggressive crash dieting, along with a diet that doesn't swing between extremes of very high fat and near-zero fat.

| Risk factor | Direction of risk |
|---|---|
| Being overweight or obese | Increases risk |
| Very rapid weight loss or crash dieting | Increases risk, short term |
| Gradual, sustained weight loss | Generally protective over time |
| High-fibre diet with regular meals | Protective |
| Prolonged fasting or skipping meals | Can increase risk |
| Family history of gallstones | Increases risk, non-modifiable |

## What this means for your diet, practically

**A diet that supports steady weight and blood sugar control tends to support gallbladder health and testosterone-relevant metabolic health at the same time.** Regular meal timing (rather than long gaps followed by large meals), a consistent intake of fibre from vegetables, fruits, and whole grains or millets, and moderate rather than extreme fat intake are the practical, boring, and genuinely useful levers here. There's no special "gallstone diet" supplement or food that reliably dissolves existing stones; if you already have symptomatic gallstones, dietary changes help manage symptoms and prevent new stones, but they aren't a substitute for medical evaluation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does low testosterone cause gallstones?**
Not directly, as far as current evidence shows. The link is through shared risk factors, mainly excess weight and insulin resistance, that affect both testosterone and gallstone risk rather than one condition causing the other.

**Can I dissolve gallstones with diet or home remedies instead of surgery?**
Generally not reliably. Some stones can be managed with medication in specific situations under medical supervision, but there's no proven home remedy or diet that reliably dissolves established gallstones, and relying on one while ignoring worsening symptoms can be dangerous.

**Is gallbladder removal surgery safe, and will I need lifelong diet changes afterward?**
Laparoscopic gallbladder removal is a common, generally safe procedure with a short recovery for most men. Many people experience no major long-term dietary restriction afterward, though some notice mild sensitivity to very fatty meals and manage this by moderating fat intake.

**Should I avoid ghee and fried food entirely to prevent gallstones?**
Complete avoidance isn't usually necessary for most men; moderation and consistent meal timing matter more than eliminating any single food. If you already have symptomatic gallstones, your doctor may suggest tighter fat restriction in the short term.

Gallstones and declining testosterone often show up in the same midlife man for the same underlying reason: accumulated weight and metabolic change, not because one causes the other. Manage the shared cause through gradual, sustainable habits, and treat sudden severe abdominal pain, fever, or jaundice as the emergency it is.
