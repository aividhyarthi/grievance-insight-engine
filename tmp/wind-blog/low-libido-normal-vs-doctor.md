A change in sex drive is one of the symptoms men are least likely to mention to a doctor, even though it is one of the more common and most directly connected to declining testosterone. Some of that hesitation is cultural, some of it is simple discomfort discussing something so personal, and some of it comes from not knowing whether what you are experiencing is even worth raising. It is worth raising. A meaningful, persistent drop in libido is a legitimate medical symptom, not a private failing, and it deserves the same straightforward attention you would give any other change in your body.

## Why does libido naturally change with age?

Sex drive is influenced by many factors, and testosterone is one of the more significant ones. **Levels decline gradually for most men from around age 30 onward**, typically by 1 to 2 percent a year, and a modest, gradual softening of libido over decades is a normal part of that process for many men. This is different from a sudden or severe drop, and it does not mean every man will experience clinically significant symptoms. Many men remain sexually healthy and satisfied well into their 60s and 70s without needing any medical intervention at all.

## What counts as a meaningful change versus normal variation?

The distinction that matters most is not the presence of some change, but its degree, speed, and impact. **A gradual, mild reduction in interest over many years, without distress and without other symptoms, generally sits within the range of normal ageing.** A more relevant pattern to raise with your doctor looks different: a marked, relatively sudden drop in interest, a change that arrived alongside fatigue, low mood, or reduced muscle strength, or a change that is causing you real distress or affecting your relationship. Erectile changes specifically, difficulty achieving or maintaining an erection, are also worth mentioning separately, since they can have overlapping but distinct causes from libido itself, including vascular and psychological factors alongside hormonal ones.

| Pattern | Usually normal ageing | Worth a doctor's evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Speed of change | Gradual, over several years | Sudden or noticeably fast |
| Other symptoms | None | Fatigue, low mood, or reduced strength alongside it |
| Emotional impact | Minimal distress | Causing real distress or relationship strain |
| Erectile function | Unaffected | New difficulty achieving or maintaining an erection |

## What else besides testosterone could be causing this?

Low testosterone is one possible explanation, but it is far from the only one, and a good evaluation should not assume it is the answer before checking. **Chronic stress, depression and anxiety, relationship difficulties, certain medications including some blood pressure treatments and antidepressants, poor sleep, heavy alcohol use, and underlying conditions like diabetes or cardiovascular disease can all reduce libido independently of testosterone**, and several of these can coexist. This is precisely why a proper conversation and appropriate tests matter more than assuming you already know the cause.

## How do I bring this up without the embarrassment?

Bringing this up does not require an elaborate explanation or an emotional conversation you are not ready to have. **A simple, direct statement works well**: "I have noticed a real drop in my sex drive over the last several months, and I would like it looked into, including a testosterone test." Doctors hear this regularly, and a factual, symptom-based framing is usually easier for both sides than either avoiding the topic entirely or over-explaining. If you feel more comfortable writing it down beforehand so you do not have to find the words in the moment, that is a reasonable way to prepare for the appointment.

## When should I see a doctor about it?

If the change has been significant, sudden, or is bothering you, whatever the cause turns out to be, it is worth a direct conversation with your doctor rather than quiet self-diagnosis or, worse, unregulated online remedies. Ask specifically for a morning testosterone test, and mention any other symptoms you have noticed, along with current medications, since some commonly prescribed drugs can affect libido as a side effect that is easily overlooked. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**At what age does libido typically start to decline in men?**
For most men, a gradual decline begins around age 30 and continues slowly over subsequent decades. The pace and degree vary widely, and many men do not notice a significant change until well into their 40s or 50s, if at all.

**How do I know if my low libido is due to testosterone or something else?**
You generally cannot tell from the symptom alone. A morning testosterone blood test, alongside a discussion of stress, sleep, medications, and mood, is what actually distinguishes a hormonal cause from the several other common causes.

**Is it normal to feel embarrassed talking to my doctor about this?**
Yes, it is a very common feeling, but doctors address this regularly and are not judging you for raising it. A short, factual statement about what you have noticed and for how long is usually all that is needed to move the conversation forward.

**Can medication be causing my low libido?**
Yes, several commonly prescribed medications, including some blood pressure treatments and antidepressants, can reduce libido as a side effect. Mentioning your current medications to your doctor helps rule this in or out alongside a hormonal evaluation.

A change in libido is a normal thing to notice and a reasonable thing to want answers about. It does not require justification, and it is not a reflection of your worth or masculinity. Getting a proper evaluation, rather than guessing or staying silent, is the more direct route to actually feeling better, whatever the underlying cause turns out to be.
