## Why is supplement quality so inconsistent in India?

**Dietary supplements in India are regulated under food safety law, administered by the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI), rather than under the stricter drug regulation that applies to prescription medicines.** This distinction matters enormously. A prescription drug typically requires extensive clinical trial data before approval. A supplement generally needs to meet food-safety standards and labeling rules, which is a meaningfully lower bar for proving what's actually in the product matches what's on the label, let alone whether it does anything the marketing claims.

### What this means in practice

**The practical result is a market where legitimate, carefully manufactured products sit on the same shelf as poorly made ones, with very little to visually distinguish them.** Both might carry similar-looking claims and packaging. The difference lies in manufacturing practices, batch testing, and ingredient sourcing that aren't visible to a buyer standing in a pharmacy, which is precisely why relying on the label alone is not a reliable way to judge quality.

**An FSSAI license number confirms a product is registered to be sold as a food product in India, it does not confirm the product contains what the label says in the amount stated, or that it's free of contaminants.** It's a necessary baseline, not a quality certification. Genuinely higher-quality brands often go further, publishing third-party lab test results (sometimes referenced as certificate of analysis or COA) that independently verify ingredient content, which is a meaningfully stronger signal than anything printed directly on the packaging by the manufacturer itself.

## What are the real risks of an unregulated or poorly made supplement?

**The most documented risk isn't that a weak supplement simply does nothing, it's that some poorly regulated products have been found to contain undisclosed ingredients, including steroids, prescription drug compounds, or heavy metal contamination, none of which appear on the label.** This is a particular concern for products marketed aggressively as "testosterone boosters" or rapid muscle-gain formulas, categories where the temptation to quietly add an active ingredient for a noticeable effect is highest, and independent oversight is thinnest.

| Risk category | What to watch for | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Undisclosed active ingredients | Products with unusually strong, fast effects for a "natural" supplement | May contain unlisted steroids or drug compounds not accounted for in dosing |
| Heavy metal contamination | Ayurvedic or herbal products from unverified small manufacturers | Poor sourcing or processing can introduce lead, mercury, or arsenic |
| Dosage inconsistency | No batch testing disclosed, inconsistent effects between bottles | Actual ingredient content may vary significantly from what's labeled |
| Drug interactions | Any supplement taken without informing your doctor | Can interact with TRT, blood pressure medication, or other prescriptions |
| Import without verification | Products bought online from unverified international sellers | May not meet even the baseline FSSAI requirements applicable in India |

**None of this means every supplement is dangerous, most reputable, established brands invest genuinely in quality control because their business depends on it.** It means the burden of verification sits more with the buyer in India than it would with a prescription medicine, and treating a supplement purchase with the same casualness as buying a snack is a mistake.

## Why does my doctor need to know if I'm just taking a supplement?

**Even supplements with genuine evidence behind them, like vitamin D, zinc, or magnesium, can interact with medications or affect existing health conditions, and a doctor can only account for that if they actually know you're taking them.** This matters even more for anyone on testosterone replacement therapy or other prescribed treatment, since certain supplements can influence how the body processes hormones or medication, in ways that aren't always predictable from ingredient lists alone.

**Many men don't mention supplements to their doctor because they don't think of them as "real" medicine, which is exactly the assumption that creates risk.** Bringing the bottle itself to an appointment, rather than trying to describe it from memory, gives your doctor the clearest picture of what you're actually taking.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does an FSSAI license mean a supplement is safe and effective?**
No. It confirms the product is registered to be sold as a food item in India, but it doesn't independently verify that the product contains what the label claims or in the stated amount. It's a baseline requirement, not a quality or safety certification.

**How can I tell if a supplement brand actually tests its products?**
Look for a published certificate of analysis or third-party lab testing referenced on the brand's website or packaging, ideally from an independent lab rather than the manufacturer's own facility. Brands that genuinely test their products are usually willing to make that information easy to find.

**Are Ayurvedic or herbal supplements safer than synthetic ones?**
Not automatically. Herbal products can be well made or poorly made just like any other supplement category, and some documented contamination cases (particularly heavy metals) have involved herbal products from smaller, less rigorously monitored manufacturers.

**Do I really need to tell my doctor about a supplement if it's "natural"?**
Yes. Natural ingredients can still interact with prescribed medication, including TRT, and your doctor can only account for that interaction risk if they know what you're taking. Bring the bottle or a photo of the label to your appointment.
