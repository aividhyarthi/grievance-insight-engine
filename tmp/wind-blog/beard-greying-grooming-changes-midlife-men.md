Suresh noticed it first at his sideburns, then along his jawline: patches of white coming in faster than he expected, in a beard that had been mostly black just a couple of years earlier. He's not thinning on top, his hairline is fine, but his beard looks like it belongs to someone ten years older. He's not imagining the mismatch, and he's not alone in noticing it.

Beard greying often runs ahead of scalp greying in men, and it tends to accelerate through the 40s and into the 50s. Some of this is simply the biology of ageing pigment cells. Some men also notice their beard growing more slowly, feeling coarser, or growing in patchier than before, changes that sit at the edge of what's purely cosmetic and what may connect to hormonal shifts happening at the same time.

## Why does a beard go grey before, or faster than, the hair on your head?

**Greying happens because melanocyte stem cells in the hair follicle gradually stop producing pigment, and this process doesn't happen at the same rate everywhere on the body.** Each hair follicle has its own population of pigment-producing cells, and they wear out on their own timeline. Facial hair follicles, for reasons researchers don't fully understand, often show this ageing effect earlier or more visibly than scalp follicles in many men, which is why a full head of dark hair can sit above a noticeably salt-and-pepper beard.

**This is a genuinely different process from hair loss, and different again from hormonal hair changes.** Greying is about pigment. Thinning or loss is about the hair growth cycle shrinking. The two often get lumped together as "getting old," but a doctor evaluating either one is looking at different biology.

## Is beard greying connected to testosterone at all?

This is where it gets more nuanced than most grooming articles let on.

**The greying process itself is not primarily testosterone-driven.** Genetics, oxidative stress on pigment cells, and simple chronological ageing are the dominant factors. Men with identical testosterone levels can grey at very different rates, and greying continues in men on testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) too.

**Beard growth rate and density, separate from colour, are androgen-sensitive.** Facial hair follicles are more responsive to androgens (testosterone and its more potent derivative, DHT) than scalp follicles are, which is part of why beard growth accelerates during puberty. As testosterone gradually declines through midlife, some men do notice their beard growing in more slowly or feeling a bit less dense than it once did, alongside the colour change, even though the two processes have different causes.

**Texture changes are common and usually benign.** Grey and white hairs, in beards as on scalps, tend to be structurally coarser and drier than pigmented hair, due to changes in the hair shaft's keratin structure, not testosterone. This is why a greying beard often feels wirier even before it's fully white.

## What should actually prompt a visit to the doctor?

Most beard change after 40 is cosmetic, not medical. But a few patterns are worth flagging.

| Change | Likely just ageing | Worth a doctor's look |
|---|---|---|
| Gradual, even greying over years | Yes | No |
| Beard feels coarser or drier | Yes | No |
| Slower growth, alongside other symptoms (fatigue, low libido, mood changes) | Sometimes hormonal | Yes, worth discussing with a broader symptom picture |
| Sudden patchy loss in defined spots | No | Yes, could indicate an autoimmune condition |
| Beard greying paired with rapid overall greying and unexplained weight loss | No | Yes, worth ruling out thyroid or other systemic causes |

**Sudden, patchy hair loss in the beard, distinct from gradual thinning, is usually not an andropause issue.** It's more often associated with a condition called alopecia areata, an autoimmune process, or occasionally a fungal infection. Either way, it's a reason to see a doctor rather than wait it out.

## What are men actually doing about it?

**Some men lean into it, others reach for a dye or trim it shorter.** There's no health reason to colour a greying beard, it's purely a personal or professional choice, and beard dyes marketed for this purpose are generally considered safe for occasional use, though a patch test is wise given that skin sensitivity can also shift with age. Others find that keeping the beard trimmed shorter reduces the visual impact of coarser, wirier grey hair, since shorter length hides the texture change better than length does.

**If a slowing beard is part of a bigger pattern, that's the more useful thing to raise with your doctor.** A beard growing in more sparsely on its own, with everything else normal, usually isn't worth chasing medically. But if it's showing up alongside fatigue, reduced libido, mood changes, or unexplained muscle loss, it's worth mentioning as one data point in a conversation about a testosterone blood test, not as the headline complaint on its own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does low testosterone cause a beard to go grey?**
No, not directly. Greying is driven by the ageing of pigment-producing cells in the hair follicle, a process that's largely independent of testosterone. Testosterone can influence how thick and fast a beard grows, but not its colour.

**Can testosterone replacement therapy reverse a grey beard?**
No. TRT can support beard density and growth rate in some men with clinically low testosterone, but it does not restore pigment to hair that has already gone grey, and greying will typically continue with age regardless of treatment.

**Why is my beard greying faster than the hair on my head?**
This is common and not fully understood, but is thought to relate to facial hair follicles ageing on a different timeline than scalp follicles. It's not a sign of anything wrong, just a normal variation in how greying plays out across the body.

**Is it safe to dye a greying beard regularly?**
Beard-specific dyes are generally considered safe for periodic use, but facial skin can become more sensitive with age, so a patch test before each new product and choosing formulations designed for facial hair rather than scalp dye is a reasonable precaution.

A greying, changing beard is one of the more visible markers of midlife, and for most men it's simply cosmetic ageing playing out on schedule. If it's showing up alongside other symptoms you've been noticing, that's worth mentioning to your doctor as part of the bigger picture, not something to chase down on its own.
