This scene has become familiar across India's IT, finance, and manufacturing sectors, and it lands on midlife men with a weight that a 28-year-old losing a first job simply does not carry.

## Why a layoff hits harder at 45 than it did at 28

**The financial stakes are simply higher in midlife.** A home loan EMI, a child's school or college fees, ageing parents who may depend on you, and a lifestyle built over two decades of steady income all sit on top of the job loss itself. There is rarely the safety net of living with parents or having minimal obligations that made career setbacks easier to absorb in your twenties.

**There is also an identity cost that is easy to underestimate.** A senior designation, a team that reported to you, a seat at the table in client meetings, these things quietly become part of how a man sees himself. Losing them is not just a loss of income; it is a loss of a role, and in a joint-family setting where you may be expected to still look and act like "the provider" in front of relatives, that loss can feel impossible to admit out loud.

**Ageism in hiring makes the practical path back harder, too.** Many Indian employers, openly or quietly, favour younger and cheaper candidates for mid-level roles, which means job searches for men over 45 often take longer and involve more rejection than earlier in their careers, adding months of uncertainty on top of the initial shock.

## The cortisol-testosterone seesaw: what job loss does to your hormones

**A sudden, major stressor like a layoff activates the body's stress response, raising cortisol, and chronically elevated cortisol is known to suppress the signalling pathway the brain uses to tell the testes to produce testosterone.** This is a real physiological mechanism, not just a figure of speech. For a man already in his 40s or 50s, whose testosterone is naturally declining a little each year, an extended period of high stress can push symptoms like fatigue, low libido, irritability, and poor sleep further than age alone would explain.

**This effect is generally temporary and tends to ease once the acute stress resolves, whether that means finding new work, stabilising finances, or simply the passage of time and support.** It is not a reason to assume you now have a permanent hormonal problem, but it is a reason to take the stress of this period seriously as a health issue, not just a career setback.

## The emotional toll nobody warns you about

**Shame is often the heaviest part, heavier than the financial math.** Many men delay telling extended family, invent reasons for being home during work hours, or avoid neighbourhood gatherings where the question "so what are you doing these days?" feels unbearable. This kind of prolonged secrecy and isolation adds its own stress on top of the job loss itself, and it can tip into a deeper low mood or depression, which is a separate and serious concern from situational stress.

If you notice persistent hopelessness, loss of interest in things you normally enjoy, or thoughts of harming yourself, that is not something to manage alone. ## A practical playbook for the first few months

**Financial triage comes first, before anything else.** Sit down, often with your spouse, and map out exactly how many months your savings can realistically cover, which expenses can pause, and whether any loan restructuring conversation with your bank is needed early rather than after a missed payment.

**Tell your family sooner than feels comfortable.** Most men report that the anticipation of the conversation was worse than the conversation itself, and keeping it hidden from a spouse or adult children for weeks tends to add stress rather than protect anyone.

**Protect your sleep, movement, and daily structure deliberately.** Unemployment removes the forced routine a job provides, and it is easy to drift into irregular sleep and sedentary days that worsen both mood and energy. Treat the job search itself like a structured part of your day, with fixed hours, rather than something that bleeds into every waking moment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does job loss actually lower testosterone?**
The stress response triggered by a major life event like a layoff can temporarily suppress testosterone production through elevated cortisol. This is usually situational and tends to ease as the acute stress resolves, rather than being a permanent change caused by the job loss itself.

**How long is it normal to feel low after being laid off?**
Some sadness, anger, or anxiety for the first few weeks is a normal reaction to a major loss. If exhaustion, loss of interest, or low mood persists well beyond that, lasting most days for several weeks, it is reasonable to speak with a doctor rather than wait it out.

**Should I get a testosterone test if I feel constantly exhausted after losing my job?**
It can be worth discussing with your doctor, especially if fatigue, low libido, and irritability persist even after your stress levels have stabilised. A blood test can help clarify whether age-related testosterone decline is contributing alongside the situational stress.

**How do I talk to my children about losing my job?**
Most family counsellors suggest being honest in age-appropriate terms rather than inventing a cover story, since children often sense that something is wrong even when adults try to hide it, and secrecy can add confusion and anxiety for them too.

A layoff in midlife is genuinely one of the harder things a man can go through, financially, socially, and physically. Taking the hormonal and emotional side of it seriously, alongside the practical job search, is not a sign of weakness. It is the sensible way through a period that puts real stress on your body as well as your bank balance.
