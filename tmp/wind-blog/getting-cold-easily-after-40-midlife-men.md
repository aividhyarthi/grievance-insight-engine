Most conversations about andropause and temperature focus on the opposite problem, sudden hot flashes and night sweats. But plenty of midlife men report the reverse: they're the one asking to turn the office air conditioning down, keeping a jacket at their desk in October, or noticing their hands and feet stay cold long after everyone else has warmed up. If heat isn't your issue and cold is, you're not imagining it, and it deserves the same honest explanation.

This article covers why some men become more cold-sensitive in midlife, how it connects to testosterone and circulation, and when persistent cold intolerance is worth a doctor's attention rather than just another sweater.

## Why would low testosterone make you feel colder, not hotter?

**Muscle tissue is metabolically active and generates a meaningful amount of body heat.** Testosterone supports muscle mass, and as levels decline, many men gradually lose lean muscle, especially if activity levels have also dropped. Less muscle means less baseline heat production, which can leave you feeling chillier in conditions that never used to bother you, an office at 22 degrees, an early morning walk, a plain cotton kurta on a mild evening.

**Circulation to your extremities may also become less efficient.** Testosterone plays a role in vascular function, and some men notice their hands and feet specifically feel cold even when their core body temperature is normal, a pattern often described as poor circulation. This is the same underlying system involved in the heat-regulation problems some men experience, just showing up in the opposite direction.

### Can the same man get both hot flashes and feel cold easily?

**Yes, and it's more common than you'd expect.** Testosterone's role in temperature regulation isn't one-directional. Some men experience sudden internal heat surges at certain times, particularly at night, while generally running colder than they used to during the day. The underlying issue, a less stable thermoregulatory system, can produce both patterns in the same person.

## What else could be causing it, besides testosterone?

**Thyroid function is the most important thing to rule out first.** An underactive thyroid is a well-established, common cause of persistent cold intolerance, and it becomes more common with age in both men and women. Since thyroid problems and low testosterone can produce overlapping symptoms, including fatigue and cold sensitivity, a proper blood panel that checks thyroid function alongside testosterone gives a much clearer picture than guessing.

**Anaemia and poor cardiovascular fitness both worsen cold sensitivity too.** Low iron levels reduce your blood's ability to carry oxygen efficiently, which can leave extremities feeling cold, and general deconditioning, less muscle and less cardiovascular fitness together, compounds the effect regardless of the exact hormonal cause.

## Cold sensitivity: what's typical, what's worth checking

| What you're noticing | Likely a mild, common shift | Worth a doctor's attention |
|---|---|---|
| Reaching for a jacket a bit earlier in the season than before | Common with age and reduced muscle mass | Not urgently |
| Cold hands and feet specifically, core otherwise fine | Fairly common | Mention at your next checkup |
| Feeling cold constantly, even in warm rooms others find comfortable | Less typical as a mild shift | Yes, worth checking thyroid and testosterone |
| Cold intolerance alongside fatigue, weight gain, or dry skin | Suggestive of thyroid involvement | Yes, get tested |
| Sudden onset of severe cold sensitivity | Uncommon for gradual ageing alone | Yes, see a doctor promptly |

## What actually helps?

**Rebuild some muscle mass through regular resistance training.** Since muscle is a genuine source of body heat, strength training a few times a week, something we've covered in detail separately, can meaningfully improve baseline warmth over a few months, on top of its other well-established benefits for testosterone and general health.

**Layer practically rather than just tolerating discomfort.** Keeping a light jacket or shawl at your desk, dressing in layers you can adjust through the day, and prioritising warm extremities, socks, closed shoes rather than sandals in cooler months, are simple, unglamorous fixes that genuinely help day to day.

**Get a full panel done rather than assuming it's "just ageing."** If cold sensitivity is new, persistent, or comes with fatigue, weight change, or dry skin, ask your doctor for both a thyroid panel and a testosterone check. Treating the wrong cause, or assuming there's no treatable cause at all, means staying uncomfortable longer than necessary.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is feeling cold more easily a real symptom of andropause, or am I imagining it?**
It's a real, if less discussed, pattern for some men. Reduced muscle mass and circulatory changes linked to declining testosterone can genuinely lower how efficiently your body generates and retains heat.

**Can the same person experience both hot flashes and feeling cold easily?**
Yes. Testosterone's role in temperature regulation affects the whole system, not just one direction, so some men experience sudden heat surges at certain times while generally running colder during the day.

**Should I get my thyroid checked as well as my testosterone?**
Yes, it's a sensible first step. An underactive thyroid is a common, well-established cause of cold intolerance and can overlap with low testosterone symptoms, so testing both gives a far clearer picture than assuming one cause.

**What's the fastest way to actually feel warmer day to day?**
Resistance training to rebuild muscle mass tends to help meaningfully over a few months, and in the short term, practical layering and keeping hands and feet warm make a genuine difference while you address any underlying hormonal or thyroid issue.

Running cold instead of hot doesn't get talked about as much as hot flashes do, but it's just as real a shift for the men who experience it. A proper checkup, not just another layer of clothing, is what actually gets to the bottom of it.
