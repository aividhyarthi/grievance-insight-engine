Floor seating is everywhere in Indian life: a pooja at home, a langar at the gurdwara, a wedding mandap, a family lunch on the floor at a grandparent's house, sitting on a durrie during a condolence visit. A man who sat cross-legged without a second thought for thirty years finds himself at 45, after twenty minutes on the floor, needing to grab a chair's armrest and haul himself up while his knees crack and his lower back groans. It can feel embarrassing in a room full of people who seem unaffected. It is also an extremely common, well-explained part of midlife joint and muscle change.

This article looks at why floor-sitting gets harder specifically after 40, how much of it is actually about testosterone versus simple mechanics, and what genuinely helps.

## Why does floor-sitting get harder specifically after 40?

**Hip and ankle flexibility decline with age.** Cartilage and connective tissue gradually lose elasticity, synovial fluid in the joints reduces, and the ankle range of motion needed to fold the legs comfortably shrinks, especially in men who spend most of the day in chairs. **Weaker supporting muscles make the position harder to hold.** The glutes, hip flexors, and core all tend to weaken with less daily use, which makes maintaining the seated position tiring and makes pushing back up to standing noticeably harder without using the hands for support.

### The desk job factor

Most Indian men in midlife now spend their working day in an office chair, unlike earlier generations who routinely sat and worked on the floor. That quiet disappearance of daily floor-sitting "practice," independent of ageing itself, is a large part of why today's 45-year-olds struggle with something their fathers managed easily into their seventies.

## Is this about testosterone, or is it just mechanical?

**Mostly mechanical, with testosterone playing a supporting role.** Muscle mass maintenance depends partly on adequate testosterone, so declining levels can accelerate the loss of hip and core strength that floor-sitting requires. But the primary driver is disuse and connective tissue ageing, a pattern that affects men and women alike regardless of hormone levels. Struggling to sit cross-legged is not, on its own, a diagnostic symptom of andropause.

## When floor-sitting discomfort is something more

**General stiffness that eases with movement and regular stretching is usually normal ageing**, nothing more concerning than that. **Sharp pain, pain on only one side, clicking accompanied by pain rather than just a harmless crack, swelling, or discomfort that does not ease shortly after standing up** are a different pattern, and worth a doctor's evaluation for possible arthritis, a meniscus issue, or other joint pathology, rather than something to simply push through with more stretching.

## What actually helps

**Daily hip and ankle mobility work**, even just a few minutes of hip flexor stretches, seated forward folds, and ankle circles, restores range of motion gradually over weeks. **Practising short periods of cross-legged sitting regularly**, rather than only attempting it occasionally at family functions, rebuilds tolerance faster than stretching alone. **Targeted glute and core strength work** makes both sitting and standing back up considerably easier. **A cushion under the hips** reduces strain while that strength and flexibility are being rebuilt, and there is no shame in using one, or a low stool, at a family gathering. **Managing weight around the midsection** also helps, since extra abdominal weight makes the folded position mechanically harder to hold comfortably.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is struggling to sit cross-legged after 40 a sign of low testosterone?** Not on its own. It is far more commonly a mechanical issue from reduced hip and ankle flexibility and weaker supporting muscles, which affects men and women alike. Testosterone decline can contribute indirectly through muscle mass loss, but it is rarely the main cause.

**Why do some older relatives seem to sit cross-legged easily while I struggle at a younger age?** Lifetime habit matters enormously. Someone who has sat on the floor daily for decades, for meals, prayer, or work, retains more hip and ankle range of motion than someone who switched entirely to chairs and sofas, regardless of age.

**Can stretching actually reverse this, or is it a permanent part of ageing?** For most men, regular hip and ankle mobility work genuinely improves floor-sitting comfort within a few weeks, even starting in midlife. It will not necessarily return a man to the flexibility he had at 20, but meaningful improvement is realistic.

**When should I see a doctor instead of just stretching more?** If pain is sharp, one-sided, accompanied by swelling, or does not ease shortly after standing up, that pattern suggests a joint or cartilage issue rather than simple stiffness, and deserves a doctor's evaluation.

Struggling to sit cross-legged at 45 after doing it effortlessly for decades can feel like a small but humbling reminder of ageing, particularly in a culture where floor seating is part of nearly every family and religious occasion. For most men it is a fixable, mechanical issue rather than a hormonal one, and a few minutes of regular stretching and strength work goes a long way.
