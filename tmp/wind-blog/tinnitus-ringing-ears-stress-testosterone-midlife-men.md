It usually starts in a quiet moment: lying in bed at night, sitting in a silent room, or waiting for sleep to come, and a thin, high-pitched ringing or a low buzzing sound in one or both ears that was not there before. For a lot of men in their forties and fifties, tinnitus arrives alongside a cluster of other midlife changes, disrupted sleep, more stress, a general sense that the body is not working quite as reliably as it used to, and it is natural to wonder if it is all connected, including to testosterone.

## What actually causes tinnitus after 40?

**Tinnitus is the perception of sound, ringing, buzzing, hissing, or humming, when no external sound source is present, and it is a symptom rather than a diagnosis in itself.** After 40, the most common underlying causes are cumulative noise exposure over the years (traffic, loud workplaces, headphones at high volume, festival firecrackers and loudspeakers that are a routine part of Indian life), age-related changes in the inner ear's hair cells, and simple earwax buildup, which is often the easiest cause to rule out and treat.

**High blood pressure, certain medications, and jaw or neck tension can also contribute**, and for men already managing blood pressure medication or other midlife conditions, it is worth mentioning tinnitus to your doctor as part of the full picture rather than treating it as an isolated ear problem.

### Does low testosterone cause tinnitus?

There is no established direct biological mechanism or strong clinical evidence linking testosterone decline to tinnitus onset. The research connecting the two is thin and inconsistent. What is better supported is an indirect relationship: andropause-related sleep disruption, irritability, and chronic stress can lower a person's tolerance for tinnitus and make an existing low-level ringing feel far more intrusive, especially at night when the surrounding environment is quiet and there is less to distract from it.

## Why does it feel worse at night or when stressed?

**This is one of the most consistently reported patterns with tinnitus, and it has a straightforward explanation.** During the day, ambient noise, conversation, and general activity mask a low-level internal sound. At night, in a quiet bedroom, that masking disappears, and the same underlying tinnitus becomes far more noticeable. Stress and poor sleep do not create tinnitus out of nothing, but they do lower the threshold at which it becomes distracting and can create a frustrating cycle: tinnitus disrupts sleep, poor sleep raises stress, and higher stress makes the tinnitus feel louder the next night.

### Can andropause-related anxiety make it feel worse?

Yes, indirectly. Men going through andropause-related mood changes, irritability, or health anxiety sometimes report their tinnitus became noticeably more bothersome during a stressful period of life, even when the underlying ear condition itself had not changed. This is a genuine phenomenon, well documented in tinnitus research generally, and it is one reason stress management and good sleep hygiene are part of standard tinnitus management advice, alongside addressing the ear itself.

## When does tinnitus need medical attention?

**Most tinnitus is not dangerous, but certain patterns should prompt a proper evaluation rather than being dismissed as "just stress" or "just getting older."** New tinnitus in only one ear, tinnitus that comes with sudden or worsening hearing loss, tinnitus paired with dizziness or balance problems, or tinnitus that throbs in time with your heartbeat (pulsatile tinnitus) all warrant seeing a doctor, who may refer you to an ENT specialist for further assessment.

| Pattern | Likely significance | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Mild ringing, both ears, worse at night | Common, often stress- or noise-related | Mention at your next checkup, improve sleep hygiene |
| Sudden onset in one ear | Needs prompt evaluation | See a doctor or ENT specialist soon |
| Tinnitus with hearing loss or dizziness | Possible inner ear or nerve involvement | See an ENT specialist for assessment |
| Pulsing or whooshing in time with heartbeat | Possible vascular cause | See a doctor promptly for evaluation |
| Earwax buildup suspected | Often easily treatable | A doctor can check and clear it safely |

## What actually helps day to day?

**For ongoing, non-urgent tinnitus, the most evidence-backed approaches focus on reducing its impact rather than eliminating the sound entirely**, which is often not fully achievable. Sound therapy using a fan, white noise machine, or soft background audio at night can reduce the stark contrast between silence and the ringing. Managing overall stress, protecting your remaining hearing from further loud noise exposure, limiting caffeine late in the day if it worsens your symptoms, and addressing sleep quality all have reasonable evidence behind them. If anxiety about the tinnitus itself has become a significant burden, India's mental health helpline iCall (9152987821, Monday to Saturday, 8am to 10pm) is a confidential resource worth knowing about, and in a medical emergency the number to call is 112.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is ringing in the ears a sign of low testosterone?**
There is no strong direct evidence connecting the two. Tinnitus in midlife is far more commonly explained by noise exposure, age-related hearing changes, earwax, or underlying conditions like high blood pressure, though stress linked to andropause can make existing tinnitus feel more noticeable.

**Why is my tinnitus worse when I'm stressed?**
Stress does not create tinnitus, but it lowers your tolerance for it and reduces the natural masking your brain otherwise provides, making an existing ringing feel significantly louder and harder to ignore during stressful periods.

**Should I see a doctor for tinnitus, or is it just part of ageing?**
New, sudden, one-sided, or worsening tinnitus, especially alongside hearing loss or dizziness, should always be checked by a doctor. Mild, stable tinnitus in both ears is common with age but is still worth mentioning at a routine checkup.

**Can treating low testosterone fix tinnitus?**
There is no evidence that testosterone treatment resolves tinnitus, since the two are not directly linked. If your tinnitus is genuinely bothering you, addressing it directly through an ENT evaluation and sound or stress management is the appropriate path, separate from any testosterone conversation.

Tinnitus is unsettling precisely because it is invisible to everyone but you, and it is reasonable to wonder whether everything happening in your body in midlife is connected. In this case, the ringing itself is very unlikely to be about your hormones, but how much it bothers you may well be, which is exactly why stress management and a proper ear evaluation both deserve a place in how you handle it.
