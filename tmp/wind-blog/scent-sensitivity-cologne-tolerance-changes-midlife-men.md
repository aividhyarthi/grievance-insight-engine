## Why would strong scents suddenly feel like too much?

**A general narrowing of sensory tolerance is a documented pattern in midlife and older adulthood, affecting how people respond to noise, crowding, bright light, and strong smells, not smell in isolation.** This connects to a broader shift where the nervous system's baseline threshold for what counts as "too much" input appears to lower with age for a meaningful proportion of people. A man who used to comfortably tune out a strong scent, loud music, or a crowded room may find all three genuinely more grating in his late 40s and 50s, even without any single dramatic cause.

**This overlaps with, but is distinct from, the sensory and crowd-tolerance shifts some men notice in busy public spaces, since scent sensitivity specifically can show up even in a quiet, otherwise comfortable environment, a meeting room, a car, a home, if someone nearby is wearing a strong fragrance.**

## Does your sense of smell actually change with age?

### A paradox worth understanding

**The sense of smell typically declines gradually starting in midlife and more noticeably after 60, which sounds like it should make strong scents less bothersome, not more, but the reality is more nuanced.** Age-related smell decline often affects the ability to detect and distinguish subtle or pleasant scents (a mild perfume, a fresh dish) more than it affects the perception of very strong, sharp, or synthetic ones, which can still register, and sometimes register as more unpleasant or "chemical," because the finer, more pleasant notes that would normally balance them out are harder to detect.

**In practice, this means a heavy synthetic cologne or perfume can genuinely smell worse, more one-dimensional and overwhelming, to a midlife nose than it did to the same nose at 25, even though overall smell sensitivity has technically gone down.** This is a real physiological shift, not a matter of taste or mood alone.

### Cortisol and sensory tolerance

**Because testosterone and cortisol have an inverse relationship in many midlife men, and cortisol influences the body's general stress and sensory-processing threshold, a shift in this balance can plausibly lower tolerance for strong sensory input across several senses at once, including smell.** A man managing broader andropause symptoms, disrupted sleep, more irritability, midlife stress, may notice scent sensitivity as one small but tangible part of that same picture rather than an isolated, unrelated annoyance.

| Possible contributor | What it affects | Practical response |
|---|---|---|
| General sensory tolerance narrowing | Noise, crowding, bright light, and smell together | Reduce total sensory load where you can control it |
| Age-related smell decline | Subtle scents harder to detect, strong ones feel harsher | Switch to lighter, higher-quality fragrances |
| Cortisol/stress threshold shift | Lower tolerance for strong input during high-stress periods | Address broader stress and sleep alongside scent habits |
| Genuine fragrance allergy/irritation | Headaches, sinus irritation, skin reaction | See a doctor if symptoms are more than mild annoyance |

## When is scent sensitivity worth mentioning to a doctor?

**Mild irritation or a stronger-than-before reaction to perfumes and colognes is usually just a sensory tolerance shift and doesn't need medical attention on its own.** What's worth raising with your doctor is if strong scents are triggering genuine physical symptoms, headaches, sinus congestion, breathing difficulty, or skin reactions, which points toward a fragrance sensitivity or allergy rather than a general midlife tolerance shift, and deserves its own evaluation.

**It's also worth mentioning if a sudden, significant loss of smell (rather than just changed tolerance) shows up, since that can have several causes worth ruling out with a doctor, separate from the gradual, expected decline discussed here.** A gradual shift in tolerance is common and manageable. A sudden, marked loss of smell altogether is a different situation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does my own cologne feel too strong now when it didn't before?**
A combination of a general narrowing of sensory tolerance and an actual decline in smell sensitivity, which paradoxically can make strong or synthetic scents register as harsher rather than milder, is the most likely explanation. Using less product, or switching to a lighter fragrance, usually resolves it.

**Is being bothered by other people's perfume a sign of an allergy?**
It can be, if it comes with physical symptoms like headaches or sinus irritation, but for many midlife men it's simply a lower sensory tolerance threshold rather than a true allergy. If physical symptoms are present, a doctor can help distinguish the two.

**Does this have anything to do with testosterone specifically?**
Indirectly, through testosterone's inverse relationship with cortisol and cortisol's role in general sensory and stress tolerance. It's a plausible contributing factor rather than a direct, isolated cause, and it often shows up alongside other andropause-related changes rather than on its own.

**Should I stop wearing cologne altogether?**
Not necessarily. Most men find that using less product or switching to a lighter, higher-quality fragrance solves the problem without giving it up entirely. Fragrance-free is a reasonable choice for shared, closed spaces regardless.

A stronger reaction to scents than you used to have isn't a strange quirk. It fits a real, documented pattern of sensory tolerance shifting in midlife, and a few small adjustments to your own habits, and some consideration for the people sharing your space, usually settle it without any need to give up fragrance altogether.
