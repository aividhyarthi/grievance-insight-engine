You tell yourself you are looking forward to it, and part of you genuinely is. But there is another part quietly bracing for something harder to name: the moment you walk in and start comparing your life, your body, your career, against people who knew you when none of that had happened yet.

## Why do reunions feel more loaded in your 40s and 50s than they used to?

**A reunion is unlike almost any other social event because it collapses a huge amount of time into one room.** At a regular dinner with current friends or colleagues, you already know roughly where everyone stands, their job, their family situation, their health. At a reunion, you are suddenly updating twenty or more individual life files at once, often within the first hour, while also being evaluated yourself. That density of comparison, who looks older, who got promoted furthest, who seems happiest, who has put on the most weight, is simply not something most social situations ask of you, and it can be quietly exhausting even before the evening properly starts.

### The specific comparisons that sting most

For many midlife men, three comparisons tend to land hardest: career trajectory (who became the boss, the founder, the one who "made it"), physical appearance (who still looks like themselves and who looks a decade older), and family stability (marriages, children's achievements, visible happiness). None of these comparisons are rational measures of a life well lived, but the part of the brain doing the comparing rarely consults rationality first.

## Is there an actual hormonal piece to this, or is it just social anxiety?

**Both, and they feed each other.** Walking into a high-stakes social comparison situation activates the body's stress response, raising cortisol, the primary stress hormone. Cortisol and testosterone have a well-documented inverse relationship: chronically elevated cortisol is associated with lower testosterone over time, and even short bursts of social stress can produce a temporary dip in how energised and confident you feel, on top of whatever baseline andropause-related changes you are already managing. This is why a reunion evening that should feel joyful can instead leave some men feeling flat, irritable, or drained for a day or two afterward, not because anything went wrong, but because the body treated the evening as genuinely stressful.

### Why it can feel worse than it did at 25

At a 10-year reunion, differences between classmates are usually still modest, everyone is roughly at the same career stage. By 20 or 25 years out, life paths have diverged enormously, some classmates are CEOs, some are still finding their footing, some have lost parents or partners, some are dealing with health issues. The spread is simply wider, which gives the comparing mind more material to work with, and more chances to land unfavourably.

## How can you actually enjoy a reunion instead of dreading it?

**Shifting your goal for the evening matters more than preparing your appearance does.** Walking in with a goal of "looking successful" sets you up for comparison and disappointment no matter how the evening goes, because someone will always seem to be doing better by that measure. Walking in with a goal of "reconnecting with two or three people I genuinely liked" is achievable, enjoyable, and almost entirely within your control.

### Practical ways to lower the pressure

A few small shifts make a real difference: decide beforehand which handful of old friends you actually want meaningful time with, rather than trying to "work the room"; prepare a simple, honest, low-drama one-line answer for "so what are you doing now" so you are not caught improvising under pressure; and remind yourself that almost everyone in that hall, including the ones who look the most composed, is running some version of the same internal comparison you are.

| Reunion mindset | Likely outcome |
|---|---|
| "I need to prove I'm doing well" | Exhausting, comparison-driven, hard to enjoy |
| "I want to reconnect with a few real friends" | Achievable, genuinely enjoyable |
| Avoiding the event entirely out of dread | Short-term relief, but often regret afterward |
| Going with one trusted friend as a "buddy" | Lower social pressure, easier to pace the evening |

## What if the reunion leaves you genuinely low afterward?

**A day or two of flatness or low mood after an emotionally loaded event is common and usually passes on its own.** But if reunions, or social comparison generally, regularly trigger a mood dip that lingers much longer, or if you notice this pattern alongside other symptoms, persistent fatigue, low libido, irritability, or difficulty concentrating, it is worth raising with your doctor rather than assuming it is "just" the reunion talking. These overlapping symptoms are exactly the kind of picture andropause can produce, and a proper testosterone blood test can clarify what is actually going on. If low mood ever tips into something heavier, lasting sadness, hopelessness, or loss of interest in things you normally enjoy, India's mental health helpline iCall (9152987821, Monday to Saturday, 8am to 10pm) is a confidential place to talk it through, and in a genuine emergency, 112 connects you to help immediately.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why do I feel worse after a reunion instead of happy I went?**
A post-reunion low is often a mix of social comparison fatigue and a real stress hormone response from the evening, which can temporarily affect mood and energy. It usually settles within a day or two and does not mean the reunion was a mistake to attend.

**Is it normal to dread a school or college reunion in midlife?**
Yes, this is a very common feeling, and it usually comes from anticipating comparison rather than the actual people you will see. Most men who attend despite the dread report enjoying it more than they expected once they are actually there.

**Should I skip the reunion if I feel self-conscious about my appearance or career?**
That is a personal choice, but skipping purely out of comparison anxiety often leads to more regret than discomfort would have. Reframing your goal around reconnecting with specific people, rather than being evaluated by the room, usually helps more than skipping entirely.

**Can social stress from events like reunions actually lower testosterone?**
Acute social stress raises cortisol, which can temporarily blunt how energised and motivated you feel, and chronically elevated stress is linked to lower testosterone over time. A single evening is unlikely to meaningfully change your testosterone level on its own, but it can affect how you feel for a day or two.

A reunion is, underneath the nostalgia and nerves, just a room full of people your own age quietly managing the same midlife comparisons you are. Walking in with that in mind tends to make the evening feel far less like an exam and far more like what it is supposed to be: old friends, checking in.
