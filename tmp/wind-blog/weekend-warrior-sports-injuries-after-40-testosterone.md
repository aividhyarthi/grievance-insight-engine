Every society apartment complex and office group has one: the Sunday cricket match or badminton session that's been running for years, where a man who played competitively in college now shows up once a week to relive it. In his thirties, a hard slide or an awkward smash left nothing more than mild soreness by Monday. In his late forties, the same session leaves a pulled hamstring, a tender shoulder, or a knee that takes two weeks to feel normal again. He hasn't gotten less fit, exactly. Something about recovery itself has changed, and testosterone is part of the reason why.

## Why does the same match leave a bigger mark than it used to?

**Three things are working against you at once, and testosterone touches at least two of them.** First, testosterone plays a meaningful role in muscle protein synthesis, the process by which muscle fibres repair and rebuild themselves after strain. As testosterone gradually declines through your forties and fifties, that repair process slows down somewhat, meaning the same degree of muscle damage from a hard match takes longer to resolve than it did a decade earlier.

Second, tendons and ligaments, the connective tissue holding joints together, naturally lose some collagen quality and elasticity with age, a process that happens regardless of testosterone but is compounded when testosterone is also low, since testosterone supports connective tissue health too. This is a large part of why a hamstring pull, a rolled ankle, or a tennis-elbow-type injury from badminton becomes more common and takes longer to settle.

## Is it really about age, or is it about how you're actually training?

### The "once a week" problem

**For most weekend players, the bigger issue isn't age itself but the mismatch between a sedentary week and one burst of high-intensity activity.** A body that sits at a desk for five days and then sprints for boundaries or dives for a badminton shuttle on day six is being asked to handle forces it hasn't prepared for all week. This mismatch gets less forgiving with age because recovery capacity is also lower, so the same training gap produces a bigger injury risk than it would have in your late twenties.

### What actually helps between matches

Some baseline strength work during the week, even two short sessions focused on legs, hips, and shoulders, meaningfully reduces injury risk for weekend sport, because stronger surrounding muscles take load off tendons and ligaments during sudden movements. This matters more than any amount of stretching done only on match day.

| Habit | Injury risk if skipped | Why it matters after 40 |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamic warm-up before play | Higher risk of muscle strain in the first 15 minutes | Cold tissue is less elastic and tears more easily |
| Strength training between matches | Higher risk of joint and tendon injury overall | Stronger muscles protect joints during sudden movement |
| Adequate hydration and sleep before match day | Slower reaction time, higher fall and collision risk | Recovery capacity is already reduced with age |
| Rest or light activity the day after | Prolonged soreness, risk of playing through unresolved strain | Tissue repair genuinely takes longer than it used to |

## Should you just stop playing?

**Almost never, and doctors generally don't recommend that.** Weekend sport is one of the more enjoyable, socially sustaining forms of exercise many Indian men have in midlife, and the camaraderie of a regular match matters for mental health, not just fitness. The better approach is adjusting how you prepare and recover around it: a genuine five to ten minute dynamic warm-up before play, some strength and mobility work on two other days of the week, and treating the day after a hard match as a recovery day rather than pushing through soreness at the gym too.

**If an injury happens, giving it proper time to heal matters more after 40 than it did at 25.** Playing through a half-healed strain to avoid letting the team down is a common way a two-week injury becomes a two-month one. A short break from the match, with your teammates knowing why, costs far less than a tendon injury that lingers all season.

## When does a sports injury need more than rest and ice?

Most muscle strains and minor sprains improve steadily within a week to ten days with rest, ice, and gentle movement. **Pain that isn't improving after a week, any joint that feels unstable or gives way, visible swelling that doesn't reduce, or a sharp pain during a specific movement that doesn't ease up deserves a doctor's assessment** rather than waiting it out, since some injuries that feel minor at first, like a partial ligament tear, need proper diagnosis and sometimes physiotherapy to heal correctly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does a pulled muscle take so much longer to heal now than it did in my thirties?**
Declining testosterone slows the muscle repair process somewhat, and connective tissue naturally loses some elasticity with age, both of which extend typical recovery time for the same injury.

**Should I stop playing weekend cricket or badminton because I'm over 40?**
Generally no. The game itself isn't the problem; an unprepared body meeting sudden high-intensity demands once a week is. Adjusting your warm-up and adding some strength training between matches addresses the real issue.

**Is testosterone replacement therapy useful for faster sports injury recovery?**
TRT is prescribed for diagnosed low testosterone with broader symptoms, not as a sports recovery aid, and should never be started or adjusted for this reason alone. Talk to a doctor if you suspect low testosterone is a broader issue.

**How long should I rest an injury before playing again?**
It depends on the injury, but as a general rule, resuming before pain has clearly settled and you have close to full range of motion risks turning a minor injury into a prolonged one. When in doubt, especially with joint injuries, get a doctor's opinion before returning to play.

The weekend match doesn't have to become a casualty of getting older. A bit of preparation during the week, a real warm-up, and a willingness to actually rest an injury instead of playing through it will do more for your game, and your knees, than anything else on this list.
