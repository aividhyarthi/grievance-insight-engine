You used to be able to hear a pointed comment from your manager, shrug, and move on with your day. Lately, the same kind of feedback sits with you for hours, sometimes longer. You replay it in the car, bring a version of it home, and find yourself irritable with your family over something that has nothing to do with them. If this sounds familiar, you're not imagining it, and you're not alone. Plenty of men notice that criticism, even mild, constructive criticism, lands differently after 40 than it did at 28.

This shift is real, and it has real contributors: hormonal changes, accumulated career pressure, and a midlife identity that can feel more fragile than it looks from the outside. Understanding why it's happening is the first step to handling it without either shutting down or lashing out.

## Why does criticism feel heavier after 40?

**It's rarely just about the specific comment.** By midlife, a piece of feedback often lands on top of an already fuller emotional plate: financial pressure, caregiving responsibilities for ageing parents, a body that doesn't recover from a bad night's sleep the way it used to, and a career where the room for correction feels smaller than it did at 25. A comment that would have rolled off you in your first decade of work can now feel like confirmation of a fear you've been quietly carrying, that you're slowing down, becoming replaceable, or losing your edge.

**There's also a hormonal layer.** Testosterone plays a role in mood regulation and stress resilience, and as levels decline, many men report feeling more reactive, more prone to rumination, and less able to bounce back quickly from a setback than they used to. This doesn't mean testosterone alone explains it, but it's a real contributor that's easy to overlook when the more obvious story is "I'm just more sensitive now" or "work has gotten worse."

### The cortisol and testosterone feedback loop

Chronic stress raises cortisol, and sustained high cortisol can suppress testosterone production over time. Lower testosterone, in turn, is linked to reduced resilience to stress. The result for some men is a loop: a stressful feedback moment raises cortisol, that contributes to lower testosterone over time, and lower testosterone makes the next piece of feedback feel even harder to absorb. Recognising this loop doesn't make any single comment sting less in the moment, but it explains why the sensitivity feels cumulative rather than tied to any one incident.

## Ego, identity, and why status feels more fragile now

**For many Indian men, professional standing carries weight beyond the paycheck.** It's tied to being seen as capable, as the provider, as someone whose family and community respect. In your 20s and 30s, there's an implicit sense that you have time to recover from a setback. By your late 40s, with children's education, ageing parents, and retirement all visibly closer, a piece of critical feedback can feel less like a correction and more like a verdict on whether you're still keeping pace, which raises the emotional stakes considerably.

This isn't irrational. The stakes genuinely are different in midlife. But recognising that the intensity of your reaction is partly about the broader context, not solely about the feedback itself, can help you separate the signal (what's actually being asked of you) from the noise (what it's triggering emotionally).

## Practical ways to receive feedback without spiraling

**The goal isn't to stop feeling anything, it's to shorten the spiral.** A few concrete habits make a measurable difference for most men who struggle with this.

| Old reaction pattern | More useful response |
|---|---|
| Replaying the comment for hours, adding your own harsher commentary on top | Write down the actual feedback in one neutral sentence, then set it aside for a few hours before deciding what to do with it |
| Taking it home and being short with family | Build in a 15-20 minute buffer (a walk, a drive, music) between work and home to reset before you're back with your family |
| Assuming it means something bigger about your competence or future | Ask a trusted colleague or your manager for one concrete clarifying question rather than guessing at the subtext |
| Avoiding the person who gave the feedback afterward | A brief, low-stakes follow-up ("noted, working on it") closes the loop and reduces lingering tension |

## When sensitivity points to something more than an off day

**Feeling stung by feedback occasionally is normal at any age.** What's worth paying attention to is a pattern: if you find yourself unable to shake off criticism for days, withdrawing from people, losing interest in things you normally enjoy, or noticing this alongside fatigue, low libido, or disrupted sleep, that combination points toward something more than a rough week, potentially depression linked to andropause, which is genuinely under-recognised and under-treated in men.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal for criticism to bother me more now than it did in my 20s?**
Yes, this is a commonly reported pattern in midlife, tied to a combination of hormonal changes affecting mood regulation, accumulated life stress, and the higher personal stakes that often come with career and family responsibilities in your 40s and 50s. It doesn't mean you've become weaker or less capable.

**Can low testosterone actually make me more sensitive to feedback?**
It can be a contributing factor for some men, since testosterone plays a role in mood regulation and stress resilience. It's rarely the sole cause, and other factors like accumulated stress and sleep quality usually play a part too, but it's worth mentioning to your doctor if the change feels significant.

**How do I stop bringing work frustration home to my family?**
Building in even a short buffer between work and home, a walk, a drive with music, a few minutes of quiet before you walk in the door, gives your nervous system time to downshift before you're interacting with people who didn't cause the stress and shouldn't absorb it.

**When should I see a doctor about how I'm handling feedback and stress?**
If reactions to feedback are lasting days rather than hours, coming with persistent low mood, sleep disruption, or loss of interest in things you usually enjoy, or affecting your relationships regularly, it's worth a conversation with your doctor, who can assess for depression, hormonal factors, or both.

Taking criticism harder than you used to isn't a sign you've lost your resilience, it's a sign that several real things, hormonal, emotional, and situational, are stacking on top of each other in a way they didn't when you were younger. Naming that clearly is usually the first step toward handling it with more steadiness.
