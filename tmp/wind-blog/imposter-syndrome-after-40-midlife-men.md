A man in his late forties walks into a board meeting he has prepared for all week, sits at the head of the table because the role now requires it, and spends the next hour quietly convinced that everyone else can tell he doesn't actually belong there. He built an entire career to reach this room, and yet the feeling of being a fraud who got lucky has only grown louder with each promotion, not quieter.

This is imposter syndrome, and while it is usually discussed as an early-career problem, it shows up with real force in midlife too, often at exactly the point when a man's resume would suggest he has the least reason to doubt himself. This article looks at why that happens, how it is tangled up with the physical changes of andropause, and what genuinely helps.

## Why does imposter syndrome get louder after 40, not quieter?

It seems backwards: more experience should mean more confidence, not less. **The reason is structural, not personal.** Early in a career, feedback is frequent and explicit, a manager tells you directly whether you did well. Senior roles strip that feedback away; fewer people are positioned to evaluate you honestly, and the stakes of any visible mistake are higher, so the mind fills the silence with doubt instead of reassurance.

At the same time, midlife often brings a visible generational shift at work: younger colleagues who seem effortlessly fluent with new tools, new platforms, or new ways of communicating, which can make an experienced man feel outdated even while he is objectively still highly capable. The comparison is unfair, since competence doesn't disappear, but it still lands as evidence against him.

### The hormone link: why this can feel sharper now

**Testosterone decline plays a real, if partial, role here.** Lower testosterone is linked to reduced baseline confidence, lower stress resilience, and in some men, a flatter mood overall, which means the same self-critical thought that might have been shrugged off at 30 can feel heavier and harder to dismiss at 48. This doesn't mean imposter syndrome is "just" a hormone problem, but for many men the hormonal backdrop is quietly turning up the volume on a psychological pattern that was always there.

## How this differs from other midlife work struggles

It helps to know what this specifically is, since it gets confused with adjacent experiences that need different responses.

| Experience | What it actually is | What tends to help |
|---|---|---|
| Imposter syndrome | Doubting that earned success is deserved or real | Naming the pattern, seeking outside perspective, mentoring others |
| Career plateau | Objectively being passed over or stalled | Honest conversations about growth path, possibly a role or company change |
| Feedback sensitivity | Reacting more strongly to criticism than before | Separating the message from the messenger, pausing before responding |
| Burnout | Depleted energy from sustained overwork | Rest, workload change, addressing underlying health |

## When it's more than just a confidence wobble

A bad week of self-doubt before a big presentation is normal and usually passes. **Pay closer attention if the feeling is constant, is paired with low mood, poor sleep, loss of interest in things you used to enjoy, or thoughts of wanting to disappear or that things would be easier if you weren't around.** That combination is no longer just imposter syndrome and deserves a direct conversation with a doctor, since it may reflect depression, which has a well-documented link to low testosterone and deserves proper evaluation rather than being pushed through quietly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is imposter syndrome a real medical condition?**
It isn't a formal diagnosis, but it is a well-documented psychological pattern studied extensively since the 1970s, and it is common enough in senior professionals that naming it often brings real relief on its own.

**Can low testosterone cause or worsen imposter syndrome?**
Not directly cause it, but lower testosterone is linked to reduced baseline confidence and resilience, which can make an existing tendency toward self-doubt feel more intense and harder to shake off than it used to.

**How is this different from just lacking confidence?**
Lacking confidence is usually proportionate to actual skill gaps. Imposter syndrome specifically involves doubting success that is objectively earned and documented, often despite clear external evidence of competence.

**Should I talk to my doctor about this, or just a therapist or counsellor?**
Either is a reasonable starting point. A doctor is the right call if low mood, poor sleep, or physical symptoms are part of the picture too, since those deserve medical evaluation; a counsellor is well suited to working through the thought pattern itself once anything medical has been ruled out or addressed.

Reaching a senior role in midlife and still feeling like a fraud is far more common than most men admit out loud. It is a pattern with a name, a known set of triggers, and real ways to loosen its grip, not a verdict on whether you actually belong where you are.
