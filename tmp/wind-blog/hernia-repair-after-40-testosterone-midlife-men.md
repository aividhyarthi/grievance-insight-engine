A man notices a soft bulge near his groin, usually after lifting something heavy or straining during a bowel movement. It is not always painful at first, so he tells himself it is nothing, maybe a pulled muscle, and goes back to his day. Months later, the bulge is still there, maybe a little bigger, and he has quietly worked out a system of avoiding heavy lifting rather than getting it checked. This pattern, more than almost any other midlife health issue, is one Indian men put off for embarrassingly long periods.

This article covers what an inguinal hernia actually is, why so many men delay the simple fix for it, and what it does and doesn't have to do with the broader hormonal picture of midlife.

## What exactly is a hernia, and why does it happen more after 40?

**A hernia happens when internal tissue, usually a loop of intestine or fatty tissue, pushes through a weak point in the abdominal wall, most commonly in the groin area.** Men have a natural anatomical weak spot here, the inguinal canal, through which structures pass down into the scrotum during development. This weak spot can widen or give way over time, especially under repeated strain from heavy lifting, chronic coughing, straining during constipation, or simply the gradual loss of core muscle tone that comes with age and reduced activity.

**Midlife is a common time for hernias to become noticeable, not because of any single cause, but because of accumulated strain meeting weaker tissue.** Years of desk-job sitting, reduced core strength, and sometimes weight gain around the midsection all add pressure to a part of the body that was already structurally vulnerable. A single heavy lift, a bout of hard coughing, or straining on the toilet is often just the final trigger on a weak spot that had been building for years.

## Why do so many Indian men delay getting it treated?

**The embarrassment factor is real and specific.** A bulge near the groin feels different to talk about than a knee problem or back pain, and many men, especially in joint-family settings without much privacy to discuss personal health, simply avoid raising it with anyone, including a doctor. Some men quietly modify their lives around it instead: avoiding lifting grandchildren, skipping the gym, letting a younger colleague carry the heavier boxes, all without explaining why.

**There is also a genuine, if mistaken, belief that a hernia is "manageable" without surgery.** Trusses and hernia belts are still sold and used by some men as a way to hold the bulge in place rather than fix the underlying weakness. These can provide temporary comfort but do not repair the hernia, and relying on one for years, rather than as a short-term bridge to surgery, increases the risk of complications developing unnoticed.

### What happens if a hernia is genuinely left untreated for years

A hernia that is reducible, meaning the bulge can be gently pushed back in or disappears when lying down, is uncomfortable and inconvenient but not usually dangerous in the short term. The real danger is a hernia becoming incarcerated (stuck, unable to be pushed back) or strangulated (the blood supply to the trapped tissue is cut off). A strangulated hernia is a surgical emergency, since the trapped tissue can die within hours if not treated.

## Does testosterone have anything to do with hernias?

**No, there is no established link between testosterone levels and the development of a hernia.** A hernia is a mechanical and structural problem, a weak point in connective tissue under physical strain, not a hormonal one. Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) does not cause hernias, and low testosterone does not cause them either.

**Where there is a loose, indirect connection is through weight and muscle mass.** Weight gain around the midsection, which is more common as testosterone gradually declines and metabolism shifts, adds extra pressure on the abdominal wall. Reduced core muscle strength, also more common with lower activity levels in midlife, means there is less supportive tissue around the weak spot. Neither of these means low testosterone caused your hernia, but both are reasons that general strength maintenance after 40 matters for more than just how you look.

The surgery itself is far less of an ordeal than the years some men spend quietly avoiding it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a hernia go away on its own without surgery?**
No, a hernia will not heal or close on its own once the muscle wall has given way. It may stay stable and manageable for a long time in some men, but it does not reverse itself, and surgery remains the only definitive fix.

**Is hernia surgery painful or risky at my age?**
Age alone is rarely a barrier to hernia surgery, which is performed safely in men well into their 70s and beyond, with the main consideration being overall fitness for anaesthesia, which your surgeon will assess beforehand. Most men describe the recovery as far easier than they expected.

**Can exercise or a hernia belt fix it instead of surgery?**
No. Core-strengthening exercise is good for general health and may slow the progression of a mild weakness, but it cannot close an established hernia. A hernia belt or truss provides temporary support and comfort at best, not a repair, and should not be relied on long term instead of seeing a doctor.

**How urgent is it if the bulge doesn't hurt?**
A painless, reducible hernia is not an emergency, but it is not something to leave indefinitely either, since hernias generally enlarge over time and the risk of a sudden complication, while low at any given moment, accumulates the longer it is left untreated. Book a consultation rather than waiting for pain to force the decision.

A hernia is one of the most fixable problems in midlife medicine, and also one of the most needlessly delayed. If you've noticed a bulge and have been quietly working around it, that avoidance is costing you more than the ten minutes it would take to get a doctor's opinion.
