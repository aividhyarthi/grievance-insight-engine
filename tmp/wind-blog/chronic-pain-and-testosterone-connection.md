A 52-year-old man with a decade-long history of lower back pain assumes his low energy, flat mood, and reduced libido are simply what happens when you live with constant pain. He isn't entirely wrong, chronic pain does take a real toll on all of these. But he's also probably never been told that long-term pain itself, and some of the medications used to manage it, can directly lower testosterone, and that low testosterone can in turn make pain feel more intense and harder to cope with. It's a connection that rarely comes up in either the pain clinic or the general physician's office, largely because the two specialities don't often talk to each other.

## Why would pain lower testosterone in the first place?

**Chronic pain keeps the body's stress response switched on for months or years at a time, and that has hormonal consequences.** Persistent pain signals sustained activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, the same stress-response system involved in cortisol release. Elevated cortisol over long periods tends to suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, the signalling chain that tells the body to produce testosterone. In simple terms, a body that's been treating pain as an ongoing emergency for years has less hormonal bandwidth left for normal testosterone production.

**Inflammation plays a role too.** Many chronic pain conditions, including inflammatory arthritis and some back conditions, involve elevated inflammatory markers circulating in the body. Chronic inflammation is separately associated with lower testosterone levels, which means conditions like rheumatoid arthritis or ankylosing spondylitis can lower testosterone through both the stress pathway and a direct inflammatory pathway at once.

## What about pain medications specifically?

**This is the part most men are never told.** Long-term use of opioid painkillers is one of the most well-documented medical causes of low testosterone, a condition sometimes called opioid-induced androgen deficiency. Opioids can suppress the brain's signal to the testes (reducing LH release), which lowers testosterone production, sometimes substantially. This is a recognised effect in pain medicine, not a rare side effect, and it applies to men on long-term opioid therapy for chronic back pain, post-surgical pain, or cancer-related pain.

**Not every pain medication carries this risk equally, and this is a conversation for your prescribing doctor, not something to self-manage.** If you've been on a long-term opioid prescription and have also noticed fatigue, low libido, or mood changes, it's worth asking your doctor directly whether hormone monitoring is appropriate, rather than assuming the pain condition alone explains everything.

| Pain treatment context | Testosterone impact | What to ask your doctor |
|---|---|---|
| Short-term pain medication (days to weeks) | Minimal impact expected | Usually no specific monitoring needed |
| Long-term opioid therapy (months or more) | Well-documented risk of suppression | Ask about periodic testosterone monitoring |
| Chronic inflammatory pain conditions (arthritis-type) | Possible suppression via inflammation | Ask whether hormone levels have been checked alongside inflammatory markers |
| Chronic pain managed mainly with non-opioid approaches | Lower direct medication risk, stress pathway still possible | Mention any andropause symptoms even without opioid use |

## Does low testosterone make pain feel worse?

**There's evidence for this running in the other direction too.** Testosterone appears to play a role in the body's pain-processing pathways, and low levels are associated with lower pain tolerance and higher pain sensitivity in some studies. This means a man with declining testosterone and a pre-existing pain condition may genuinely experience his pain as more intense than he would have a decade earlier, independent of whether the underlying pain condition itself has worsened.

**This creates a difficult loop for many men to untangle on their own.** More pain leads to more stress and possibly more medication, which can further lower testosterone, which can then make the pain feel worse and add fatigue and low mood on top of it. Breaking into this loop usually requires looking at it from both directions at once, rather than only adjusting pain management or only checking hormones.

## What should a man in this situation actually do?

**Bring both conditions into the same conversation, ideally with the doctor managing your pain, rather than treating them as unrelated.** Many men see a pain specialist or orthopaedist for the physical condition and, separately, might mention fatigue to a general physician, without either doctor connecting the two. Explicitly saying "I've been on this pain medication for a long time and I've also noticed low libido and constant fatigue, could these be related?" prompts the right test, a testosterone panel alongside LH and FSH, rather than each symptom being managed in isolation.

**If testosterone is confirmed low, treatment decisions need to account for the pain condition and its medications, not be made independently of them.** This is not a situation for self-directed supplement use or unprescribed testosterone. A doctor familiar with both the pain management plan and the hormonal picture is best placed to decide on next steps, which might include adjusting pain medication where feasible, addressing inflammation directly, or considering testosterone replacement therapy in appropriate cases.

**Don't dismiss new or worsening fatigue and mood changes as "just the pain talking."** It's a reasonable assumption to make, and it's often partly true, but "partly" is the key word. A treatable hormonal component sitting underneath years of chronic pain is worth identifying rather than accepting as an unavoidable package deal.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can chronic back pain alone lower testosterone, even without pain medication?**
Yes, sustained pain-related stress and inflammation can suppress testosterone production independent of any medication, through the body's prolonged stress response. Medication, particularly long-term opioid use, adds an additional and often more significant risk on top of that.

**Is opioid-induced low testosterone reversible?**
It often improves when opioid dosing is reduced or the medication is changed, under a doctor's guidance, though this needs to be balanced carefully against pain control needs. This is a decision for your prescribing doctor, not something to adjust independently.

**Should every man on long-term pain medication get a testosterone test?**
Guidelines increasingly suggest periodic hormone monitoring for men on long-term opioid therapy, so it's a reasonable thing to raise proactively with your doctor rather than waiting to be offered it. It's a simple blood test that provides useful information either way.

**Could treating low testosterone actually reduce how much pain I feel?**
Some men report improved pain tolerance and reduced fatigue when confirmed low testosterone is treated appropriately, though it's not a guaranteed pain solution and shouldn't replace proper pain management. It's best considered a complementary piece of the overall picture, evaluated with your doctor.

Living with chronic pain is exhausting enough without an unrecognised hormonal layer making it harder than it needs to be. If you've been managing long-term pain and also feel like something's shifted beyond the pain itself, fatigue, mood, libido, it's worth asking directly whether your testosterone has ever actually been checked.
