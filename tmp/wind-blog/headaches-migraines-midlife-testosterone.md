You've always had the occasional headache, the kind that comes after a long day or too little sleep. Somewhere after 40, they started showing up more often, sometimes without an obvious trigger, and over-the-counter painkillers that used to work quickly now barely take the edge off. Most men reach for the usual explanations, screen time, stress, dehydration, and stop there. Those are real factors, but for some men, declining testosterone is a genuine, rarely discussed piece of the picture too.

This article explains what the actual evidence says about testosterone and headaches, how to tell a hormone-linked pattern from other common causes, and when a headache pattern deserves more than a painkiller and a shrug.

## Can low testosterone actually cause headaches?

**Testosterone plays a genuine role in how the body regulates pain and manages blood vessel tone**, both of which are directly relevant to how headaches develop. Lower testosterone is associated with increased general pain sensitivity, meaning existing headache triggers, tension, dehydration, poor sleep, can produce a more intense or longer-lasting headache than they once did in the same man.

**The clearest documented link is with cluster headaches, a specific and severe headache type.** Research has found that men with cluster headaches often have lower testosterone levels than men without them, and some case reports describe improvement in cluster headache frequency after treating the underlying low testosterone. Cluster headaches are intensely painful, occur in clusters over weeks, and are typically felt around one eye, they're a distinct and less common pattern from ordinary tension headaches or standard migraines, and testosterone's role here is more established than in general headache patterns.

### What about ordinary tension headaches and migraines?

**The evidence is less direct here, but plausible through related mechanisms.** Declining testosterone is linked to higher inflammation, disrupted sleep, and increased stress reactivity, chronic tension headaches and stress headaches are strongly tied to exactly these factors. So while testosterone may not be pulling the trigger on an ordinary tension headache directly, it can be shaping the background conditions, poor sleep, higher stress sensitivity, more muscle tension, that make headaches more frequent overall.

## What else is commonly behind more frequent headaches after 40?

**Sleep apnoea is a major and often missed cause of morning headaches specifically.** Men who wake with a dull, band-like headache that eases within an hour or two of waking should consider sleep apnoea as a real possibility, particularly if a partner has also noticed loud snoring or pauses in breathing. Sleep apnoea and low testosterone frequently occur together, which makes this an important overlap to raise directly with a doctor.

**Eye strain, dehydration, and skipped meals remain some of the most common everyday triggers**, and midlife men often underestimate how much longer screen hours, less water intake through a busy workday, and irregular meals contribute compared to their 20s and 30s, when the body tolerated these lapses more easily.

## When should you actually see a doctor?

**A headache diary for two to three weeks makes the conversation with your doctor far more useful than a vague complaint.** Note when headaches happen, how long they last, what eases them, and any other symptoms present at the same time, fatigue, mood changes, or libido changes, since that pattern helps a doctor decide whether a hormonal evaluation makes sense alongside standard headache assessment.

**New, worsening, or unusual headache patterns deserve prompt attention, not just stronger painkillers.** A headache that's new after 40, has changed in character or frequency, wakes you from sleep, or occurs alongside vision changes deserves a proper evaluation. A sudden, severe headache unlike any you've had before, especially with confusion, vision loss, or difficulty speaking, is a medical emergency and needs immediate care (call 112 in India).

**If a hormonal link seems plausible, ask your doctor for a testosterone test alongside standard headache workup, not instead of it.** Ruling out sleep apnoea, blood pressure issues, and other common causes remains essential, testosterone is one piece of a larger picture, not a shortcut past proper diagnosis.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are headaches a recognised symptom of andropause or low testosterone?**
Headaches aren't typically listed among the classic andropause symptoms like fatigue or low libido, but there's genuine research linking low testosterone to increased pain sensitivity and, specifically, to cluster headaches. It's a real but less commonly discussed connection worth raising if headaches are part of a broader symptom pattern.

**What's a cluster headache, and how is it different from a migraine?**
Cluster headaches are severe, typically felt around one eye, and occur in repeated episodes ("clusters") over days or weeks, often at the same time of day. They're less common than migraines and have a more established research link to testosterone levels in men, so they're worth specifically mentioning to your doctor if that pattern matches yours.

**Could my morning headaches actually be about my testosterone rather than poor sleep?**
They could involve both, since sleep apnoea and low testosterone frequently occur together and can each worsen the other. A doctor can assess both possibilities, often starting with a sleep evaluation, since treating apnoea can meaningfully reduce morning headaches on its own.

**When is a headache a medical emergency rather than something to monitor?**
A sudden, extremely severe headache unlike any you've experienced before, especially with confusion, vision changes, weakness, or difficulty speaking, is a medical emergency. Call 112 immediately rather than waiting to see if it passes.

Most headaches after 40 still come down to familiar causes, screens, sleep, dehydration, stress, but for some men, declining testosterone is a real, underexamined piece of the puzzle. Tracking your pattern and bringing it to your doctor as part of a broader picture is worth more than reaching for another painkiller and hoping it passes.
