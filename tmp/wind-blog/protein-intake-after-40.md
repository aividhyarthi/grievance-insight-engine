A man who has eaten more or less the same diet for twenty years often notices, sometime in his 40s, that he is losing muscle and gaining fat despite nothing obviously changing in his food or his gym routine. Part of the answer usually lies in something simple and fixable: the same protein intake that maintained his body at 25 is no longer enough at 45, and declining testosterone is a direct reason why.

## Why does protein need actually go up with age?

The core reason is a phenomenon researchers call anabolic resistance. **As men age, muscle tissue becomes less responsive to the same amount of dietary protein, meaning the body needs a larger protein signal to trigger the same amount of muscle building it once achieved effortlessly in your 20s and 30s.** This is not a personal failing or a sign that something is wrong specifically with you, it is a well-documented, near-universal shift in how ageing muscle responds to nutrition.

**Declining testosterone adds a second, independent layer on top of this.** Testosterone plays a direct role in muscle protein synthesis, the process by which the body uses dietary protein to build and repair muscle tissue. As testosterone falls with age or with andropause, medically termed late-onset hypogonadism (LOH), that synthesis process becomes less efficient even when protein intake stays exactly the same. The combined effect of anabolic resistance and lower testosterone means many men in midlife are quietly losing muscle on a diet that would have maintained it a decade or two earlier.

## How much protein do I actually need?

**Most healthy, moderately active men over 40 benefit from roughly 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day**, noticeably higher than the 0.8 grams per kilogram often cited as a general adult minimum. A 75-kilogram man, for example, would target somewhere between 90 and 120 grams of protein daily, spread across meals, rather than concentrated in one sitting.

| Activity level | Approximate protein target (g per kg body weight) | Example for a 75kg man |
|---|---|---|
| Sedentary, minimal exercise | 1.0-1.2 g/kg | 75-90g per day |
| Moderately active, regular walking or light exercise | 1.2-1.4 g/kg | 90-105g per day |
| Strength training 3+ times a week | 1.4-1.6 g/kg | 105-120g per day |
| Recovering from illness or significant muscle loss | 1.6g/kg or higher, with medical guidance | 120g+ per day |

These are general guidelines, not fixed prescriptions, and men with kidney disease or other specific medical conditions should have protein targets set individually by their doctor rather than following a general range.

## Why do Indian diets often fall short?

**Traditional Indian meal patterns, particularly vegetarian ones built heavily around rice, roti, and dal, can genuinely fall short of higher midlife protein targets without deliberate adjustment.** Dal, while a valuable protein source, contains less protein per serving than most people assume relative to its volume, and a typical vegetarian thali can look substantial while still landing well under 60 grams of total protein for the day.

### Practical ways to close the gap

This does not require abandoning familiar food or switching to imported supplements. Adding a second portion of dal or legumes at a meal, including paneer, curd, or eggs where diet allows, and treating protein as something to plan for at each meal rather than an afterthought at dinner all meaningfully close the gap. For non-vegetarian eaters, ensuring at least one meal includes fish, chicken, or eggs rather than relying only on carbohydrate-heavy sides makes a measurable difference across a week.

## Does timing across the day actually matter?

**Yes, spreading protein across three or four meals is more effective for muscle maintenance than eating most of it in one large dinner**, which is a common pattern in households where breakfast and lunch are lighter and dinner is the main meal. Aiming for at least 25 to 30 grams of protein at each of three main meals gives the body a more consistent building signal throughout the day than the same total protein concentrated at night.

**This matters even more for men who are strength training**, since muscle repair after a workout depends on adequate protein being available in the hours around exercise, not just somewhere in the day's total. A man who trains in the morning but eats his main protein source at dinner is working against his own effort.

## When is it worth speaking to a doctor or dietitian?

If you have noticed unexplained muscle loss, ongoing weakness, or unintentional weight loss alongside a normal or even high-protein diet, it is worth raising this with your doctor rather than assuming diet alone is the issue. This pattern can sometimes point to a hormonal cause worth investigating with a testosterone blood test, or occasionally another underlying medical condition. A dietitian can also help translate general protein targets into an actual meal plan suited to your food preferences, budget, and any medical restrictions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram safe for healthy men?**
For most healthy men without kidney disease, this range is considered safe and is commonly recommended for active adults over 40. Men with kidney conditions or other relevant medical issues should get an individualised target from their doctor.

**Can vegetarians realistically hit these protein targets in India?**
Yes, with deliberate planning. Combining dal or legumes with paneer, curd, milk, or eggs across meals, rather than relying on any single source, makes it realistic to reach midlife protein targets on a vegetarian diet.

**Does eating more protein alone raise testosterone?**
No, protein intake supports muscle maintenance and overall metabolic health, but it does not directly raise testosterone levels. Adequate protein works alongside, not instead of, addressing any underlying hormonal decline through proper testing and medical guidance.

**Is it too late to fix muscle loss if I have already lost strength over the years?**
No. Increasing protein intake alongside resistance training can rebuild meaningful strength and muscle at almost any age, even if some loss has already occurred, though the timeline and extent of recovery vary by individual.

Protein needs genuinely shift with age and hormonal change, and the gap between what most men eat and what their midlife body actually needs is often smaller to close than it seems. Small, consistent adjustments at each meal tend to matter more than any single dramatic change.
