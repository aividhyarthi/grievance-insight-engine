Ravi, 46, has read enough to know he needs more protein now than he did at 30. So he does what a lot of men do: he eats a light breakfast, a rushed lunch at his desk, and then makes up for it with a large dinner, sometimes a second helping of dal and paneer, sometimes a protein shake before bed. He assumes it all evens out over the day. It might not.

As testosterone declines through your 40s and 50s, your body's ability to build and hold onto muscle from a given dose of protein appears to weaken slightly, a phenomenon researchers call "anabolic resistance." That has led to a real question: does spacing protein out across the day help counter this, or is total daily intake still the only thing that matters?

## Why does protein timing even become a question after 40?

**Muscle mass and testosterone are linked in both directions.** Testosterone supports muscle protein synthesis, and as levels decline with age, many men lose lean mass gradually, often 3-8% per decade after 40, even without major weight change. Less muscle mass, in turn, is associated with lower testosterone output, so the two trends can reinforce each other.

What's newer is the idea that ageing muscle doesn't respond to protein the same way younger muscle does. Studies on older adults have found that a "threshold" dose, roughly 25-35 grams of high-quality protein in one sitting, is needed to meaningfully switch on muscle protein synthesis, compared to a lower threshold in younger people. Eat less than that in a single meal, and much of the protein gets used for other things rather than muscle repair.

This is why a single dinner heavy in protein, even a large one, may be less effective than the same total amount spread across the day.

## What does the actual evidence on timing show?

The picture is more nuanced than fitness content often suggests.

**The "even distribution" hypothesis has moderate support.** Several studies comparing even protein distribution (roughly equal amounts at breakfast, lunch, and dinner) against a skewed pattern (small breakfast, small lunch, large dinner, the common pattern in many Indian households) found modestly better muscle protein synthesis rates with even distribution, particularly in older adults. The effect size is real but not dramatic.

**Total daily intake still predicts outcomes better than timing does.** Large reviews on protein and muscle mass consistently find that hitting an adequate total daily amount (commonly cited as 1.2-1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight for active older adults, more than the general adult recommendation) matters more than exactly when you eat it. Timing appears to offer an incremental benefit on top of an adequate total, not a substitute for one.

**Pre-sleep protein has specific, if modest, evidence.** A small but consistent body of research, much of it from resistance-training contexts, suggests 20-40 grams of protein (such as a glass of milk or a bowl of curd with nuts) 30-60 minutes before sleep can support overnight muscle protein synthesis, especially on days you've trained. This is not the same as needing a protein shake before bed regardless of activity.

## What does this actually look like on an Indian plate?

Most Indian breakfasts are protein-light, a couple of parathas, poha, or idli with sambar, none of which get close to 25 grams. Lunch and dinner, built around dal, sabzi, and roti or rice, often carry more protein but still fall short of the threshold unless paneer, curd, eggs, chicken, or fish are deliberately added.

**A practical approach beats a perfect one.** You don't need to overhaul your diet, you need to notice where breakfast and lunch are protein-light and add one deliberate source to each: eggs or curd at breakfast, an extra portion of dal or a paneer side at lunch, rather than trying to hit your entire day's target at dinner.

| Meal | Common pattern | Adjustment worth trying |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | Poha, paratha, tea, low protein | Add 2 eggs, or curd with nuts and seeds |
| Lunch | Dal, sabzi, roti, moderate protein | Add extra dal, a paneer or soy chunk side, or curd |
| Evening snack | Tea and biscuits, negligible protein | Roasted chana, sprouts, or a boiled egg |
| Dinner | Large portion, protein-heavy already for many | Keep as is, don't feel you must add more here |
| Before bed (optional, training days) | Often nothing | A small glass of milk or bowl of curd |

## Does this matter more if you're on testosterone replacement therapy?

**TRT changes the muscle-building equation somewhat, but doesn't remove the need for adequate protein.** Men on testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) generally see improved lean mass over the first year of treatment, but that improvement is still built from dietary protein. If anything, adequate and reasonably well-timed protein intake becomes more relevant during TRT, since your body has a stronger anabolic signal available to act on. Talk to your doctor about whether your current diet supports the training and recovery goals you're working toward together.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much protein do I actually need after 40?**
Most research on active midlife adults suggests 1.2-1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily, higher than the general adult recommendation of around 0.8 g/kg. For a 75 kg man, that's roughly 90-120 grams a day. Your doctor or a dietitian can help you personalise this based on kidney health, activity level, and other conditions.

**Is a large protein-heavy dinner really a problem?**
Not a problem exactly, but likely less efficient than the same total protein spread earlier in the day too. If dinner is your only substantial protein meal, you're probably under-dosing breakfast and lunch relative to the threshold your muscles need to respond well.

**Do I need a protein supplement to hit these targets?**
Not necessarily. Many Indian vegetarian and non-vegetarian diets can reach adequate totals through dal, paneer, curd, eggs, soy, nuts, and fish or chicken if consumed, with deliberate planning. Supplements can help close gaps but aren't required, and any supplement decision should be discussed with your doctor, especially if you're on any other medication.

**Does protein timing affect testosterone levels directly?**
Not in any meaningful, direct way that current evidence supports. Protein timing affects how efficiently your body uses protein to build and preserve muscle, which is a downstream consequence of testosterone's effects, not a lever that raises testosterone itself.

Protein timing is a genuine, evidence-backed refinement, not a magic fix, and it only matters once your total daily intake is already adequate. If you're unsure where you stand, a conversation with your doctor or a registered dietitian, ideally alongside a broader midlife health checkup, is a more useful starting point than chasing an exact meal-by-meal formula.
