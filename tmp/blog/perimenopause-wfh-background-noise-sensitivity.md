You used to be able to work from the dining table with the television on in the next room, the pressure cooker whistling, and a phone call happening somewhere in the house, all without losing your train of thought. Now, the same background hum that used to be invisible feels like it is actively competing for space in your head. A door closing two rooms away makes you lose the sentence you were writing. Your partner's video call bleeding through the wall makes it impossible to concentrate on your own. If working from home has quietly become louder and harder in a way that has nothing to do with your actual workload, perimenopause is a very plausible reason, and it is a distinct pattern from general crowd or noise sensitivity in public spaces.

## Is This the Same as Being Sensitive to Crowds and Noise in General?

**Related, but not quite the same thing.** General crowd and noise sensitivity in perimenopause tends to show up in public settings, markets, wedding halls, loud family gatherings, where the overwhelm is broad sensory overload. What's happening while working from home is more specific: it's about your brain's ability to filter background sound while concentrating on a task, which can be disrupted even by fairly quiet household sounds that wouldn't overwhelm you in a non-work context at all. You can be perfectly fine chatting in a noisy kitchen and still lose focus completely trying to write an email in the next room over the same noise level.

## Why Do Calls Feel Harder Than In-Person Conversations Right Now?

**Calls ask more of your auditory processing than face-to-face conversation does.** You lose the visual cues, like lip movement and body language, that normally help your brain fill in gaps, and you're often multitasking, typing notes, watching a screen, managing your own video feed, all at once. If you've noticed yourself needing to ask people to repeat themselves more on calls than in person, or feeling drained after back-to-back video meetings in a way you didn't before, this is a recognisable pattern, not a sign your hearing or your competence has declined.

## What Actually Helps?

**A good pair of noise-cancelling headphones is one of the highest-impact, lowest-effort changes available.** Even inexpensive ones make a real difference when the goal is filtering competing household sound rather than achieving studio silence. **Define a specific quiet zone and quiet hours, even in a small home.** This does not need to be a separate room, a corner with a clear signal (headphones on, a small sign, a specific time block) that the household recognises as focus time is often enough to reduce interruptions substantially. **Batch calls together and protect blocks of silence for deep work in between**, rather than letting calls interrupt concentration randomly throughout the day. **Ask directly for what you need rather than hoping people notice.** "I need it quiet in here between 10 and 12 for a call" is a specific, actionable request that most households can actually accommodate, compared to a vague hope that everyone will simply be quieter.

## In the Indian Context

**Many Indian homes were never designed with a dedicated home office in mind**, and working from a shared living space, bedroom, or dining table often means overlapping directly with domestic help, family members, or a joint household's daily rhythm. It is entirely reasonable to have a direct, matter-of-fact conversation with household help or family about a specific quiet window during the day, most people accommodate this readily once it's asked for plainly rather than implied. **If you manage domestic staff schedules yourself, shifting louder tasks, like a mixer grinder or vacuuming, outside your core focus hours** is a small logistical change that can meaningfully reduce your daily noise load. **Ongoing construction noise from neighbouring flats, common in many Indian apartment complexes, is genuinely one of the harder triggers to control**, and in that case, noise-cancelling headphones paired with scheduling your most demanding work during quieter parts of the day tends to help more than trying to eliminate the noise itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is this just because I have more to manage at home, or is it actually hormonal?**
Both are usually true together. Household demands genuinely add cognitive load, but the specific difficulty filtering background sound while concentrating, sounds you could tune out easily before, points to an auditory-processing change connected to fluctuating oestrogen, not just a busier household.

**Will noise-cancelling headphones actually help if the issue is my brain, not the sound itself?**
Yes, because even if the underlying cause is reduced filtering capacity, removing more of the competing sound at the source reduces the total load your brain has to filter, which measurably helps most people regardless of the underlying cause.

**Should I ask to go back to the office if working from home has become this hard?**
That's a valid option to consider if it's available and would genuinely reduce your noise load, but for many women, structural changes at home (headphones, quiet hours, clearer boundaries) resolve enough of the problem that a full return to office isn't necessary. It's worth trying the smaller changes first.

**Could this be a hearing issue rather than perimenopause?**
It's worth ruling out, especially if you're also noticing tinnitus, muffled hearing, or difficulty in one ear specifically. Perimenopause can affect hearing too, but a hearing check is a reasonable, low-effort step if the noise sensitivity is new and pronounced.
