Beyond specific aches like joint pain or headaches, many women notice something broader in perimenopause: a general sense that pain simply hurts more than it used to. An old knee twinge feels sharper. A minor knock stings longer. Period cramps that were always manageable suddenly floor you. This is not you being dramatic or losing your resilience. There is a genuine, hormone-linked shift in how the body processes and experiences pain during this transition.

## Why Pain Sensitivity Changes

**Oestrogen influences the body's pain-processing system.** Oestrogen affects the nervous system's pain pathways, including the production and activity of the body's natural pain-modulating chemicals, such as endorphins and serotonin, which help dampen and regulate how pain signals are experienced. As oestrogen fluctuates and falls in perimenopause, this natural pain-dampening system becomes less consistent, meaning the same physical signal can be experienced as more intense than it once was.

**Inflammation can increase.** Falling oestrogen is associated with a rise in inflammatory activity throughout the body, and inflammation itself heightens pain sensitivity, contributing to why joints, muscles, and other tissues can all feel more reactive during this stage, as covered in our guide to joint pain and muscle aches in perimenopause.

**Poor sleep lowers pain threshold.** This is well established and significant: inadequate or broken sleep, extremely common in perimenopause due to night sweats and hormonal wakefulness, directly reduces the body's pain threshold. Exhausted, under-slept bodies simply hurt more, independent of any other change, creating a compounding effect during this stage.

**Stress and anxiety amplify pain perception.** The heightened stress response of perimenopause keeps the nervous system in a more reactive state generally, and a more reactive nervous system tends to interpret and amplify pain signals more strongly.

## What This Looks Like

Women describe old injuries or areas of previous discomfort feeling more noticeable than they have in years, period cramps or other cyclical pain feeling more intense than the same pattern used to, minor bumps, headaches, or muscle soreness lingering longer or hurting more sharply, and a general sense of being more physically fragile or reactive than before, even without a specific new injury or diagnosis.

## What Helps

**Prioritise sleep as pain management, not just comfort.** Because poor sleep so directly lowers pain threshold, addressing night sweats and sleep disruption, through the strategies covered in our guide to perimenopausal insomnia, is genuinely one of the most effective things you can do for overall pain sensitivity, not simply a separate issue.

**Move regularly, gently.** Regular, moderate exercise reduces inflammation over time and supports the body's natural pain-modulating chemicals. It can feel counterintuitive when everything already hurts, but gentle, consistent movement, walking, swimming, yoga, tends to reduce overall pain sensitivity rather than worsen it, more so than rest alone.

**Manage inflammation through diet.** As covered in our guide to foods to avoid in perimenopause, reducing sugar, refined carbohydrates, and excess processed food, alongside increasing anti-inflammatory foods like oily fish, nuts, and colourful vegetables, can meaningfully support the body's inflammatory balance.

**Address stress directly.** Because a heightened stress response amplifies pain perception, the breathing practices, regular movement, and rest that ease general perimenopausal anxiety genuinely reduce overall pain sensitivity too, this is a real physiological link, not simply a mindset fix.

**Do not dismiss your own experience.** Many women are told, by others or by themselves, that they are overreacting to pain that "shouldn't" hurt this much. Understanding that there is a genuine physiological shift behind this experience is validating and can reduce the additional distress of feeling doubted, including by yourself.

**Consider the hormonal angle.** Because this pattern is rooted in oestrogen's role in pain regulation, some women find their overall pain sensitivity eases with treatments that steady hormones, including HRT for some. Worth discussing with your gynaecologist, particularly if pain is a significant, wide-ranging feature of your perimenopause.

## When to See a Doctor

**Routine appointment** if increased pain sensitivity is affecting your daily life, so it can be discussed as part of your broader perimenopause picture, and sleep, inflammation, and stress can all be addressed together.

**Also worth checking** if widespread pain is a dominant, persistent feature, as your doctor may want to consider other causes of widespread pain sensitivity, including thyroid problems or, in some cases, fibromyalgia, which shares some features with this experience but is a distinct condition warranting its own assessment and management.

If everything seems to hurt more than it used to, and you have wondered whether that is really possible, or whether you are simply being oversensitive, know that it is a real, documented, hormone-linked shift. Addressing sleep, inflammation, and stress genuinely helps, and you are not imagining the change.
