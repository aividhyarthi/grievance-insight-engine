There is a good chance tulsi is growing within fifty metres of wherever you are reading this, in a courtyard, on a balcony, or in a small pot near the front door, watered every morning as part of a routine older than most of us can remember. It is worshipped as sacred, brewed as kadha for colds, chewed on an empty stomach for "general immunity," and, increasingly, reached for by perimenopausal women hoping it might take the edge off stress, hot flushes, or the sense of being permanently on edge. Tulsi's reputation as a cure-all is centuries deep. Whether that reputation holds up for the specific, hormonally driven symptoms of perimenopause is a narrower and more useful question.

## What Is Tulsi Actually Used For, Traditionally?

**In Ayurveda, tulsi is classified as a rasayana, a category of herbs believed to promote longevity, resilience, and balance across body systems, rather than a remedy aimed at one specific complaint.** It is used for respiratory infections, digestive upset, skin conditions, and, very commonly, as a general tonic for stress and fatigue, usually as a tea, a chewed fresh leaf, or a kadha brewed with ginger and black pepper. Its near-universal presence in Indian households, often grown as a sacred plant (Tulsi Vrindavan) rather than purely a medicinal one, means most women reaching for it already have a lifelong relationship with it, long before perimenopause enters the picture.

**More recently, tulsi has been studied specifically as an adaptogen**, a term used for substances thought to help the body resist and recover from stress more efficiently, a category that also includes ashwagandha, already covered in our piece on ashwagandha for perimenopause stress.

## What Does the Research Actually Show?

**Tulsi is genuinely one of the better-studied Ayurvedic herbs, with several small-to-moderate human trials, which puts it on firmer ground than many herbs discussed in this series.** Research has found that tulsi extract can modestly lower cortisol, the body's primary stress hormone, and improve self-reported measures of stress, anxiety, and sleep quality over several weeks of regular use. Separately, a reasonably consistent body of evidence shows tulsi can modestly improve fasting blood sugar and post-meal glucose levels, relevant given that insulin resistance tends to worsen for many women through perimenopause.

**What the research does not show is any meaningful effect on reproductive hormones.** No good-quality human trial has found that tulsi raises or stabilises oestrogen, affects progesterone, or directly reduces hot flashes, night sweats, or irregular bleeding. Any benefit perimenopausal women notice is more plausibly explained by its calming, blood-sugar-steadying effects than by a direct hormonal mechanism.

## Could Lower Stress Still Help With Perimenopause Symptoms?

**Indirectly, quite possibly, even without a hormonal mechanism.** Chronic stress and poor sleep are known to worsen the perception and frequency of hot flashes, irritability, and brain fog in perimenopause, partly through the stress hormone cortisol's interaction with an already fluctuating hormonal system. A herb with reasonable evidence for lowering stress and steadying blood sugar is a sensible, low-risk thing to add to a broader routine, even while being honest that it is not treating the underlying hormonal shift itself.

**This distinction matters because it sets realistic expectations.** Tulsi is more likely to leave you feeling calmer and more even through a stressful week than to measurably reduce the number of hot flashes you have, and treating it as the latter risks delaying a conversation about hormone therapy or other evidence-based options if symptoms are significant.

## Is It Safe, and Who Should Be Careful?

**For most healthy women, tulsi taken as tea or a modest daily supplement is considered low risk, reflecting its long history of food-level use.** The main caution is for women on blood-thinning medication, since some research suggests tulsi may have a mild effect on blood clotting, and for women on diabetes medication, since its blood-sugar-lowering effect could add to that of their prescribed treatment and increase the risk of symptoms like dizziness or shakiness from blood sugar running too low. Pregnant women are typically advised to avoid medicinal doses, though this is less relevant in perimenopause itself.

## In the Indian Context

**Tulsi has an advantage almost no other herb in this series has: it is already free or nearly free for most Indian households, grown at home rather than bought from a pharmacy.** This makes it an easy, zero-cost first step for stress management, though it is worth being clear with family that drinking tulsi tea alongside, not instead of, medical care for significant symptoms is the sensible approach, rather than treating a home remedy and a doctor's visit as competing choices.

**If tulsi is already part of your daily routine**, there is little reason to stop, and mentioning it to your doctor at your next visit, particularly if you are starting hormone therapy or diabetes medication, is a simple, worthwhile habit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can tulsi reduce hot flashes?**
There is no good clinical evidence that tulsi reduces hot flashes or night sweats directly. Its better-supported benefits are for stress, cortisol, and blood sugar, which may help indirectly but are not the same as a targeted vasomotor treatment.

**Is tulsi safe to take every day?**
For most healthy women, tulsi as tea or in modest culinary amounts is considered low risk given its long history of everyday use in India. Regular medicinal-dose supplements are best discussed with your doctor first, particularly if you take other medication.

**Can I drink tulsi tea alongside hormone therapy?**
There is no strong evidence of a direct interaction, but telling your doctor what you are taking, including home remedies, is good practice so they have your complete picture.

**Does tulsi interact with any medications?**
It may have a mild blood-thinning effect and can lower blood sugar, so caution and disclosure matter most if you are on blood thinners or diabetes medication.

**Is tulsi the same as ashwagandha?**
No. Both are classed as adaptogens in Ayurveda, but they are different plants with different traditional uses and somewhat different evidence bases. Our separate piece on ashwagandha for perimenopause stress covers that herb specifically.
