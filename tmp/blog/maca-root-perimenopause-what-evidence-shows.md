Maca root has had a steady run of attention over the past decade, usually described in glowing, slightly vague terms: an "adaptogen," a libido booster, an energy fix, a root vegetable from the Peruvian Andes that somehow ended up in supplement aisles and wellness blogs across the world, including India. For a woman whose energy and interest in sex have both quietly dropped since perimenopause began, that marketing can sound like exactly what she needs. The actual research tells a narrower, more specific story than the supplement labels usually suggest.

## What exactly is maca, and how is it different from herbal options like black cohosh?

**Maca (Lepidium meyenii) is a cruciferous root vegetable grown at high altitude in the Peruvian Andes, traditionally eaten as a food rather than taken as a medicinal extract, usually dried and ground into a powder.** This matters for understanding how it is thought to work. Unlike black cohosh, red clover, or soy isoflavones, maca does not contain plant compounds that bind to oestrogen receptors. Researchers instead believe it may act on the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, the brain's hormone-regulating control centre, rather than supplying any hormone-like compound directly, though the exact mechanism is still not fully understood.

**This non-hormonal mechanism is part of why maca is sometimes suggested as an option for women who cannot or prefer not to use phytoestrogen-based herbs**, for instance those with a hormone-sensitive condition where oestrogen-mimicking compounds are generally avoided. It is worth stressing, though, that "non-hormonal mechanism" does not automatically mean "proven safe for every woman," and the same conversation with your doctor about your personal health history still applies.

## What does the research actually show for perimenopausal symptoms?

**The clinical trial evidence for maca is small in scale and mixed in quality, similar to the position most other herbal options occupy.** A handful of placebo-controlled trials in peri- and postmenopausal women have found modest improvements in mood, anxiety symptoms, and some measures of sexual function and satisfaction with maca compared to placebo. Fewer and less consistent results exist for hot flashes and night sweats specifically, with some trials showing a benefit and others showing no meaningful difference from placebo.

**This pattern, better evidence for mood and sexual function, weaker evidence for vasomotor symptoms, is actually a reasonably distinct profile compared to the phytoestrogen herbs covered elsewhere on this site.** If hot flashes are your main complaint, maca is unlikely to be your most evidence-backed option. If low mood, fatigue, and reduced interest in sex are your more pressing concerns, it may be more relevant, though "may be relevant" is still a considerably more modest claim than the marketing around it usually implies.

## Who should be particularly cautious with maca?

**Maca is a cruciferous vegetable and contains compounds called goitrogens, which can interfere with thyroid hormone production in some people, particularly with high intake or in those who already have thyroid dysfunction.** Since thyroid problems become more common around the same age as perimenopause and can themselves cause fatigue, mood changes, and irregular cycles that mimic perimenopausal symptoms, this overlap is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing. Anyone with a known thyroid condition should discuss maca with their doctor before starting it, and anyone with unexplained fatigue or mood symptoms should have their thyroid checked regardless of whether they try maca at all.

## In the Indian context

**Maca is imported rather than grown in India, so it tends to sit at a higher price point than Ayurvedic herbs like shatavari or ashwagandha, commonly ₹600 to ₹1,500 for a month's supply depending on the brand and form (powder versus capsules).** As with most supplements in this category, India's loosely regulated market means the labelled dose does not always match what is actually inside, and independent testing of imported supplement powders is uncommon, so sticking to a well-established, transparent brand is a reasonable, if imperfect, precaution.

**It is also worth being realistic about cost versus evidence when a family member suggests an imported supplement over a locally available, better-studied option.** If budget is a genuine constraint, discussing whether a more extensively researched option, including hormone therapy itself where appropriate, might be a better use of that money is a fair question to raise with your doctor.

## A reasonable way to think about trying it

**Maca is a reasonable, modestly evidence-backed option to discuss with your doctor if mood, fatigue, and low libido are your main concerns, rather than hot flashes specifically, and if you do not have a thyroid condition that would warrant extra caution.** As with other herbal options, giving it a defined trial period, most studies used it for twelve weeks or longer before assessing benefit, while independently tracking your own symptoms, gives you a more honest basis for deciding whether it is worth continuing. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does maca contain plant oestrogens like soy or black cohosh?**
No. Maca does not contain phytoestrogens and is not believed to bind oestrogen receptors, which is part of why it is sometimes considered by women who prefer to avoid oestrogen-mimicking compounds specifically.

**Can maca help with hot flashes?**
The evidence here is limited and inconsistent, weaker than its evidence for mood and sexual function. If hot flashes are your primary symptom, other options have somewhat stronger supporting evidence.

**Is maca safe if I have a thyroid condition?**
Discuss this with your doctor first. Maca contains compounds that can affect thyroid hormone production in some people, so this is a genuine consideration, not just a theoretical caution.

**How long does maca take to work?**
Most trials assessing benefit used it consistently for around twelve weeks before evaluating effect, so a short trial of a week or two is unlikely to show much either way.

**Can I take maca alongside hormone therapy?**
Tell your doctor you are considering it. While a direct interaction is not well documented, your doctor can assess your overall situation and flag anything specific to your health history.
