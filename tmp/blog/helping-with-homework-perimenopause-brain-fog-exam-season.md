You used to be able to help with homework without a second thought, now you find yourself re-reading the same question three times, losing the thread of an explanation halfway through, or feeling your patience run out over something you'd normally handle without blinking. Board exam season, with its tuitions, test schedules, and constant small deadlines, makes this collide head-on with perimenopausal brain fog, and it can leave you feeling like you're failing your child when really, your brain is just working differently right now.

## Is It Normal to Suddenly Struggle With Things I Used to Explain Easily?

**Yes, and it's a specific, well-recognised pattern, not a sign something is wrong with your intelligence or your parenting.** Many women in perimenopause describe exactly this: subjects they once explained fluently now require more effort, more re-reading of the question, or a longer pause before the explanation comes together. This tends to be most noticeable with anything requiring you to hold several pieces of information in mind at once, a multi-step maths problem, a long chemistry equation, a history answer with several dates and names, rather than with simple recall. It's worth separating the cognitive slip itself, which is hormonal and temporary in character, from the harsher story you might tell yourself about what it means.

## How Do I Get Through Exam Season Without Losing My Patience?

**By lowering your own bar for what "helping" has to look like.** You don't have to be the one explaining every concept from scratch. A paid tuition class, a school WhatsApp group where parents share explanations, an older sibling or cousin, or simply letting your child attempt the problem with the answer key nearby and checking their reasoning afterward, are all legitimate forms of support that don't depend on your own cognitive bandwidth in the moment. Timing matters too: if mornings feel clearer than evenings, or vice versa, shifting homework sessions to your better window, even by an hour, can make a real difference. And when patience does run short, a short, honest pause, "give me five minutes, I need to reset," is a better model for your child than pushing through irritably.

## What Do I Say When My Child Notices I'm Struggling?

**Honesty, kept simple and age-appropriate, works better than pretending nothing's happening.** Something like, "My brain is having a slow day, it happens sometimes as you get older, let's figure this out together instead of me just telling you," is accurate and reassuring. It avoids both extremes, performing effortless competence you don't currently have, and over-explaining perimenopause in a way that worries a child unnecessarily. Most children are more understanding of a parent's honest "I need a minute" than of a sudden, unexplained burst of frustration, which is often what actually happens when the struggle goes unnamed.

## In the Indian Context

**Board exam pressure in India, CBSE, ICSE, and state boards alike, often falls disproportionately on mothers to manage, track, and support, on top of everything else perimenopause is already asking of your attention and patience.** It's entirely reasonable to involve your spouse, an older child, or extended family more deliberately during exam season rather than defaulting to doing it all yourself because that's simply how it's always been divided. If tuition classes are financially workable, even a modest investment, often a few hundred rupees per session depending on the subject and city, can meaningfully reduce the daily cognitive load on you during the toughest exam months. If brain fog is significantly affecting your daily functioning, not just occasionally but consistently, it's worth raising with your doctor, since ruling out thyroid changes, poor sleep, or iron deficiency alongside the hormonal picture is a reasonable step.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it possible my brain fog is affecting my child's academic results?**
It's unlikely to be the deciding factor on its own. What matters more is making sure your child has adequate support overall, whether that comes primarily from you, tuition, school, or a combination, rather than whether you personally explain every concept.

**Should I tell my child's school or teacher about what I'm going through?**
That's a personal choice, not a requirement. Some parents find it useful to mention perimenopause briefly if it's affecting their involvement in school communication, but it's entirely reasonable to simply adjust your support privately without disclosing it.

**Will this cognitive difficulty with explaining things improve over time?**
Many women notice brain fog easing somewhat as hormone levels stabilise after perimenopause, though it varies by individual. In the meantime, practical adjustments to how you provide support tend to help more than waiting it out.

**How do I stop feeling guilty about needing help with something I used to manage alone?**
Reframing it as reasonable delegation, not failure, helps. Plenty of parents without perimenopause already share homework support across tuition, family, and school, you're simply doing deliberately what many households already do by default.
