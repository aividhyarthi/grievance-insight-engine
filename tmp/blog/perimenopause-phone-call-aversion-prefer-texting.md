There was a time you would pick up a call from a friend and happily talk for forty minutes. Now the phone rings, you look at the screen, and something in you flinches before you even see who it is. You let it go to voicemail and text back "sorry, was busy, what's up?" even when you were sitting on the sofa doing nothing. Video calls are worse, the thought of being "on" for a camera, making the right facial expressions, filling silences, can feel like more effort than the call itself is worth. If this sounds familiar, you are not becoming cold or antisocial. A real, hormonally influenced shift in how much live, real-time conversation your nervous system can comfortably absorb is a common and under-discussed part of perimenopause.

## Why Live Calls Suddenly Take So Much Out of You

**A phone or video call is a much harder cognitive task than it feels like, once you break it down.** You have to listen, hold what was just said in mind, retrieve the right words to respond, read tone and pauses for meaning, and do all of this within a second or two, with no chance to pause, reread, or edit. Perimenopausal brain fog affects exactly the skills this depends on: working memory and quick word retrieval. Text removes almost all the time pressure, which is a large part of why it can feel like such a relief by comparison, not because you have less to say.

**Video calls add a second demand on top: watching yourself.** Seeing your own face in a small box while also trying to listen and respond is a form of split attention that many women describe as newly exhausting in perimenopause, distinct from the plain conversation fatigue of a voice call. If a video call leaves you unusually drained afterward, that self-monitoring load, not the conversation itself, is often the real cause.

**Sensory and mood sensitivity plays a role too.** Fluctuating oestrogen can lower tolerance for sustained noise and demand, so a call that used to feel like pleasant company can start to register as an intrusion, particularly at the end of a long day. This is closely related to the broader need for quiet many women notice in perimenopause, but it shows up specifically in the phone and video space because those formats offer no way to pace yourself the way an in-person conversation or a text thread does.

## This Isn't the Same as Losing Interest in People

**Wanting to text instead of talk is a format preference, not a relationship judgement.** Many women worry that avoiding calls means they are becoming distant or unkind, especially with parents or close friends who grew up treating an unanswered call as a small rejection. In most cases the opposite is true: the person still wants the connection, just in a format that does not demand instant, real-time performance.

**It is worth distinguishing this from a broader withdrawal from people altogether.** If texting still feels easy and enjoyable, and you are simply steering away from the live-call format, that is the capacity shift described here. If you find yourself avoiding contact in every format, including messages, and losing interest in people you usually care about, that points toward something broader, possibly low mood, worth mentioning to your doctor.

| Situation | Likely explanation | What tends to help |
|---|---|---|
| You reply to texts easily but dread calls | Real-time processing demand, not disinterest | Offer texting or voice notes as your default |
| A video call leaves you far more drained than a voice call | Added self-monitoring load from seeing your own face | Turn off self-view where the app allows it |
| An unscheduled call feels like an ambush | No time to prepare mentally for real-time demand | Ask people to text first to check if it's a good time |
| You avoid all contact, including messages, and feel low | Possible broader withdrawal, not just format preference | Worth raising with your doctor |

## Setting the Boundary Without It Landing as Rudeness

**In the Indian context, phone calls carry a lot of relational weight, particularly with parents, in-laws, or older relatives who may see an unanswered call as being ignored rather than as a format preference.** Saying it plainly tends to work better than letting the pattern speak for itself: "I find calls a bit much some days, can I message you back or call you at a set time instead?" is usually received better than a call quietly going unanswered week after week, which can be read as distance or even as a slight.

**Agreeing on a standing call time with the people who matter most removes the ambush element.** A Sunday evening call with parents, or a fixed weekly slot with a close friend, gives you the benefit of connection without the unpredictability of a phone ringing mid-task. If the aversion to calls is part of a wider pattern of needing more control over your social energy, [our community](/community) has other women discussing exactly this kind of boundary-setting within families that expect instant availability.

## When to Look Deeper

**If avoiding calls has expanded into avoiding almost all contact, or comes with persistent low mood, loss of interest in things you enjoy, or a sense of dread about any interaction, formal or informal, that pattern is worth a proper conversation with your doctor.** A format preference that still leaves room for real connection is different from a broader withdrawal that leaves you isolated, and only the second one needs medical attention rather than a scheduling fix.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why do I dread video calls specifically more than voice calls?**
Video calls add the extra task of watching and managing your own on-screen appearance while also listening and responding, which is a heavier cognitive load than voice alone. Turning off self-view, where the platform allows it, often reduces the fatigue noticeably.

**Is it rude to ask people to text instead of calling?**
Not if you say it plainly and offer an alternative, such as a scheduled call time or voice notes. Most people would rather know your preference than have calls go unanswered without explanation.

**Could this be social anxiety rather than perimenopause?**
The two can overlap, but a perimenopause-linked call aversion typically applies specifically to unplanned, real-time formats while texting and in-person contact with the same people still feel fine. If anxiety extends to most social situations, including in-person and text contact, it is worth discussing with your doctor separately.

**Will this phase pass?**
For many women it eases as hormones stabilise after menopause, though some permanently prefer text-first communication afterward simply because they have learned it suits them better. Either way, the aversion itself is not something to push through by force.
