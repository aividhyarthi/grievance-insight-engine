You used to be the one who bookmarked recipes, who tried a new sabzi because a cousin mentioned it at a wedding, who enjoyed the small creative puzzle of what to make for dinner. Now you stand in front of the fridge most evenings and make the same four or five dishes on a loop, not because you don't know how to cook anything else, but because even deciding on something new feels like more effort than you have left in you. If cooking has quietly gone from something you enjoyed to something you just get through, perimenopause is very likely part of why, and you are far from the only woman in her 40s describing exactly this.

## Why Did I Suddenly Lose Interest in Cooking Something New?

**It isn't really a loss of interest, it's a drop in available bandwidth.** Many women describe this exact confusion: they still enjoy eating well, still care about nutrition, and would say they "like cooking" if asked directly, yet the actual act of planning something unfamiliar now feels like homework. That gap between caring about food and having the mental energy to be adventurous with it is a hallmark of perimenopausal brain fog showing up in a very ordinary, daily task.

**The kitchen is a uniquely decision-dense space.** Unlike many chores, cooking a new dish involves a long chain of small choices in sequence: what to buy, what order to chop things, how long each step takes, what to do if something goes wrong midway. Each of those choices used to happen almost unconsciously. Now each one can require a small, deliberate effort, and that adds up fast across a single meal.

**Hormonal fluctuation genuinely affects motivation, not just memory.** Brain fog gets talked about mostly as forgetfulness, but the motivational piece matters just as much here. Dopamine, which is closely tied to oestrogen, plays a large role in why a new recipe used to feel like a fun project rather than a chore. When that reward signal dampens, the exact same task simply feels flatter and less worth the effort, even though nothing about your taste in food has changed.

**Related brain fog symptoms tend to show up alongside this one.** If you're also noticing word-finding trouble, forgetting items at the grocery store, or feeling overwhelmed by too many choices on a shelf, cooking fatigue usually isn't a standalone issue, it's one more face of the same underlying cognitive shift that's affecting other decision-heavy parts of your day.

## What Actually Helps When Cooking Feels Like Too Much

**Build a small, rotating repertoire on purpose, rather than feeling guilty about it.** Pick eight to ten meals you can make without thinking, write them down somewhere visible, and treat that list as a genuine meal plan rather than a sign you've given up. A repertoire you can execute on autopilot is not a lesser form of cooking, it's a sustainable one.

**Separate "deciding what to cook" from "cooking it" into two different moments.** Doing your weekly menu decision once, ideally when you have more mental energy (a weekend morning, for many women), rather than fresh every evening when you're depleted, removes the hardest part of the task from the moment you have the least capacity for it.

**Batch prep the boring parts in advance.** Washing and chopping vegetables, soaking dal, par-cooking a base gravy, these can often be done once for two or three days at a time, which shrinks the daily decision-and-effort load considerably even if the final cooking still happens fresh each day.

**Let "new" be occasional and low-stakes, not a weekly expectation.** If you do want to keep some novelty in your cooking, try one new dish every week or two on a day you have more energy, rather than expecting yourself to be adventurous every single evening. This protects the enjoyment of trying something new without it becoming another source of pressure.

**Delegate or simplify where you genuinely can.** If other members of the household are capable of cooking a meal or two a week, or a simpler one-pot dish can replace a multi-dish thali on your most tired days, using that flexibility is not cutting corners, it's matching your output to your actual capacity.

| Old pattern | What changed in perimenopause | What helps now |
|---|---|---|
| Trying a new recipe most weeks | Working memory and sequencing feel heavier | Save "new" for a lower-fatigue day, once every week or two |
| Deciding dinner fresh each evening | Decision fatigue peaks by evening | Decide the week's menu in one sitting, ideally earlier in the day |
| Enjoying the process of experimenting | Dopamine reward from novelty is blunted | Lean into a reliable rotation without guilt; enjoyment can return in smaller doses |
| Cooking most dishes from scratch daily | Daily effort compounds on top of fatigue | Batch-prep bases (chopped vegetables, soaked dal) over two to three days |

**In the Indian context**, the expectation that a woman, especially the primary home cook in a joint family, will produce varied, fresh, often elaborate meals daily is a significant unspoken load. Many women also feel they cannot openly say "I don't have it in me to cook something new right now" without it being read as laziness or a lapse in care for the family. Naming this as a real physiological symptom, the same way you would name fatigue or joint pain, can make it easier to ask for a simpler rotation or some help without feeling you are failing at a core role.

## When to See a Doctor

**If cooking fatigue is part of a broader pattern** of forgetfulness, word-finding trouble, or feeling mentally foggy that is affecting work or daily function, it is worth raising with your doctor or gynaecologist as part of your overall perimenopause picture, since there are genuine strategies and, for some women, hormone therapy that can ease cognitive symptoms.

**If you've also lost interest in eating itself**, not just in cooking something new, or notice significant changes in appetite or weight alongside low mood, that combination is worth a conversation with your doctor, since it can point to something beyond typical perimenopausal brain fog.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to lose all enjoyment in cooking during perimenopause?**
It's common to lose enjoyment in the effortful, decision-heavy parts of cooking, like planning something new, while still caring about eating well. This usually reflects dopamine and bandwidth changes rather than a genuine loss of interest in food itself.

**Will this get better, or is this permanent now?**
For many women, the intensity eases somewhat once hormone levels stabilise post-menopause, and building simpler systems (a rotating repertoire, batch prep) helps considerably in the meantime, regardless of where you are in the transition.

**Should I feel guilty about cooking the same meals repeatedly?**
No. A reliable, repeated rotation that gets a reasonably nutritious meal on the table with less daily effort is a legitimate adaptation, not a failure, especially while you're managing a genuine hormonal shift in mental bandwidth.

**Could this be linked to depression rather than perimenopause?**
It can overlap. If low mood, loss of interest in things you normally enjoy, or appetite and sleep changes are present more broadly, not just around cooking, it's worth mentioning to your doctor, since perimenopausal mood changes and depression can look similar and sometimes coexist.
