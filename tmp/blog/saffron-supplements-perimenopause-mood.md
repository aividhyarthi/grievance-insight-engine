Saffron, kesar, is one of the most expensive spices in the world and also one of the most familiar to Indian households, a pinch added to kheer, milk, or biryani on special occasions. It has a long history in Ayurvedic and Unani medicine as a mood-lifting, "heart-opening" substance, and in recent years it has also become one of the more seriously studied herbal supplements for low mood and anxiety in modern clinical research. That combination, old tradition and genuinely modern evidence, makes it worth a closer, honest look for perimenopausal mood symptoms specifically.

## Why Is Saffron Being Studied for Mood at All?

**Saffron contains compounds, including crocin and safranal, that researchers believe interact with serotonin and other mood-related brain chemistry**, which is part of why it has attracted more rigorous scientific attention than many traditional remedies. Unlike several herbs covered elsewhere in this series, where the evidence is mostly small animal studies, saffron has been tested in a meaningful number of randomised, placebo-controlled human trials specifically for mood, which puts it on comparatively firmer ground.

## What Does the Research Actually Show?

**Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses, pooling results across multiple small trials, have found that standardised saffron extract can meaningfully reduce symptoms of mild to moderate depression and anxiety, with effects in some studies comparable to low-dose conventional antidepressants over short treatment periods.** This is a genuinely more substantial evidence base than exists for many other herbal mood supplements, though it is worth being precise about its limits: most trials are still relatively small, short in duration (typically six to eight weeks), and conducted outside India, so how well the findings generalise to perimenopausal Indian women specifically is not established.

**No trial has tested saffron specifically in perimenopausal women, or specifically for hot flashes or other vasomotor symptoms.** The existing evidence is for general depressive and anxiety symptoms, not for the particular hormonal mood swings many women describe during this transition, so while the underlying mechanism may well be relevant, the direct evidence gap is real and worth acknowledging honestly.

## Does the Saffron in Your Kitchen Count?

**The doses used in clinical trials, typically around 30 milligrams a day of a standardised extract, are far higher than what most people use in cooking, where a pinch of a few strands flavours an entire dish.** This means the mood-related research applies to a concentrated supplement form, not to adding saffron to your evening milk, however pleasant that habit remains. Anyone considering saffron specifically for mood support should understand they are looking at a supplement product, not simply cooking with the spice more often.

## Is It Safe, and Who Should Be Cautious?

**At studied doses, saffron extract has generally been well tolerated in trials, with mild side effects like dry mouth, drowsiness, or appetite changes reported occasionally.** The more important caution is for women already on antidepressants or anti-anxiety medication, since combining serotonin-affecting substances carries a theoretical risk of excessive serotonin activity, and for anyone on blood-thinning medication, since saffron has a mild blood-thinning effect at higher doses. Pregnant women are typically advised against medicinal doses, though this is less relevant in perimenopause itself.

## In the Indian Context

**India's saffron market, like its broader supplement market, is loosely regulated, and saffron in particular is one of the world's most commonly adulterated spices, with cheaper plant material, dyes, or even synthetic colouring sometimes sold as genuine kesar.** This matters more for a supplement meant to be taken regularly at a specific, standardised dose than it does for the small pinch used in cooking. Buying a dedicated saffron supplement product from an established brand, rather than assuming kitchen-grade saffron threads are a reliable substitute, matters if you are genuinely trying to replicate what was studied. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does cooking with saffron give the same benefit as the mood studies?**
No. Clinical trials use a concentrated, standardised extract at doses well above what is typically used in cooking. Everyday culinary use is a pleasant habit but not equivalent to the studied supplement form.

**Can saffron supplements replace my antidepressant?**
No, and this should not be decided without your doctor. Saffron has reasonable evidence for mild to moderate symptoms, but stopping or replacing prescribed antidepressants without medical guidance is not advisable, particularly given the theoretical serotonin interaction risk between the two.

**Is saffron safe to combine with hormone therapy?**
There is no strong evidence of a direct interaction with hormone therapy specifically, but telling your doctor what you are taking, including supplements, remains good practice so they have your complete picture.

**How do I know if a saffron supplement is genuine?**
Given how commonly saffron is adulterated in the wider market, buying from an established, transparent brand rather than an unbranded or unusually cheap product improves your odds, though it is not a complete guarantee without lab testing.
