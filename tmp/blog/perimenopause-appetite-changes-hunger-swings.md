Some days, you are genuinely hungry within an hour of breakfast, thinking about your next meal before the last one has fully settled. Other days, you realise at 4pm that you haven't eaten anything since morning and don't feel particularly bothered by it. Your relationship with food, once fairly predictable, now feels like it has a mind of its own, and it can leave you second-guessing whether something is wrong, either with your body or your willpower. Neither is likely true; this is a genuine, hormone-driven shift many women experience in perimenopause.

## Why Does Appetite Swing So Much Now?

**Oestrogen and progesterone both influence hunger hormones directly.** Oestrogen affects leptin, the hormone that signals fullness, while progesterone can influence ghrelin, the hormone that signals hunger. As both hormones fluctuate unpredictably through perimenopause rather than declining smoothly, the hunger and fullness signals they regulate become correspondingly unpredictable.

**Blood sugar regulation becomes less stable.** Hormonal changes in perimenopause can affect insulin sensitivity, which means blood sugar can dip and spike more sharply than before, and those dips are often what drive sudden, intense hunger rather than a gradual, predictable build-up.

**Stress and cortisol scramble appetite in both directions.** For some women, elevated stress suppresses appetite almost entirely; for others, it drives strong cravings and a much bigger appetite than usual. Both are real cortisol-driven patterns, which is part of why the swings can feel so inconsistent from one stressful week to the next.

**Sleep quality affects appetite hormones independently.** Poor sleep, already common in perimenopause, is separately linked to higher ghrelin and lower leptin, meaning a bad night's sleep can drive a hungrier day regardless of what your hormones are doing on the reproductive side.

## What This Actually Looks Like

Women describe being ravenous by mid-morning on some days and genuinely forgetting to eat lunch on others, sudden, sharp hunger that feels almost urgent rather than gradual, a loss of interest in food during high-stress weeks followed by strong cravings once the stress eases, and a general sense of not being able to predict or trust their own appetite the way they used to.

## What Helps

**Don't judge a low-appetite day as a good day or a high-appetite day as a bad one.** Both are physiological, not a reflection of discipline or health; treating a big-appetite day as a failure tends to create more disordered patterns than it prevents.

**Prioritise protein and fibre at the meals you do eat.** These two nutrients do the most to stabilise blood sugar and extend fullness, which reduces how dramatic the next swing tends to be.

**Avoid long gaps without food, even on low-hunger days.** A very long gap tends to set up a sharper hunger crash later, so even a small, easy snack on a low-appetite day is often more helpful than skipping entirely.

**Keep hydration in the mix.** Thirst is often mistaken for hunger, and perimenopause can affect thirst cues too; a glass of water before assuming you need food is a reasonable, low-effort check.

**Track loosely for a few weeks if the unpredictability is bothering you.** A simple note of what you ate and roughly how hungry you felt can reveal a pattern, whether it's cycle-linked, stress-linked, or sleep-linked, that makes the swings feel less random.

**See a doctor for persistent, dramatic changes.** A sustained loss of appetite, unintentional weight loss, or appetite changes paired with other concerning symptoms should always be evaluated properly rather than assumed to be routine perimenopause.

## In the Indian Context

Indian meal culture often runs on set times and a strong social expectation to eat what's served, at a family meal, at a relative's house, at work. An unpredictable appetite can create quiet friction, being pressed to eat more on a low-hunger day, or feeling judged for a second helping on a genuinely hungry one. It's reasonable to gently push back on both, eating a smaller portion without over-explaining on a low-appetite day, or simply eating what your body needs on a hungrier one without apologising for it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to go a whole day barely thinking about food?**
Occasionally, yes, especially during high-stress or hormonally quieter periods. If it becomes frequent, prolonged, or comes with unintentional weight loss, it's worth mentioning to a doctor rather than assuming it's routine.

**Why am I suddenly hungrier than I've ever been, even after a full meal?**
This often reflects a mix of blood sugar instability and ghrelin fluctuation. Anchoring meals with protein and fibre, and avoiding long gaps between eating, usually reduces how intense and sudden this hunger feels.

**Could this be linked to weight gain in perimenopause?**
It can contribute, particularly if unpredictable hunger leads to skipped meals followed by overeating later. Eating consistently, rather than relying purely on hunger cues, tends to support more stable weight than reacting to each swing as it comes.

**Should I be worried this is something other than hormones?**
Mild, fluctuating appetite changes alongside other perimenopause symptoms are usually hormonal. Significant, persistent appetite loss, especially with unintentional weight loss or other unexplained symptoms, should always be evaluated by a doctor to rule out other causes.
