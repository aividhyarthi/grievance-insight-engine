Somewhere between a WhatsApp forward from a cousin and a targeted Instagram ad, most perimenopausal women in India end up looking at a supplement bottle, wondering if it is worth buying. Before getting into which specific supplements have real evidence behind them (that is a separate conversation, one worth having with your doctor), it is worth pausing on a more basic question: once you decide to buy something, how do you know what you are actually getting?

This is not a small concern. Unlike prescription medicines, supplements in India sit in a regulatory grey zone, and that has real consequences for what ends up in the bottle.

## Why "It's Just a Supplement" Is a Riskier Assumption Than It Sounds

**In India, most dietary supplements fall under the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI), regulated as a category of food rather than as medicine.** This matters because the evidentiary bar for a food product is very different from the bar for a drug. A pharmaceutical company has to prove a medicine is safe and effective in clinical trials before it can be sold. A supplement manufacturer generally has to show the product is safe to consume and meets basic labelling and manufacturing standards, but does not have to prove the health claims printed on the box.

**This gap shows up in real, documented ways.** Independent testing of supplements, both globally and in India, has repeatedly found products where the actual content does not match the label, sometimes containing less of the active ingredient than claimed, sometimes more, and occasionally containing contaminants, fillers, or undisclosed ingredients not listed at all. Herbal and "proprietary blend" products are particularly prone to this, partly because blends make it harder for a consumer (or even a lab) to verify individual ingredient amounts.

**None of this means every supplement on an Indian shelf is unsafe or fraudulent.** Many reputable manufacturers do maintain good quality control well above the regulatory minimum. But the point is that the licence to sell does not, by itself, tell you which category a given product falls into.

## What an FSSAI Licence Number Actually Tells You (and Doesn't)

**A genuine FSSAI licence number on the packaging confirms the manufacturer is registered and the product has cleared basic food-safety requirements.** It is worth checking for this as a minimum baseline, and being wary of any product, especially one sold only through social media or unverified online sellers, that does not display one clearly.

**What it does not confirm is potency, purity, or that the product contains what the label says in the amount the label says.** FSSAI licensing is not the same as a pharmaceutical-grade quality audit, and routine batch testing for supplement content is not a universal requirement the way it is for medicines.

**Third-party testing exists but is not mandatory.** Some manufacturers voluntarily submit products for independent lab verification (sometimes displayed as a certification mark on the packaging), which is a genuinely useful extra signal when present. Its absence does not automatically mean a product is bad, but its presence is a meaningfully positive sign worth looking for.

## Practical Checks Before You Buy

**Look for the FSSAI licence number and manufacturer details printed clearly on the packaging**, and be cautious of products where this information is missing, blurred, or only available on a website rather than the physical or digital product listing itself.

**Be skeptical of dramatic claims.** Words like "cure," "reverse," "balance your hormones instantly," or "clinically proven" (without a citation you can actually check) are marketing language more than scientific description. Genuine evidence-based supplements are usually described in more measured terms, because the underlying research itself is measured.

**Prefer single-ingredient, well-known supplements over vague proprietary blends when you can.** It is far easier to verify the quality of a straightforward vitamin D or magnesium product from an established manufacturer than a 12-herb "menopause support" blend where individual ingredient amounts are rarely disclosed.

**Buy from established pharmacies or manufacturers with a verifiable track record**, rather than unregulated online marketplaces or social media sellers, where counterfeit or substandard products are more likely to slip through.

**Check the batch number and expiry date**, standard practice for any health product, and store as directed, since heat and humidity common across much of India can degrade some supplements faster than their stated shelf life suggests.

| What to check | Why it matters | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| FSSAI licence number | Confirms basic registration and food-safety compliance | Missing, illegible, or unverifiable |
| Ingredient list and dosage per serving | Lets you compare against what your doctor recommends | Vague "proprietary blend" with no individual amounts |
| Manufacturer name and address | Traceability if something goes wrong | Only a brand name, no manufacturer details |
| Health claims made | Should be measured, not miraculous | "Cures," "reverses," "balances hormones instantly" |
| Where you're buying it | Established pharmacy or manufacturer reduces counterfeit risk | Unverified online seller or social media DM |

## In the Indian Context

**The supplement market in India has grown very fast, and enforcement has not always kept pace with the volume of new products entering the market, particularly online.** Social media advertising, WhatsApp forwards, and influencer endorsements have become major channels for supplement sales, and the products promoted this way are not necessarily held to a higher standard than what you would find on a pharmacy shelf, sometimes the opposite.

**Cost is not a reliable proxy for quality either direction.** An expensive imported product is not automatically better regulated than an affordable domestic one, and a cheap product is not automatically substandard. The FSSAI licence and ingredient transparency matter more than the price tag or the packaging's polish.

**This is a genuinely useful thing to raise with your gynaecologist or doctor, not just once but as an ongoing conversation.** Many doctors in India are used to patients quietly taking supplements without mentioning it, whether out of habit or a sense that it is not medically relevant. It is worth changing that habit: bring the actual bottle or a photo of the label to your appointment if you are unsure, so your doctor can look at the ingredient list directly rather than going on a brand name alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does FSSAI approval mean a supplement is proven to work?**
No. FSSAI licensing confirms the product meets basic food-safety and labelling requirements as a food category product, not that its health claims have been scientifically proven. Efficacy and licensing are two separate questions.

**Are imported supplements automatically better regulated than Indian ones?**
Not necessarily. Regulatory standards vary by country of origin, and an imported product sold through an unverified reseller may have unclear supply-chain quality control regardless of where it was manufactured. The same checks (licence details, ingredient transparency, reputable seller) apply either way.

**Should I stop taking a supplement I have already been using?**
Not automatically, but it is worth reviewing the product against the checks above, and mentioning it to your doctor at your next visit so they can flag any concerns specific to your health situation, especially if you are on hormone therapy or other regular medication.

**Is it safe to buy supplements through social media ads or WhatsApp forwards?**
This carries higher risk than buying from an established pharmacy or a manufacturer's verified listing, since counterfeit and substandard products are more commonly reported through these informal, less traceable channels.

**Why does this matter more in perimenopause specifically?**
Perimenopause is a stage where many women, often for the first time, start actively looking for supplements to manage symptoms, sometimes while also on hormone therapy or other medication. That combination makes both quality and potential interactions genuinely relevant, not just theoretical concerns.
