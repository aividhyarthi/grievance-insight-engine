Durga Puja and Dussehra used to mean five or ten nights of genuinely looking forward to the evening: a new outfit each night, pandal-hopping with friends until midnight, standing in the anjali queue, eating late at the food stalls, doing it all again the next day. Somewhere in your 40s, that same schedule started to feel less like celebration and more like an endurance event. By the third night your feet ache, the crowds feel louder than they used to, a hot flash hits right as you are squeezed into a queue with no room to move, and you are secretly doing the math on how early you can leave without anyone noticing. If pandal-hopping has quietly become something you dread rather than look forward to, perimenopause is very likely part of why, and there are real ways to get the joy back without giving up the season.

## Why Does a Festival You Used to Love Suddenly Feel Like So Much Work?

**Pandal-hopping asks your body to do several demanding things at once, for several nights in a row.** Standing for long stretches in a queue, navigating a packed crowd, staying out later than your usual bedtime, and eating off your regular schedule are each individually manageable. Stacked together across five or more consecutive nights, they add up to a genuine physical load, one that falling oestrogen makes harder to absorb and recover from than it used to be in your 20s and 30s.

**Crowds and noise are a well-documented hot flash and sensory-overload trigger in perimenopause.** As covered in our piece on crowds and noise feeling unbearable in perimenopause, dense, loud environments can genuinely overwhelm a nervous system that is already managing hormonal fluctuation, which is part of why a packed pandal at its loudest hour can feel disproportionately draining compared to how it felt a decade ago.

**Broken sleep across the festival stretch compounds everything else.** Staying out past midnight several nights running, especially with the excitement and sugar of festival food, disrupts the sleep that your body needs to recover from the physical demands of the day before, creating a cumulative deficit by the third or fourth night that is harder to climb out of than it used to be.

## Making the Nights Actually Work for You

**Go earlier in the evening when you can.** The crowds, heat, and noise at a pandal build steadily through the night; arriving at 7pm rather than 9pm often means a meaningfully calmer, cooler experience for the same visit, with less standing in the thickest part of the crowd.

**Build in a rest day between big nights if the schedule allows it.** Durga Puja and Dussehra calendars vary, but where you have any flexibility, alternating a big pandal-hopping night with a quieter one at home noticeably improves how you feel by the end of the stretch.

**Delegate the parts that drain you most.** If standing in a long anjali queue is the hardest part physically, going with a family member who can hold your place while you step aside briefly, or choosing a pandal known for a shorter queue, is a reasonable accommodation, not a compromise on devotion.

**Say the quiet part out loud to the people you're going with.** Telling a friend or family member "I want to do three pandals this year, not all of them, and I might need to sit down partway through" removes the pressure to perform stamina you do not currently have, and most people respond with far more understanding than you expect.

| What's draining you most | Likely perimenopause factor | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Standing in queues for hours | Joint stiffness, fatigue, circulation changes | Supportive footwear, go earlier, take seated breaks |
| Feeling overheated and flushed in the crowd | Hot flashes triggered by heat and dense crowds | Hand fan, loose breathable layers, hydration |
| Feeling overwhelmed by noise and lights | Sensory sensitivity | Choose quieter pandals or visit earlier in the evening |
| Exhaustion by day three or four | Cumulative sleep debt across multiple late nights | Space out big nights, protect at least one rest night |

## In the Indian Context

Durga Puja in particular carries deep community and emotional significance, especially for Bengali households where the five main days are effectively the calendar's emotional centre, which makes "doing less" feel like a bigger concession than it would for a more optional outing. It helps to reframe this clearly: choosing which nights and which pandals to prioritise is not skipping the festival, it is making sure you are actually present and enjoying the ones you do attend, rather than physically there but running on fumes. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to need more recovery time after festival nights than I used to?**
Yes, this is a very common experience in perimenopause. Broken sleep, standing for long stretches, and crowd-related stress all take a greater physical toll and take longer to recover from than they did in your 20s and 30s, and needing a quieter day or two afterward is a reasonable response, not a sign anything is wrong.

**Will hormone therapy help with festival-season exhaustion specifically?**
Hormone therapy is not prescribed for festival fatigue on its own, but if hot flashes, poor sleep, and fatigue are significant parts of your broader symptom picture, treating those underlying symptoms with your gynaecologist's guidance can meaningfully improve how festival-heavy weeks feel.

**What if my family expects me to attend every single night?**
A direct conversation ahead of the festival, explaining that you plan to attend a chosen few nights rather than all of them, usually goes better than quietly pushing through and then being too exhausted to enjoy any of it. Most families would rather you were present and genuinely enjoying three nights than depleted through ten.

**Are there specific foods that help get through a long pandal-hopping night?**
A protein-forward meal before you leave the house, rather than relying on festival snacks alone, helps steady your blood sugar and energy through the evening. Carrying a small water bottle and avoiding very sugary drinks in the heat also makes a noticeable difference to how you feel by the time you get home.
