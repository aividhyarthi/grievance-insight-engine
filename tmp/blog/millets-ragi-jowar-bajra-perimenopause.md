There is something almost funny about millets becoming a nutrition trend in urban India. For most of the country, ragi mudde, jowar bhakri, and bajra roti were never exotic health foods, they were simply what grandmothers cooked before rice and wheat took over daily plates. Now that perimenopause has brought blood sugar swings, bone density worry, and digestive changes into focus, millets are quietly worth a second, more deliberate look, not as a fad but as grains with a genuinely useful nutritional profile for this exact life stage.

## What each millet actually brings to the table

**Ragi (finger millet)** stands out specifically for calcium, containing considerably more per 100 grams than wheat or rice, which makes it a genuinely useful addition as oestrogen decline starts to affect bone density. It is also naturally gluten-free and has a mild, slightly nutty flavour that works well as mudde, dosa, or porridge.

**Jowar (sorghum)** is a good source of iron and has a reasonably neutral taste that adapts well into rotis and bhakri, making it an easy one-to-one style swap for a portion of your usual wheat flour. Its iron content is particularly relevant if heavier perimenopausal bleeding has been pulling down your ferritin levels.

**Bajra (pearl millet)** is traditionally considered a "warming" grain and is often eaten more in winter in parts of North India. It brings a good combination of fibre, magnesium, and some protein, and its slower digestion contributes to the steadier blood sugar response millets are generally known for.

## Why the glycaemic angle matters specifically in perimenopause

**Blood sugar instability becomes more noticeable for many women during perimenopause**, partly because fluctuating oestrogen affects insulin sensitivity, showing up as sharper energy crashes, stronger sugar cravings, and sometimes increased abdominal weight gain. Millets generally digest more slowly than refined wheat flour or white rice because of their fibre and starch structure, which translates into a gentler rise and fall in blood sugar after a meal rather than a sharp spike and crash.

**This does not mean millets are a treatment for blood sugar problems**, and they are not a substitute for proper evaluation if you are experiencing symptoms that suggest prediabetes or diabetes, which becomes more common around this age. It simply means that, meal for meal, a millet roti tends to be a gentler choice than the same quantity of refined wheat or white rice.

## A realistic way to bring millets back into your kitchen

**You do not need to replace rice and wheat entirely**, which is neither necessary nor, for most Indian households, practical. A realistic approach is rotating millets in a few times a week, ragi dosa for breakfast, a jowar or bajra roti alongside dinner once or twice, or millet khichdi as a lighter meal option, rather than treating it as an all-or-nothing dietary overhaul.

**If you are new to cooking with them**, millet flours behave differently from wheat, they can be slightly more crumbly and benefit from resting the dough and using a bit more water than you would for wheat atta. Pre-mixed multi-millet flours are widely available now in Indian grocery stores and can be an easier starting point than sourcing and grinding individual grains yourself.

## In the Indian context

**Millets have the advantage of already being deeply embedded in regional Indian cooking**, which makes this one of the easier evidence-aligned dietary changes to make, since you are not importing an unfamiliar "superfood" but reviving something your own grandmother likely cooked regularly. Many Indian states have also been actively promoting millet cultivation and availability in recent years, so good quality ragi, jowar, and bajra flour is more accessible and often more reasonably priced (commonly ₹60 to ₹120 per kilogram depending on the grain and brand) than many imported "health food" alternatives marketed for similar benefits.

**If you have thyroid concerns**, which become more common around perimenopause, it is worth knowing that bajra and some other millets contain goitrogenic compounds that can mildly interfere with thyroid hormone production in very large quantities, though this is generally not a concern at normal dietary intake, especially if your iodine intake is adequate. If you have a diagnosed thyroid condition, mention your millet intake to your doctor rather than assuming it is irrelevant.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can millets replace calcium or iron supplements if I'm deficient?**
No. If a blood test shows a genuine deficiency, food alone, including millets, is usually not enough to correct it quickly, and your doctor may still recommend supplementation. Millets are a good ongoing dietary habit, not a treatment for a diagnosed deficiency.

**Are millets safe to eat every day?**
Yes, for most people, millets are a traditional staple safely eaten daily across many parts of India. The only specific caution is for people with diagnosed thyroid conditions, who should discuss overall diet, not just millets specifically, with their doctor.

**Do millets help with weight gain in perimenopause?**
They are not a weight-loss food on their own, but their fibre and lower glycaemic impact can support better satiety and steadier blood sugar, both of which indirectly support weight management as part of an overall balanced diet.

**Which millet is best to start with if I've never cooked with them?**
Ragi is often the easiest starting point since ragi dosa and ragi porridge are widely familiar and simple to make, and pre-mixed multi-millet flours are now widely available for a gentler introduction than cooking with a single unfamiliar grain.
