Every Indian kitchen already knows its greens, palak, methi, sarson, coriander, moringa, but during perimenopause they earn a more deliberate place on your plate. Their deep colour comes from chlorophyll and a cluster of nutrients and plant compounds that support exactly the systems under pressure at this stage: bones, mood, blood, and heart. This is the first in a short series on eating by colour through perimenopause, starting with the green end of the spectrum.

## Why Green Matters Right Now

**Calcium and vitamin K for bones.** As covered in our guide to preparing your bones for perimenopause, falling oestrogen accelerates bone loss. Leafy greens are among the richest plant sources of calcium, and vitamin K, found generously in greens, plays its own supporting role in bone health, working alongside calcium and vitamin D rather than instead of them.

**Folate and B vitamins for mood and energy.** Folate, plentiful in leafy greens, supports the brain chemistry involved in mood regulation, relevant given how much perimenopause affects mood and energy, as covered in our guides to anxiety and fatigue during this stage.

**Iron, especially valuable with heavier periods.** As covered in our guides to heavy bleeding, iron loss is a real risk in perimenopause. Leafy greens contain non-haem iron, and eating them with a source of vitamin C (lemon, tomato, amla) meaningfully improves how much of that iron your body actually absorbs.

**Magnesium for sleep and cramps.** Magnesium, present in good amounts in greens like spinach, supports the sleep and muscle-relaxation issues covered in our guide to restless legs and insomnia.

**Gentle plant compounds and fibre.** Cruciferous greens in particular (methi, mustard greens, and their cousins cabbage and broccoli) contain compounds studied for their role in oestrogen metabolism, alongside fibre that supports the gut-hormone connection covered in our related guide.

## The Greens Worth Prioritising

**Palak (spinach)** — versatile, iron- and folate-rich, easy to fold into dal, sabzi, or a simple palak paneer.

**Methi (fenugreek leaves)** — slightly bitter, calcium-rich, traditionally used in Indian kitchens through hormonal transitions; methi thepla or methi dal are easy, regular ways in.

**Sarson (mustard greens)** — rich in calcium and vitamin K, classically paired with makki di roti, though it works well simply sautéed too.

**Moringa (drumstick leaves, saijan patta)** — exceptionally nutrient-dense, with notable calcium, iron, and vitamin content; a small daily portion, even as a powder stirred into dal or soup, goes a long way.

**Coriander and mint** — used generously as garnish rather than a main vegetable, but their frequent, daily use adds up, and both bring genuine nutritional value beyond flavour.

**Cabbage and broccoli** — not leafy in the traditional Indian sense, but part of the same cruciferous family as methi and sarson, worth including for the same reasons.

## How to Eat More Without It Feeling Like a Project

**Add a handful to what you already cook.** Stir chopped palak or methi into dal, khichdi, or a vegetable sabzi you are already making, rather than treating greens as a separate dish to prepare.

**Keep frozen greens on hand.** Frozen spinach and similar greens retain most of their nutritional value and remove the barrier of daily fresh preparation, useful on busy days.

**Pair with vitamin C for iron absorption.** A squeeze of lemon over a greens-based dish, or a side of tomato or amla, meaningfully improves iron uptake, worth doing as a habit rather than an afterthought.

**Do not overcook them.** Very long cooking reduces some nutrient content. A shorter sauté or a quick blanch preserves more of what makes greens valuable.

**Rotate your greens.** Different greens carry slightly different nutrient profiles, so a rotation through palak, methi, sarson, and moringa over a week offers more than relying on just one.

## A Word on Choosing Your Greens

Buying seasonal, local greens, and washing them thoroughly, is a sensible, practical way to eat well without needing to chase specific organic certifications. If organic options are accessible and affordable to you, they are a reasonable choice, particularly for reducing pesticide exposure, but the single most impactful habit remains simply eating greens regularly, in whatever form is realistic for your kitchen and budget.

## When to See a Doctor

**Routine appointment** to discuss your diet as part of your broader perimenopause care, particularly if you have concerns about bone health, iron levels, or are considering how food fits alongside any treatment you are on.

**Also worth checking** your vitamin D and iron levels with a simple blood test, since greens support but do not replace addressing an existing deficiency, which needs to be identified and, where necessary, treated directly.

Green leafy vegetables are one of the simplest, most accessible ways to support your body through perimenopause, and for most Indian kitchens, the ingredients are already familiar. The habit worth building is not a dramatic overhaul, just a more deliberate, regular place for greens on your plate.
