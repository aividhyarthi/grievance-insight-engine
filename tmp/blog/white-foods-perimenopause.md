Garlic, onion, ginger, cauliflower, mushroom, these are the aromatics and everyday staples that begin most Indian meals, so ordinary they rarely get a second thought. But this white and pale-toned group of foods carries its own distinct set of plant compounds, ones worth understanding specifically for perimenopause. This is the second in our short series on eating by colour through this transition, following our guide to green leafy foods.

## Why White and Aromatic Foods Matter Now

**Allicin and immune support.** Garlic contains allicin, a compound studied for supporting immune function, relevant given the more frequent colds and infections some women notice in perimenopause, as covered in our guide to immunity during this stage.

**Quercetin and anti-inflammatory support.** Onions are rich in quercetin, a plant compound with anti-inflammatory properties, relevant to the increased pain sensitivity and joint aches many women experience as oestrogen falls, covered in our related guides.

**Ginger for nausea, inflammation, and digestion.** As covered in our guide to nausea in perimenopause, ginger is a well-established, gentle remedy. It also supports digestion, relevant to the constipation and gut changes covered in our digestive health guide.

**Fibre and gut-supporting compounds.** Garlic and onion contain prebiotic fibres that feed beneficial gut bacteria, tying directly into the gut-hormone connection covered in our estrobolome guide, a healthy gut microbiome supports healthy oestrogen regulation.

**Vitamin D from mushrooms.** Mushrooms, particularly when exposed to sunlight during growth, are one of the few plant sources of vitamin D, a nutrient covered extensively in our supplements guide as commonly deficient in India and important for bone health.

**Cauliflower's cruciferous benefits.** Like its greener cruciferous cousins, cauliflower contains compounds studied for their role in supporting healthy oestrogen metabolism, alongside fibre and vitamin C.

## The Staples Worth Prioritising

**Garlic (lehsun)** — used daily in most Indian cooking already; the habit worth building is simply not skipping it, and adding a little extra where the dish allows.

**Onion (pyaz)** — similarly already central to most meals; red onions in particular carry higher levels of beneficial plant compounds than white onions, worth choosing when convenient.

**Ginger (adrak)** — beyond chai, fresh ginger grated into dal, sabzi, or steeped in hot water is an easy daily habit, particularly useful on days with nausea, bloating, or joint discomfort.

**Cauliflower (phool gobi)** — works well roasted, in a sabzi, or added to parathas, an easy way to bring cruciferous benefits to the table without the slightly stronger flavour of greens like methi or sarson.

**Mushroom** — increasingly available and versatile, mushroom sabzi or added to a mixed vegetable dish is a simple way to include a genuinely useful, less common nutrient source.

**Radish and white pumpkin** — other pale, commonly used vegetables in Indian cooking that round out this group with fibre and hydration.

## How to Eat More Without It Feeling Like a Project

**Do not skip the tadka.** The garlic, ginger, and onion used to start a dal or sabzi are not just flavour, they are a meaningful, daily nutritional habit already built into how most Indian food is cooked. Simply being a little more generous with them is a low-effort win.

**Keep ginger tea as a tool, not just a habit.** A cup of ginger tea specifically when nausea, bloating, or joint discomfort flare can be a genuinely useful, immediate comfort measure, beyond its place in a daily routine.

**Include mushroom weekly, if you can.** Given how few plant foods offer vitamin D, a regular mushroom-based dish is a specific, worthwhile addition rather than an occasional afterthought.

**Choose fresh over pre-minced where practical.** Fresh garlic and ginger, prepared close to cooking, generally offer more of their beneficial compounds than long-stored, pre-processed versions.

## A Word on Freshness and Sourcing

As with the greens covered in our earlier guide, choosing fresh, seasonal versions of these staples, rather than heavily processed or long-stored alternatives, tends to preserve more of their beneficial compounds. This is less about a strict organic requirement and more about a general return to less processed, freshly prepared food, closer to how earlier generations cooked, which naturally aligns with better nutrition regardless of certification.

## When to See a Doctor

**Routine appointment** to discuss your diet as part of your broader perimenopause care, particularly around bone health and vitamin D, given how commonly deficient it is in India.

**Also worth checking** your vitamin D levels directly with a blood test, since food sources like mushroom support but are unlikely to fully correct an existing significant deficiency on their own.

Garlic, onion, ginger, cauliflower, and mushroom are already woven into how most Indian food is cooked, which makes them some of the easiest, lowest-effort additions to lean into more deliberately during perimenopause. Small, consistent habits here add up meaningfully over time.
