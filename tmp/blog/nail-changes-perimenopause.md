Nails that have suddenly become brittle, peeling at the edges, developing visible ridges, or simply growing more slowly than they used to, are a small, easily overlooked, but genuine sign of the hormonal changes of perimenopause. It is rarely talked about compared with hot flashes or mood swings, but many women notice their nails behaving differently during this transition, and there is a clear reason why.

## Why Nails Change in Perimenopause

**Oestrogen supports keratin production.** Nails are made largely of keratin, the same protein found in hair and skin, and oestrogen plays a role in supporting healthy keratin production and the overall structure of the nail. As oestrogen declines and fluctuates in perimenopause, nails can become thinner, more brittle, and more prone to splitting or peeling, part of the same broader pattern affecting skin and hair.

**Reduced moisture affects nails too.** Just as skin becomes drier with falling oestrogen, nails and the surrounding cuticle can lose moisture, making them more brittle and prone to cracking, and cuticles more prone to dryness and minor irritation.

**Circulation changes can affect nail growth.** Nail growth relies on healthy blood flow to the nail bed, and some of the circulatory changes associated with falling oestrogen may contribute to nails growing more slowly or appearing to have new ridging.

**Nutritional factors can compound it.** As with hair, nail health depends on adequate protein, iron, and certain vitamins and minerals. If diet, digestion, or heavy periods have affected your nutritional intake, as covered in our guide to digestive changes in perimenopause, this can show up in nails too.

## What This Looks Like

Common perimenopausal nail changes include increased brittleness, with nails that chip or split more easily than before, peeling at the tips or in layers, vertical ridges running the length of the nail (a very common, usually harmless ageing change that can become more noticeable during this transition), slower growth than you are used to, and dry, sometimes irritated cuticles.

## What Helps

**Moisturise nails and cuticles, not just hands.** Applying a nourishing hand cream or cuticle oil directly to the nail and surrounding skin, particularly after washing hands or bathing, helps combat the dryness behind brittleness and splitting. Regular application matters more than any single product.

**Protect nails from harsh exposure.** Wearing gloves for washing dishes, cleaning, or gardening reduces exposure to water and chemicals that further dry and weaken already-more-fragile nails.

**Keep nails trimmed and filed gently.** Shorter nails are less prone to catching and splitting. File in one direction rather than a sawing motion, which stresses the nail structure.

**Support nutrition.** Adequate protein, iron, and biotin (found in eggs, nuts, and whole grains) support nail health, much as they support hair. If diet has been affected by digestive changes or heavy periods, addressing the underlying cause, as covered in our related guides, helps here too.

**Avoid harsh nail products.** Frequent use of strong nail polish removers (particularly acetone-based ones) and gel or acrylic overlays can further dry and weaken nails already more fragile from hormonal change. Giving nails occasional breaks between treatments helps.

**Be patient.** Nails grow slowly, typically taking several months to fully regrow, so any improvement from changed habits takes time to become visible. Consistency matters more than quick fixes.

**Consider the broader picture.** Nail changes are rarely a symptom worth treating in isolation, they usually respond well simply as part of the general skin, hair, and nutrition care that helps with other perimenopausal changes.

## When to See a Doctor

**Routine appointment** if nail changes are significant, persistent despite good care, or you are concerned, particularly if they come alongside other symptoms like fatigue or hair changes, so an underlying nutritional deficiency (iron, vitamin D, or biotin) or thyroid issue, all common at this age, can be checked with a simple blood test.

**Promptly** if you notice **nail changes that look distinctly abnormal**, such as new dark streaks or spots under the nail, significant thickening or separation from the nail bed, nails that become spoon-shaped, or signs of infection like redness, swelling, pus, or pain around the nail, as these need proper assessment rather than being assumed to be simply hormonal.

Brittle, changing nails are a small, real part of the perimenopausal picture, driven by the same falling oestrogen behind so much else in this stage. A little extra care and patience usually goes a long way toward restoring their strength.
