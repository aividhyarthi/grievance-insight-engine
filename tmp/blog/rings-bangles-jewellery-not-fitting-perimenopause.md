Your mother's ring that has fit your finger for twenty years suddenly won't go past the first knuckle. The bangles you wear every day leave a groove in your wrist by evening. You take off your rings before bed out of habit now, something you never used to think about. If this has crept up on you in your 40s, you're not imagining it, and you're not alone. It is one of the quieter, more personal changes of perimenopause, and almost nobody mentions it until it happens to them.

## Is it actually water retention, or is it weight gain

**This is the first question most women ask themselves**, often with some anxiety. The honest answer is that it can be either, and sometimes both, but there are a few clues that point more toward fluid retention specifically. Water-retention swelling tends to change noticeably within a single day. It is often symmetrical, showing up in both hands or both ankles rather than just one side. Pressing a finger into the skin over your shin may leave a brief dent (doctors call this pitting). It also tends to track loosely with your cycle if you're still getting periods, worse in the days before bleeding starts and easier afterwards.

Gradual weight gain, by contrast, tends to be steadier across weeks and months rather than swinging within a day, and it usually shows up across the whole body rather than concentrated in fingers and wrists. Many women in perimenopause are dealing with both at once: a general shift in body composition (more on that below) layered on top of day-to-day fluid swings. If you genuinely can't tell which it is, that's a reasonable thing to raise with your doctor rather than guess.

## Why your hands specifically

**Hands and feet are where fluid tends to pool first**, partly because of gravity and partly because the skin and tissue there have less room to expand quietly compared to, say, your abdomen. That's exactly why a kilogram or two of fluid retention that you might not notice anywhere else becomes very obvious the moment a ring won't come off.

**Weight-bearing and hormone-sensitive tissue matters here too.** The same oestrogen receptors that respond to hormonal swings elsewhere in the body are present in the blood vessels and connective tissue of your hands, which is part of why this symptom clusters so specifically around jewellery rather than, say, your shoes (though some women do notice that too, especially by evening or on a flight).

## What actually helps

| Approach | What it does | How much it tends to help |
|---|---|---|
| Reducing added salt | Lowers fluid retention triggered by high sodium intake | Moderate, often noticeable within a few days |
| Moving your hands and wrists through the day | Improves circulation, reduces pooling | Mild to moderate |
| Elevating hands/feet when resting | Helps fluid drain away from extremities | Mild, short-term relief |
| Staying hydrated (sounds counterintuitive, but works) | Dehydration can make the body hold onto water harder | Moderate |
| Tracking the pattern across a full cycle | Tells you if it's hormonal and predictable, or worth flagging to a doctor | Diagnostic, not a treatment, but genuinely useful |
| Hormone therapy, for some women | Can ease fluid retention tied to erratic oestrogen swings, among other symptoms | Varies; a conversation for your gynaecologist |

**Don't rush to resize everything.** It is tempting, the first time a favourite ring gets stuck, to run straight to the jeweller. But if the swelling is genuinely cycle-linked or day-linked, a ring resized today to fit a swollen finger may be loose a week later. Many women find it more practical to keep a well-fitting ring on a chain for the days it won't go on, rather than permanently altering it, especially for rings with sentimental or religious significance like a wedding ring or mangalsutra pendant that's part of a chain already.

**In the Indian context**, this symptom carries an extra layer for many women because of how often bangles, rings, and a mangalsutra are worn daily as markers of marital status, not just as accessories you can simply decide to stop wearing. Glass and gold bangles in particular are notoriously unforgiving when a wrist swells, and some women have described genuine panic at a wedding or festival when a bangle won't slide off and has to be broken or soaped off. If you know you swell by evening, it's worth putting bangles on for the day before any swelling sets in, rather than trying to force them on later.

## When to actually see a doctor about it

Most jewellery-fit changes in perimenopause are benign and hormone-driven. But a few patterns are worth a proper check, ideally with your gynaecologist or a general physician:

- **Swelling only on one side** (one hand, one leg), which can point to a circulation issue unrelated to hormones.
- **Sudden, significant swelling over a day or two**, especially with shortness of breath, chest discomfort, or a rapid weight gain of several kilograms in a short window, which needs urgent medical attention (call 112 in a genuine emergency).
- **Swelling that doesn't ease up at all through the month**, which may point to a thyroid issue, since thyroid changes are common in this same age group and can also cause puffiness.
- **Pain, redness, or warmth in the swollen area**, which should be checked promptly rather than assumed to be hormonal.

A doctor can run basic checks, including thyroid function and kidney function if needed, to rule out anything beyond ordinary perimenopausal fluid shifts. If it is confirmed as hormonal, this is also a reasonable moment to bring up whether hormone therapy might help this and other symptoms together; a quick symptom check on our [free quiz](/quiz) can help you organise your symptoms before that appointment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal for my wedding ring to fit differently depending on the time of day?**
Yes, this is one of the more common and genuinely benign patterns. Fluid naturally shifts toward your hands as the day goes on, and hormonal swings in perimenopause can amplify this. It doesn't necessarily mean anything is wrong.

**Should I get my rings resized permanently?**
Only if the change has been stable for a few months and isn't swinging with your cycle or the time of day. For a short-term or fluctuating change, consider wearing the ring on a chain instead, or simply waiting it out, before paying for a permanent resize.

**Can hormone therapy help with this specific symptom?**
Some women report that stabilising oestrogen through hormone therapy reduces the fluid swings that cause this, among other symptoms, but it isn't typically prescribed for jewellery fit alone. It's worth mentioning alongside your other symptoms in a conversation with your gynaecologist.

**Is this connected to the general "water retention" perimenopause symptom?**
Yes, it's the same underlying mechanism (oestrogen fluctuation affecting fluid balance), just showing up specifically and visibly in jewellery because hands are where fluid tends to pool first and most noticeably.

**My bangles leave deep marks on my wrist by evening. Is that a concern?**
A temporary indentation that fades within an hour or two is usually just ordinary fluid settling and not a medical concern. If the mark is painful, doesn't fade, or the skin underneath looks discoloured, get it checked.
