Methi is already in most Indian kitchens, tucked into dal, parathas, and the spice box used for tempering, long before anyone thinks of it as a "remedy." So when it comes up as a suggestion for hot flashes or blood sugar swings in perimenopause, it doesn't feel like trying something new and unfamiliar, it feels like using more of something you already cook with. That familiarity is part of its appeal, but it is worth separating from what the actual evidence says about whether it helps, and how much you would realistically need.

## What Does the Evidence Actually Show for Blood Sugar?

**This is where fenugreek has its strongest, most consistent evidence base.** Several small clinical studies, including some conducted in India, have found that fenugreek seed supplementation can modestly reduce post-meal blood sugar spikes and improve insulin sensitivity, likely due to its high soluble fibre content, which slows carbohydrate absorption. For perimenopausal women dealing with the blood sugar swings that can worsen cravings, energy crashes, and mood instability, this is a genuinely relevant, plausible mechanism, not just a cultural assumption.

**This matters specifically for perimenopause because fluctuating hormones can themselves affect insulin sensitivity**, meaning some women notice blood sugar swings becoming more pronounced during this transition even without a diabetes diagnosis. A stabilising effect on blood sugar, even a modest one, can indirectly ease the mid-afternoon energy crash or sudden hunger many women describe.

## What About Hot Flashes Specifically?

**The evidence here is thinner and more inconsistent.** A small number of studies have looked at fenugreek extract for menopausal hot flash frequency and severity, with some reporting a modest improvement, but the overall body of research is limited in size and not yet robust enough for a doctor to confidently recommend fenugreek as a proven hot flash treatment the way they might discuss hormone therapy. If reducing hot flashes specifically is your main goal, it is reasonable to view fenugreek as a low-risk option worth trying alongside other strategies, not a primary solution to rely on alone.

## How Much Would You Actually Need?

**Realistically, more than typical cooking use provides.** Most studies showing a measurable effect use concentrated doses, often several grams a day of fenugreek seed powder or extract, considerably more than the teaspoon or so used in a dal tempering. This doesn't mean cooking with methi regularly is pointless, it still contributes fibre and nutrients as part of a generally healthy diet, but it does mean expecting a noticeable symptom effect from normal cooking amounts alone is probably unrealistic.

**A common traditional method many Indian women already use is soaking a teaspoon or two of methi seeds overnight in water and drinking the water, or eating the softened seeds, in the morning.** This is a reasonable, low-risk way to get a somewhat higher and more concentrated dose than cooking alone, though it still falls short of the amounts used in most clinical studies, and consistency matters more than any single dose.

## In the Indian Context

**Fenugreek is inexpensive and already present in most Indian kitchens, making it one of the more accessible natural options to try, typically costing just tens of rupees for seeds that last weeks.** Concentrated fenugreek supplements, sold as capsules or standardised extract, are also available in India but fall under the same loosely regulated supplement market as other herbal products, so dose and purity are not guaranteed to match what's printed on the label. If you are considering a concentrated supplement rather than culinary use, buying from an established pharmacy and discussing the specific product and dose with your doctor is a more reliable approach than picking whichever bottle is cheapest online.

**If you are diabetic or prediabetic and on medication, this is one of the more important disclosure conversations to have with your doctor**, since fenugreek's blood sugar lowering effect can add to your medication's effect and increase the risk of your blood sugar dropping too low. This is not a reason to avoid it, but a reason to use it with your doctor's knowledge rather than quietly alongside your existing treatment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I just eat more methi in my cooking instead of taking a supplement?**
You can, and it's a reasonable, low-risk habit to build, but don't expect the same effect size seen in studies using concentrated doses. It's a sensible complementary step rather than a guaranteed fix.

**Is fenugreek safe to take alongside hormone therapy?**
There's no well-documented dangerous interaction, but as with any supplement, tell your doctor you're using it so they have your complete picture, particularly given its blood sugar effect if you're also managing diabetes.

**Does fenugreek taste or smell affect breast milk or appetite the way it's sometimes described for lactation?**
That specific effect is more relevant to breastfeeding contexts and isn't directly related to perimenopausal use, though it's worth knowing fenugreek does have a distinct taste and smell that can come through in sweat or breath at higher intake.

**How long before I'd notice any effect on blood sugar or hot flashes?**
Most studies that found an effect used consistent daily intake over several weeks to a few months, so a single use or a few days is unlikely to tell you much either way.
