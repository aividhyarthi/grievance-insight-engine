You used to be the one who organised the group trip, who never missed a coffee catch-up, who remembered everyone's birthdays. Somewhere in the last couple of years, you started cancelling more, replying to messages days late, and choosing the couch over the group chat, not because you don't love these women anymore, but because you genuinely don't have it in you most evenings. And now the group chat has gone quieter too, and you're not sure if that's your doing or just what happens.

Loneliness rarely gets listed alongside hot flashes and irregular periods, but for many women, perimenopause reshapes their social world as much as it reshapes their body. It deserves to be named as a real symptom, not a personal failing.

## Why does perimenopause specifically make you want to withdraw?

**Fatigue changes what socialising costs you.** Disrupted sleep from night sweats or 3am waking builds up over months, and by the time evening plans roll around, the energy that used to go toward a dinner out or a long phone call simply isn't there. This isn't laziness, it's a real energy deficit that most people don't account for when comparing you to your ten-years-ago self.

**Mood swings make social situations feel unpredictable, even to you.** If irritability or sudden tearfulness has become more common, many women start avoiding situations where they might snap at a friend or embarrass themselves, and cancelling starts to feel safer than showing up as an unreliable version of yourself. The withdrawal is often protective, not indifferent.

**Hormones themselves affect social motivation.** Oestrogen interacts with the brain's reward and bonding systems, including oxytocin pathways involved in feeling connected to others. As oestrogen fluctuates and declines, some women describe a genuine drop in the pull toward socialising, not just the energy for it, which can feel confusing and even a little frightening if you've always considered yourself a social person.

**Life stage timing compounds all of it.** Perimenopause tends to land in the same decade as caregiving for ageing parents, older children's exam pressures, career demands, and often a partner going through their own midlife adjustments. The maths simply doesn't leave much room for friendship maintenance, even before hormones enter the picture.

## Why friendships specifically seem to fade, not just get quieter

**Friendships built around a shared life stage can naturally drift when that stage ends.** Mum friends from your children's toddler years, colleagues from a job you've since left, neighbours from a home you moved out of: these connections were often held together by proximity and shared routine more than by deep, effortful closeness, and once the routine changes, so does the friendship, with or without perimenopause involved.

**Reciprocity breaks down quietly.** If you've been the one initiating plans and remembering details for years, and then stop for a stretch because you don't have the capacity, some friendships don't survive the pause, simply because no one else was holding up that side of it. This says more about how the friendship was structured than about your worth as a friend.

**Shame keeps people from explaining what's happening.** Many women don't tell friends why they've gone quiet, out of embarrassment about mood swings, weight changes, or simply not wanting to explain perimenopause to someone who hasn't been through it yet. The silence gets misread as disinterest on both sides.

| Sign it's likely hormonal withdrawal | Sign it may need more support (talk to a doctor) |
|---|---|
| You still want connection, just don't have the energy to initiate | You've lost interest in connection altogether, even with low effort |
| Comes and goes with sleep, cycle timing, or stress | Persistent low mood most days for two weeks or more |
| You feel better after a good low-pressure catch-up | Even good interactions don't lift your mood |
| Mostly about energy and unpredictability, not hopelessness | Accompanied by hopelessness, worthlessness, or loss of interest in everything |

## What actually helps

**Lower the bar for what counts as connection.** A 10 minute voice note instead of a planned outing, a walk instead of a dinner, a "can't talk long but wanted to say hi" text: these keep a friendship alive without requiring the energy you don't currently have.

**Say the actual thing, briefly.** "I've been really tired and a bit all over the place lately, it's not about you" does more to preserve a friendship than silence does, even if you don't want to explain the full hormonal picture.

**Address the sleep and mood symptoms directly where you can.** Improving sleep quality and getting mood swings evaluated, sometimes with hormone therapy, sometimes with other approaches, often restores social energy as a side effect, because the underlying fatigue and volatility ease up.

**Seek out people who already understand.** Connecting with other women going through the same transition removes the burden of explaining yourself from scratch, and can feel like a relief compared to maintaining friendships that require more translation.

## In the Indian context

Indian women in their forties are frequently the emotional and logistical centre of a joint family: managing ageing in-laws, children's academics, household coordination, and often a full-time job, all at once. Friendship, the kind that exists outside family obligation, is often the first thing to be quietly deprioritised, not because it doesn't matter, but because it's the one relationship with no built-in expectation attached to it.

There's also a cultural undercurrent that frames socialising as slightly indulgent once you're a mother and a homemaker deep into midlife, "kitty parties" dismissed as frivolous even as they serve a genuine emotional function. Reclaiming even a small, regular space for friendship, a monthly call, a WhatsApp group that isn't just family logistics, an hour at the park, is not indulgent, it's a real piece of managing this stage of life well.

If loneliness has started to feel heavier than tiredness, if it's tipping into persistent low mood, hopelessness, or thoughts of not wanting to be around, please reach out for support rather than carrying it alone. iCall's mental health helpline (9152987821, Monday to Saturday, 8am to 10pm) offers confidential support, and in a crisis, India's emergency number is 112. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to feel lonelier during perimenopause?**
Yes, many women describe a genuine increase in loneliness during this stage, driven by fatigue, mood changes, and a heavier caregiving load that leaves less time and energy for friendships.

**Why do I feel less interested in socialising than I used to?**
Hormonal changes, particularly fluctuating and declining oestrogen, can affect brain pathways linked to social motivation and bonding, alongside the simple fact that fatigue and mood swings make socialising feel more effortful than it used to.

**Will my friendships come back once perimenopause symptoms ease?**
Many do, especially if you stay lightly in touch during the harder stretch rather than going fully silent. Some friendships naturally fade regardless, which is a normal part of life stages shifting, not a perimenopause failure.

**How do I explain to friends why I've been distant without oversharing?**
A short, honest line works well: something like "I've been dealing with some health stuff and my energy has been low, I still care about staying in touch" gives enough context without requiring a full explanation.

**When does loneliness in perimenopause need professional support rather than just more social effort?**
If low mood persists most days for two weeks or more, if you've lost interest in things you used to enjoy even with low-effort options, or if you feel hopeless, that's a sign to talk to a doctor or reach out to a mental health helpline rather than waiting it out.
