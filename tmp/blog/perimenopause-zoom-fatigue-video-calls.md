You join a video call, and within seconds your eyes are not on your colleagues, they are on the small square of your own face in the corner. You notice the flush creeping up your neck before you have said a single word. You notice how tired your eyes look under the office lighting, how your face seems to sag slightly compared to how you picture yourself. By the time the meeting actually starts, half your attention has already gone somewhere else entirely: managing how you look, rather than listening to what is being said. If video calls have started leaving you more drained than they used to, and a strange, low-grade self-consciousness has crept into meetings you once found unremarkable, perimenopause is very likely part of the reason.

## Is This Actually a Perimenopause Thing, or Am I Just Vain?

**Neither framing is quite right, and it is worth saying plainly: this is not vanity.** "Zoom fatigue" as a general phenomenon has been studied since large-scale video calling became routine, and researchers have specifically identified the self-view mirror effect as a measurable contributor to exhaustion and anxiety on video calls, for people of every age and life stage. What perimenopause adds is two extra layers on top of that baseline effect: visible physical symptoms (flushing, sweating) that you did not have to manage on camera before, and a broader, often unspoken process of getting used to a changing face, which the self-view window puts directly in front of you for hours a week.

**The hot flash visibility piece is genuinely specific to perimenopause.** In an office meeting room, a flush might pass largely unnoticed by colleagues focused on a discussion, or explained away quickly as "it's warm in here." On a webcam, especially with typical laptop camera lighting that tends to wash faces out or catch redness starkly, a flush can feel like it is on full, undeniable display, which understandably makes some women tense up in anticipation before a call even starts, a kind of pre-emptive self-consciousness that adds to the fatigue before the meeting has begun.

## What Actually Helps?

**Turn off self-view once the call has started.** Every major video platform allows you to hide your own video feed while keeping your camera on for others, meaning you no longer see yourself but the meeting continues normally for everyone else. This single change is consistently one of the most effective fixes researchers and workplace wellness experts point to, and it costs nothing and requires no explanation to anyone.

**Adjust lighting to reduce visible flushing rather than eliminate it.** Facing a light source rather than sitting with a window or light behind you reduces the harsh shadowing that makes flushing look more dramatic on camera than it does in a mirror. A slightly cooler-toned light, rather than a warm yellow one, also tends to soften how visible redness reads on screen.

**Keep a small desk fan or handheld fan within reach during calls.** Being able to cool down quickly at the first sign of a flush, without leaving the call, reduces both the physical intensity of the flash and the anxiety of anticipating one.

**Consider camera position and framing.** A camera at eye level, roughly arm's length away, tends to be less harshly close-up than a laptop camera angled upward from a low desk, which can exaggerate exactly the features (under-eye tiredness, jaw softness) that already feel like a sore point for many women in this stage.

## How Video Call Fatigue Compares to In-Person Meeting Fatigue

| Factor | In-person meeting | Video call |
|---|---|---|
| Visibility of facial flushing | Often unnoticed by others, easy to attribute to room temperature | Highly visible, close-up, well-lit |
| Self-monitoring | Minimal, attention naturally goes outward | Constant, via the self-view window |
| Ability to step away briefly for a flash | Easier, can step out unnoticed | Harder, absence is more visible on screen |
| Cognitive load | Single task: following the conversation | Dual task: following conversation and monitoring self-image |
| Control over lighting/appearance | Limited | Significant, if adjusted deliberately |

## In the Indian Context

**Video calls have become a fixture of Indian work life across sectors**, from IT and consulting to teaching and client-facing roles, and hybrid and remote arrangements mean many women now spend a genuinely large share of their working week on camera in a way earlier generations never had to navigate. Raising this with a manager can feel awkward in workplace cultures where symptoms are rarely discussed openly, but framing it practically, requesting flexibility to keep camera-on calls shorter, or asking whether audio-only is acceptable for certain internal meetings, is a reasonable, low-drama ask that many managers accommodate without needing a full explanation of why.

**If flushing on calls is frequent enough to genuinely affect your confidence or work performance, it is worth naming as a specific hot flash pattern to your doctor**, since treatment options exist, and a free check on `/quiz` can help clarify whether what you are noticing fits a broader vasomotor symptom pattern worth discussing at your next appointment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to feel more anxious before video calls than I used to?**
Yes, many women describe a specific pre-call anticipatory tension once they associate video calls with visible flushing or self-consciousness. This tends to ease once you have a few practical strategies in place, like turning off self-view and having a way to cool down quickly if needed.

**Does turning off self-view actually make a measurable difference?**
Yes, this is one of the more consistently studied fixes for general video call fatigue, not something specific to perimenopause. Removing the constant mirror effect reduces cognitive load and self-consciousness for most people who try it, and it takes seconds to do on any platform.

**Should I just keep my camera off instead?**
That is a reasonable option for some meetings, particularly internal ones, but constantly opting out can affect how present or engaged you appear to be in some workplace cultures. A middle ground, camera on with self-view off, tends to work well for most women without requiring an explanation.

**Can hot flashes on video calls be treated the same way as other hot flashes?**
Yes, hot flashes are hot flashes regardless of the setting that makes them feel more visible. Lifestyle adjustments, and where appropriate hormone therapy, both work the same way; it is worth mentioning to your doctor that video calls specifically make you more self-conscious about flushing, as that detail can help shape the conversation about how much this is affecting your daily life.
