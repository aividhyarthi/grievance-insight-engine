You used to be able to sit through a full workday in an air-conditioned office without thinking about it twice. Now you're the one carrying a stole to every meeting, arguing with a colleague over the thermostat, or stepping out of a cold cab into a wave of heat that makes you dizzy. If air-conditioned rooms, cars, and malls have started feeling genuinely intolerable, either too cold, too abrupt a shift from outside heat, or somehow both within the same hour, you're not imagining it and you're not being difficult. Your body's thermostat has changed, and artificial climate control exposes that change more than almost any other everyday situation.

## Is It Normal to Feel Cold When Everyone Else Is Comfortable?

**Yes, and it's one of the more confusing versions of this symptom because it seems to contradict the hot flashes many women expect from perimenopause.** The same hormonal shifts that trigger sudden heat can also trigger sudden cold, sometimes within the same day, because both are driven by an unstable internal thermostat rather than a fixed shift toward "always hot" or "always cold". Some women notice they run cold most of the time now, others swing between the two, and both patterns are consistent with what's happening hormonally.

## Why Does the AC-to-Outdoors Transition Feel So Bad Specifically?

**The transition itself is often the real trigger, not just the cold room.** Your body has to work harder to recalibrate when the temperature change is sudden and large, which describes almost every AC transition in India: a hot commute into a cold office, a cold mall into a hot parking lot, a cold cab AC blasting after standing in the sun. A narrowed thermoneutral zone means these everyday transitions, which barely registered a few years ago, can now trigger a flush, a chill, dizziness, or a headache.

## What Actually Helps?

**Layering is the single most practical fix.** A light shawl, cardigan, or stole that you can add or remove in seconds lets you adjust faster than asking someone to change the thermostat, and it works whether the issue is the AC being too cold or a hot flash arriving on top of it. **A small personal desk fan or a hand fan gives you control** over your immediate space without needing to negotiate the room's setting with everyone else. **Where you can influence it, ask for a seat away from the direct AC vent**, many offices have genuinely uneven cooling, and a seat further from the vent can solve the problem without any conversation about temperature at all. **Staying hydrated and avoiding caffeine right before a known cold-to-hot or hot-to-cold transition** (like just before leaving an air-conditioned office) can also reduce how sharply your body reacts.

## In the Indian Context

**Indian offices, malls, cars, and public transport are often kept colder than many women find comfortable even outside perimenopause**, which means this symptom collides with an environment that wasn't built with a fluctuating thermostat in mind. **Thermostat disagreements at work are common enough that it's worth naming what's actually happening rather than staying quiet about it**: saying "I run hot and cold more than I used to, would it be alright if I sat away from the vent" is a small, low-friction way to advocate for yourself without turning it into a bigger conversation than it needs to be. **At home, joint families often share one common AC setting**, and if this is a genuine daily struggle, it's worth having a simple, matter-of-fact conversation with family about needing a shawl or a personal fan nearby rather than assuming everyone will notice on their own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is being cold in AC a hot flash symptom or a separate thing?**
They're related but not identical. Both come from the same underlying instability in how your body regulates temperature, hot flashes and cold sensitivity, including in air-conditioned spaces, are two expressions of the same hormonal shift rather than two unconnected symptoms.

**Will this get better once I'm through menopause?**
For many women, temperature sensitivity does settle somewhat once hormone levels stabilise post-menopause, though the timeline varies considerably. Hormone therapy, where appropriate, can also meaningfully reduce vasomotor symptoms including this kind of temperature instability, worth discussing with your doctor if it's significantly disrupting your day.

**Should I ask my doctor about this specifically, or is it too minor?**
It's worth mentioning, especially if it's frequent or disruptive. Temperature dysregulation is part of the same vasomotor symptom picture as hot flashes, and your doctor may factor it into decisions about hormone therapy or other treatment, so it's genuinely useful information for them, not a minor complaint.

**Could this be a thyroid issue instead of perimenopause?**
It's worth ruling out, since thyroid conditions can also cause temperature sensitivity and overlap significantly with perimenopause symptoms. If temperature intolerance is new, severe, or accompanied by other symptoms like unexplained weight change or heart rate changes, ask your doctor for a thyroid panel alongside your other tests.
