You forget a term everyone under thirty seems to know instantly. You're in a meeting run by someone who could have been your junior a few years ago, and now reports differently, decides differently, communicates on a different app entirely. Add a hot flash, a word you can't find, or a wave of self-doubt that arrives out of nowhere, and a normal Tuesday at work can start to feel like proof that you don't belong there anymore. If the gap between you and your younger colleagues has started to feel wider and more personal since perimenopause began, you're noticing something real, and it's worth naming rather than quietly absorbing.

## Is It Normal to Feel Awkward Around Younger Colleagues Now?

**Yes, and it's worth separating what's genuinely hormonal from what's simply a normal generational adjustment that happens at this career stage regardless of perimenopause.** Every generation eventually works alongside, and often for, people younger than them, that's an ordinary feature of a long career, not a personal failing. What perimenopause adds is a layer of lowered confidence and heightened emotional reactivity that can make an ordinary adjustment feel like something has gone specifically wrong with you.

## What If I'm Reporting to Someone Younger Than Me?

**This is increasingly common and, on its own, says nothing about your competence or worth.** It can still feel genuinely uncomfortable, especially if it triggers comparisons to where you expected to be at this stage of your career. **Separating the discomfort from the substance helps**: if the working relationship itself is respectful and functional, the age gap is simply a fact, not a problem to solve. If specific behaviour, like being dismissed, talked over, or excluded, is the actual issue, that's worth addressing directly, the same way you would with a manager of any age.

## How Do I Handle Feeling "Behind" on Tools or Terminology?

**Ask directly, briefly, and without over-explaining.** "I haven't used this platform before, can you walk me through it once?" lands very differently than apologising extensively or staying silent and struggling. Most younger colleagues respond well to a direct, confident request for a quick explanation, and repeatedly not asking, out of fear of looking out of touch, usually costs you more visibly than the asking would have.

## What Do I Actually Bring That They Don't?

**Institutional memory, pattern recognition, and judgment built over years are genuinely valuable and often underestimated in the moment, including by you.** Knowing why a past approach failed, how a client actually behaves under pressure, or which shortcuts create bigger problems later are not soft, sentimental advantages, they're the kind of judgment that takes time to build and that younger colleagues, however sharp, simply haven't had time to accumulate yet.

## In the Indian Context

**Indian workplaces are shifting faster on hierarchy and age norms than they were a decade ago**, particularly in urban, tech-adjacent, and startup-influenced sectors, which means more women in their 40s are navigating flatter structures and younger managers than earlier generations typically did. **This shift is genuinely disorienting and worth acknowledging out loud with peers going through the same thing**, rather than assuming you're the only one struggling with it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it a bad sign for my career if I'm now reporting to someone younger?**
Not inherently. Reporting structures shift for many reasons unrelated to your ability, including how fast a company grows or restructures. It's worth evaluating the working relationship itself rather than the age gap alone.

**How do I stop comparing myself to younger, faster-seeming colleagues?**
Try noticing when the comparison is really about speed of recall (which brain fog affects) versus quality of judgment (which experience improves). Separating the two usually reveals you're comparing yourself unfairly on one narrow measure while ignoring several where you're stronger.

**Should I tell colleagues I'm going through perimenopause if it's affecting my confidence at work?**
That's a personal decision with no universally right answer. Some women find naming it to a trusted colleague or manager relieves pressure and invites understanding; others prefer to manage it privately. Either approach is valid, choose based on your specific workplace and relationships.

**Could my confidence drop around younger colleagues actually be depression or anxiety rather than perimenopause?**
It's worth checking, since the two can overlap and coexist. If low confidence has become persistent low mood, hopelessness, or anxiety that doesn't lift, that's worth raising with a doctor directly, separately from the general workplace adjustment discussed here.
