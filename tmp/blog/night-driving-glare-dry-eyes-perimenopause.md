If you've started wondering whether it's just "bad roads and worse drivers," or something about you, it's worth knowing that perimenopause can genuinely change how your eyes handle driving after dark.

## Why Does Glare Suddenly Feel So Much Worse?

**This usually comes down to the eye's tear film, and it genuinely changes with hormonal shifts.** A healthy, stable tear film helps light pass evenly across the surface of the eye. When the eye is dry, the tear film breaks up unevenly, scattering incoming light rather than letting it focus cleanly, which is a large part of why oncoming headlights can suddenly look like they're blooming or starbursting instead of forming a clean, manageable point of light. Oestrogen plays a role in maintaining the glands that produce the watery and oily components of tears, so its decline and fluctuation during perimenopause can plausibly worsen dry eye for a meaningful number of women, even those with no previous eye problems.

**It's not just glare itself, it's how long it takes your eyes to recover after being dazzled**, and a drier, less evenly lubricated eye surface tends to take longer to settle back to normal vision after a bright flash, which matters enormously on a dark highway with a steady stream of oncoming traffic.

## Is It Just My Eyes, or Is Fatigue Making It Worse Too?

**Usually both, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which is the bigger factor on any given night.** If you're also dealing with disrupted sleep from night sweats or early waking, general tiredness slows reaction time in a well-documented way, independent of anything happening in your eyes specifically. Combine a slower reaction time with reduced tolerance for glare, and a drive that used to feel routine can genuinely feel more demanding, not because you're imagining it, but because two separate effects are stacking on top of each other.

## What Actually Helps

**Lubricating eye drops used proactively, not just when your eyes already feel dry, make a real difference for many women**, and preservative-free options are widely available in India without a prescription, worth trying before assuming you need to see an eye specialist first. **A genuinely clean windscreen and headlights matter more than people think**: a film of dust or a slightly hazy windscreen scatters light considerably more than a clean one, and this is a simple, cheap fix many people overlook. **Having your own car's headlights checked and correctly aimed**, rather than assuming they're fine, also reduces how much glare you cause for others and can reduce how your own lights reflect oddly off wet roads or your own windscreen.

**On nights when you're genuinely exhausted from poor sleep, it's worth being honest about whether you should be the one driving at all**, particularly for longer, unfamiliar night routes, rather than pushing through out of habit or not wanting to ask someone else.

## When It's More Than Just Perimenopause

**If night vision problems are new, rapidly worsening, or accompanied by other visual changes like increasing difficulty with bright lights during the day, haloes around lights that weren't there before, or a general sense that your vision has dimmed, it's worth getting an eye check rather than assuming it's purely hormonal.** Cataracts become more common from your 40s onward and can specifically worsen glare and night vision, and this is easily checked and, when needed, very effectively treated, so it's not something to quietly attribute to perimenopause indefinitely without ruling it out.

## In the Indian Context

**Indian roads at night add their own layer of difficulty on top of any of this**: inconsistent streetlighting, vehicles with poorly aimed or excessively bright aftermarket headlights, and a higher density of oncoming traffic on many stretches all make glare more frequent here than in some other driving contexts. This isn't something you can fix personally, but it's worth acknowledging that if night driving feels harder for you now, the roads themselves are also genuinely more demanding, not just your eyes.

**Preservative-free lubricating eye drops are widely available at any pharmacy in India, usually costing under ₹200 for a bottle that lasts several weeks, making this one of the more affordable, low-effort fixes worth trying first.** If dryness is significant and drops alone aren't enough, an eye specialist (ophthalmologist) visit at a general hospital or eye clinic is reasonably accessible in most Indian cities and worth the appointment rather than persisting with discomfort indefinitely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can hormone therapy help with dry eyes and night driving glare?**
Some women notice an improvement in dry eye symptoms with hormone therapy, since it's addressing the hormonal driver, but it's not a guaranteed or primary treatment for dry eye specifically. It's worth discussing with your gynaecologist as part of your overall symptom picture, alongside dedicated dry eye treatment if needed.

**Are preservative-free eye drops safe to use every day long-term?**
Generally yes, preservative-free lubricating drops are designed for frequent daily use and are considered low risk, but if you find you need them constantly throughout the day, it's worth seeing an eye specialist to check whether something more is needed.

**Should I stop driving at night altogether if this is happening to me?**
Not necessarily, but be honest about your limits on any given night, particularly when you're especially tired or the symptoms feel worse than usual. For many women, proactive drops and route familiarity make ordinary night driving manageable again.

**Is this the same as the general vision changes people describe in perimenopause, like blurry vision?**
It's related but more specific. General blurry vision in perimenopause has several possible causes, while this particular pattern, glare sensitivity and slow recovery after bright light, points specifically toward dry eye as the main contributor.
