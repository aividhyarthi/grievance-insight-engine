A manager points out a minor error in your report, phrased mildly, the kind of comment you would once have noted and moved past within minutes. Now it sits with you for the rest of the day, replaying in your head, your chest tight, your face hot, maybe even tears you have to blink back at your desk. If constructive feedback has started feeling disproportionately painful in your 40s, and you are wondering whether you have simply become "too sensitive," the more likely explanation is hormonal, not a character change.

## Why Does Feedback Suddenly Feel So Much Bigger Than It Used To?

**Oestrogen has a direct relationship with serotonin activity and with the amygdala, the brain region that processes threat and emotional intensity.** As oestrogen fluctuates unpredictably during perimenopause, that regulatory system becomes less stable, which means a comment your brain would once have filed away quickly can instead get flagged as more threatening or significant than it objectively is, and the emotional charge around it can take longer to fade.

**This overlaps closely with the broader emotional volatility many women describe in perimenopause, greater irritability, a shorter fuse, and reactions that feel bigger and longer-lasting than the situation warrants.** Criticism at work is simply one of the more common and consequential places this shows up, because professional feedback already touches on competence and reputation, two things most people are naturally sensitive about even without a hormonal amplifier.

## Is This the Same as Just Being "Too Sensitive"?

**No, and that framing tends to do real damage.** There is a meaningful difference between a genuine, biologically driven shift in emotional regulation and a character trait, and collapsing the two leads women to blame themselves for a physiological change that is not a personal failing. Feedback landing harder right now does not mean your professional judgement or resilience has declined; it means one specific regulatory system, the one that normally helps buffer emotional reactions, is going through a temporary period of instability.

**It is also worth distinguishing this from feedback that genuinely is unfair, harsh, or delivered poorly.** Not every strong reaction to criticism is purely hormonal; sometimes the feedback itself is badly delivered, disproportionate, or part of a pattern worth addressing separately with HR or a mentor. Part of steadying yourself is learning to tell the two apart: is this reaction bigger than the situation warrants because of where you are hormonally, or is the situation itself actually a problem that deserves a direct response?

## How Do You Respond in the Moment Without Overreacting?

**Building in a short pause before responding is the single most useful habit here.** Reacting immediately, when the amygdala's threat response is running hot, tends to produce either an overly emotional response or a defensive one, neither of which serves you well professionally. A simple "thank you, let me think that over and get back to you" buys minutes or hours for the acute reaction to settle before you have to formulate a response.

**Physically regulating your body helps more than trying to argue yourself out of the feeling.** A few slow breaths, stepping away from your desk briefly, or a short walk can lower the physiological arousal enough that your thinking brain comes back online, rather than staying stuck in reactive mode.

**Writing down your actual response before saying it out loud, even just a few bullet points, can help separate the emotional charge from the substance of what you actually want to say**, so that when you do respond, it reflects your considered judgement rather than the heat of the initial reaction.

## In the Indian Context

**Indian workplace culture often places a high premium on composure, particularly for senior or visibly professional women, which can make an intense reaction to feedback feel like a double failure, both the reaction itself and being seen having it.** It helps to remember that a moment of visible emotion, a flushed face, briefly unsteady voice, rarely registers to colleagues with the significance it carries for you internally; most people are focused on their own tasks and unlikely to dwell on it the way you will.

**Hierarchical workplace structures common in many Indian organisations mean feedback often comes from someone senior, in a setting where pushing back or asking for time to process can feel risky.** A polite, brief request, "may I take a day to reflect on this and follow up," is a professional norm in most contexts and unlikely to be read as defiance, even in more traditional workplace cultures.

**If reactions to feedback are affecting your work relationships or your own confidence significantly, and this pattern is showing up alongside other perimenopause symptoms, it is worth mentioning to a doctor or gynaecologist as part of your overall picture.** Treatment options, including hormone therapy, can help stabilise mood and emotional regulation for many women, and connecting with others navigating similar work situations through a space like /community can also help you feel less alone in it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to cry after mild feedback that never used to bother me?**
Yes, this is a commonly reported experience in perimenopause and reflects a real shift in emotional regulation, not a personal or professional shortcoming. It does not mean you are unable to handle feedback; it means your current physiological state is making an ordinary moment feel more intense than it is.

**Should I tell my manager this is a perimenopause-related reaction?**
That is entirely your choice and depends on your relationship with your manager and your workplace culture. Some women find a brief, factual mention helpful for context; others prefer to manage it privately. Neither approach is wrong.

**How do I know if the feedback itself was actually unfair, rather than my reaction being amplified?**
Revisiting it after the initial emotional charge has settled, ideally a day later, usually gives you a clearer read. If it still feels disproportionate, harsh, or unclear once you are calm, that is worth addressing directly rather than assuming it was purely your own reaction.

**Can hormone therapy help with emotional reactions to things like feedback?**
For many women, stabilising hormone levels through treatment can reduce mood volatility and improve emotional regulation generally, which may make moments like this feel less overwhelming. Whether it is appropriate for you is a conversation to have with a gynaecologist based on your full symptom picture.
