Somewhere between the third time you forget why you walked into the kitchen and the night you lie awake replaying a conversation from 2014, a lot of Indian women end up back at the same family medicine cabinet shelf, looking for the small bottle of shankhpushpi syrup that an older relative once swore by for "mental tiredness." It is one of Ayurveda's oldest and most widely recommended herbs for the mind, sold everywhere from neighbourhood pharmacies to large Ayurvedic chains, usually pitched at students before exams and, later in life, at anyone whose memory and calm seem to be slipping. Whether it has anything to offer the specific, hormonally driven brain fog and anxiety of perimenopause is a separate question from its long tradition of use.

## What is shankhpushpi, and why is it considered a "brain herb"?

**Shankhpushpi, named for its small, conch-shaped flower, is classed in Ayurveda as a medhya rasayana, a category of herbs specifically believed to support memory, intellect, and mental clarity.** It has traditionally been given to students before exams, to people recovering from mental exhaustion, and, more generally, to anyone whose family notices their memory or composure beginning to slip with age. It is most commonly sold in India as a sweetened syrup, though capsules and powders are also available, often in combination with other brain-focused herbs like brahmi or jatamansi.

**One complication worth knowing upfront: "shankhpushpi" is not always the same plant.** Depending on the region and manufacturer, products sold under this name may actually contain *Convolvulus pluricaulis*, *Evolvulus alsinoides*, or occasionally other botanically distinct plants, all traditionally grouped under the same common name. This makes it harder to compare research findings directly across products, since a study on one species may not apply cleanly to a bottle bought under the same label elsewhere.

## What does the research actually show?

**The honest picture here is similar to several other Ayurvedic herbs discussed for perimenopause: promising early signals, but no large, well-designed human trials on the specific symptoms women are hoping it will help.** Small studies, several conducted in animal models, suggest shankhpushpi may have mild anxiolytic (anxiety-reducing) and antioxidant effects on brain tissue, along with some evidence of improved learning and memory performance in those limited settings. A handful of small human studies, often in students or in combination formulas rather than shankhpushpi alone, have reported modest improvements in measures of anxiety and cognitive performance.

**What is missing is research specifically on perimenopausal women, whose brain fog and anxiety have a distinct hormonal driver rather than general mental fatigue or exam stress.** The existing evidence base simply was not built with this particular population or mechanism in mind, so any benefit for perimenopausal symptoms specifically remains unproven rather than established.

## Why perimenopausal brain fog needs more than a calming herb

**Perimenopausal brain fog and anxiety are driven by fluctuating and declining oestrogen's effect on attention, working memory, and mood-regulating brain chemistry**, a mechanism quite different from general mental fatigue or exam-related stress. A herb that may offer mild calming or antioxidant support is unlikely, on its own, to meaningfully change that underlying hormonal picture, even if it leaves you feeling somewhat more settled day to day.

**Other common contributors deserve a look before assuming it is "just" perimenopause.** Thyroid dysfunction, poor sleep, iron deficiency, and anxiety or depression unrelated to hormones can all worsen cognitive symptoms and are worth ruling out with your doctor, especially if brain fog or anxiety is affecting your work or daily functioning significantly.

## In the Indian context

**Shankhpushpi syrup is inexpensive and widely available, often ₹100 to ₹250 for a bottle, which makes it an easy, low-commitment first step for many families before considering anything else.** Because of the species-identity issue mentioned earlier, buying from an established Ayurvedic pharmacy or brand with transparent sourcing, rather than an unbranded product, at least improves your odds of a genuine, consistent formulation, though it will not guarantee a clinical effect.

**It is also worth saying plainly within the family:** if an older relative insists shankhpushpi alone should fix noticeable memory or concentration problems, it is entirely reasonable to keep using it as a gentle, low-risk addition while still seeing a doctor about the underlying cause, rather than treating the two as competing options.

## A reasonable way to think about trying it

**If your brain fog and anxiety are mild and you want a low-risk, traditional option to try alongside lifestyle measures, discussing shankhpushpi with your doctor is reasonable**, particularly if you are not on medication it could interact with. If symptoms are significant enough to affect work, relationships, or daily function, that is the point to prioritise a proper medical evaluation, which might include checking your hormone levels, thyroid function, and discussing options like hormone therapy, rather than waiting to see if a herbal syrup helps first. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is shankhpushpi the same thing in every bottle sold in India?**
Not necessarily. Several different plants are traditionally grouped under the common name "shankhpushpi," so product identity can vary between brands and regions, which is worth knowing if you are trying to compare research findings to a specific product.

**Can shankhpushpi replace hormone therapy for brain fog?**
No. There is no evidence it addresses the hormonal mechanism behind perimenopausal brain fog. It may offer mild general calming or cognitive support, but it is not a substitute for medical treatment if symptoms are significant.

**Is it safe to take alongside hormone therapy or antidepressants?**
Tell your doctor before combining it with any medication, including hormone therapy, antidepressants, or sedatives, since formal interaction data is limited and your doctor can assess your specific situation.

**How long does it take to notice any effect?**
There is no standardised timeline from strong clinical trials. Traditional use generally suggests several weeks of consistent use before judging an effect, alongside tracking your symptoms independently so you have an honest record to discuss with your doctor.

**Should I try it before seeing a doctor, or after?**
If your symptoms are mild, there is little harm in discussing shankhpushpi with your doctor at a routine visit. If brain fog or anxiety is significant or worsening, see a doctor first rather than waiting to see if a herbal option helps.
