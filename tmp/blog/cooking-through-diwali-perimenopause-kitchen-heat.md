Diwali kitchens run hot even in the best of years: multiple burners going, oil bubbling for chaklis and shakarpara, sugar syrup for mithai reaching a rolling boil, a house full of people expecting fresh food for days on end. Add hormonal hot flashes to that already-hot kitchen, and the festival that is supposed to feel joyful can start to feel like an endurance test. If the thought of another marathon cooking day is filling you with dread rather than excitement this year, you are far from the only one, and there are real ways to make it more manageable.

## Why Does Kitchen Heat Make Hot Flashes So Much Worse?

**Standing close to direct stove heat, hot oil, and rising steam adds real, physical heat exposure on top of whatever your body is already doing hormonally.** Hot flashes are triggered when your brain's temperature-regulation centre, already made more sensitive by fluctuating oestrogen, overreacts to a small rise in core body temperature. Standing over a kadhai of hot oil for chaklis, or a pot of sugar syrup for gulab jamun, raises your immediate environmental heat considerably, which can be exactly the nudge that sets off a flash, sometimes several in a single long cooking session.

**This is why a lot of women notice their worst flash days cluster around festival cooking**, not because the hormones are doing anything different that week, but because the external heat trigger is so much more concentrated than an ordinary day.

## Realistic Ways to Cut the Heat Exposure

**Spreading the marathon over several days instead of one is the single biggest change most women can make.** Diwali mithai and snacks do not need to all be made in one exhausting day. Chaklis one evening, shakarpara another, mithai a third, spreads the stove time and heat exposure across the week instead of concentrating it into a single gruelling session that leaves you flushed, sweaty, and depleted by evening.

**Positioning matters more than people think.** Standing directly over a flame with a low or absent exhaust fan traps heat right at your face and neck. Running the exhaust fan on full, keeping a small fan running nearby (angled so it does not blow directly onto an open flame), and cooking with the kitchen door open where the layout allows it, all reduce the ambient heat you are absorbing while working.

**What you wear while cooking matters more than what you wear for the actual celebration.** Many women save their comfortable clothes for after guests arrive and cook in something heavier or more "presentable," which works against you here. Loose cotton, sleeves pushed up, hair tied back and off the neck, while you are actually at the stove, then changing into festive clothing closer to when people arrive, cuts down on trapped heat considerably.

## Delegating Without Guilt

**You do not have to hand over the whole kitchen to delegate meaningfully.** Assigning specific, contained tasks, someone else handles the shopping and prep chopping, another person manages the frying station while you handle the mixing and shaping, a younger family member takes over one entire dish start to finish, breaks the workload into pieces without requiring you to give up control of the parts that matter most to you.

**If you are the one who has always run the Diwali kitchen alone, saying "I need help with the frying this year" is not admitting defeat, it is a reasonable adjustment to a body that is genuinely working differently than it did five years ago.**

| Task | Heat exposure level | Good candidate to delegate or buy in |
|---|---|---|
| Deep frying (chaklis, shakarpara, mathri) | High, prolonged stove and oil heat | Yes, a good first task to hand off |
| Sugar syrup mithai (gulab jamun, laddoo) | High, sustained boiling heat | Consider buying from a trusted sweet shop this year |
| Mixing, shaping, assembling | Low, mostly off the stove | Good to keep for yourself if you enjoy the hands-on part |
| Dry snacks (roasted namkeen, mixtures) | Moderate, shorter stove bursts | Reasonable to keep or delegate depending on energy |
| Cleaning and setup | Low physical heat, but tiring | Good candidate for family members who "don't cook" but can still help |

## What Also Helps Beyond the Kitchen Itself

**Hydration through a hot cooking session matters more than it might seem.** Sipping water or a cool drink regularly while cooking, rather than only drinking once you finally sit down, helps your body regulate temperature better and can genuinely reduce flash frequency during a long stretch at the stove.

**A cool cloth or splash of water on your wrists and the back of your neck between tasks** is a quick, low-effort way to bring your temperature down mid-session without needing to fully stop cooking.

## In the Indian Context

**It is entirely reasonable to buy some mithai this year instead of making everything from scratch, and this does not diminish the festival or your role in it.** Sweet shops exist precisely because not every household makes every single item every year, even in generations past. If your body needs you to simplify this Diwali, a mix of homemade and bought items is a completely normal middle ground, not a compromise anyone outside your own head is likely to judge.

**If in-laws or extended family have specific expectations about who cooks what, a simple, matter-of-fact conversation ahead of the festival**, "I'm going to need some help in the kitchen this year, my body doesn't handle the heat the way it used to," tends to land better than trying to push through silently and then feeling resentful or unwell by the end of the day.

## When It's More Than Just Heat

**If flashes during cooking are frequent, severe, or leaving you feeling genuinely unwell, faint, or very fatigued, that is worth mentioning to your doctor rather than treating as an unavoidable part of festival season.** `/quiz` can help you get a clearer picture of whether your hot flash pattern fits typical perimenopause, and `/guide` covers the fuller range of options, from lifestyle adjustments to hormone therapy, for managing hot flashes long term, not just around Diwali.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it just heat, or could steam and oil vapour be a separate trigger too?**
Both contribute. Direct radiant heat from a flame or hot oil raises your immediate skin temperature quickly, while sustained steam exposure adds humidity that makes it harder for your body to cool itself through normal sweating, compounding the effect during a long cooking session.

**Will a kitchen AC or cooler actually make a noticeable difference?**
Yes, if your kitchen setup allows it. Even a modest drop in ambient kitchen temperature reduces how much additional heat your body has to manage on top of stove heat, which can meaningfully cut down on flash frequency during long sessions.

**I don't want to skip any of the traditional items this year. What's the lowest-effort change I can make?**
Spreading the cooking across more days rather than one marathon session is the change with the best effort-to-relief ratio; it keeps every traditional item on the table while cutting your single-day heat exposure significantly.

**Could this be a sign I need to see a doctor about my hot flashes generally, not just during Diwali?**
If Diwali cooking is simply the most extreme version of a hot flash pattern you deal with more mildly year-round, it is a good prompt to bring up hot flashes with your doctor generally, rather than treating festival cooking as an isolated, one-off problem.
