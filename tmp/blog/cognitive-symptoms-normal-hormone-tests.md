But your brain does not feel normal, and now you are left wondering if something else is wrong, or worse, if it is all in your head. It is neither. This is one of the most common and most confusing experiences in perimenopause, and there is a real physiological reason for it.

## Why Do Doctors Even Order the Test, Then?

Hormone tests are useful for specific situations, ruling out other conditions such as thyroid disorders, checking for early or premature menopause in women under 40, or investigating unusual bleeding patterns, but they were never designed to be a yes-or-no answer for "are you in perimenopause." **A normal result narrows down other explanations**, it does not erase your symptoms. Unfortunately, many doctors, often through no fault of their own but through limited training in menopause care, still treat a normal FSH or oestrogen level as a reason to dismiss perimenopause as the cause, when current guidance explicitly says the opposite.

## What Is Actually Happening to Your Hormones

**Perimenopause typically begins with erratic ovulation, not a steady decline.** In the years before periods stop altogether, oestrogen levels can swing between higher than your baseline and lower than your baseline, sometimes within the same cycle. A test drawn during a relative peak will look entirely normal, even while your body has been through several destabilising swings that week.

**The timing of the test within your cycle matters enormously.** Hormone levels that would look completely different on day 3 of a cycle versus day 21 mean the same body can produce two very different "normal" or "abnormal" readings depending purely on timing, something a single test often does not account for.

**Progesterone often drops earlier and more consistently than oestrogen.** Many cognitive and mood symptoms in early perimenopause are linked at least partly to falling progesterone, which is tested far less often than oestrogen or FSH, and can be low even when oestrogen numbers look unremarkable.

## What to Do When Your Tests Come Back Normal but You Still Feel Unwell

**Bring a detailed symptom history, not just a request for another test.** A written log of your cycle changes, sleep, mood, and cognitive symptoms over two to three months is far more diagnostically useful than a repeat blood test, and it is exactly what current guidelines say diagnosis should be based on.

**Ask directly whether a trial of treatment makes sense.** Many doctors are comfortable considering a treatment trial, whether hormonal or otherwise, based on a strong symptom pattern, even without an abnormal lab result, particularly if you are in your late thirties or forties.

**Ask what else is being ruled out.** Thyroid dysfunction, anaemia, low vitamin B12, and depression can all cause similar cognitive symptoms and are worth checking alongside, not instead of, considering perimenopause.

**Seek a second opinion if you feel dismissed rather than assessed.** A doctor saying "your tests are normal" as a full stop, without discussing your symptom pattern, is different from a doctor who reviews everything together and reaches a considered opinion.

## In the Indian Context

Many diagnostic labs in India will run an FSH or full hormone panel readily and relatively affordably (often ₹800 to ₹2,500 depending on the panel and city), and it is common for women to be handed a "normal" printout with little context on what that number does and does not mean at this life stage. Because family and even some general physicians can treat a normal report as reassurance that "nothing is wrong," women can end up feeling both unwell and unheard at the same time. Seeking out a gynaecologist with specific experience in perimenopause, rather than relying on the report alone, makes a real difference here; ask directly whether they diagnose perimenopause primarily from symptoms or primarily from labs, since the answer tells you a great deal about how that conversation will go.

| What the test shows | What it actually tells you |
|---|---|
| Normal FSH on one day | Rules out very little on its own; FSH fluctuates significantly during perimenopause |
| High FSH on one day | Suggestive but not definitive; a repeat test on another day can come back lower |
| Normal oestrogen | Reflects that single moment, not the pattern of swings over the month |
| Low progesterone | Often an earlier and more consistent marker, though tested less frequently |
| All hormones "normal" plus classic symptoms | Still consistent with perimenopause; guidelines prioritise symptom pattern over labs |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If my hormone tests are normal, could my brain fog be something else entirely?**
It is worth checking. Thyroid problems, iron or vitamin B12 deficiency, sleep disorders, and depression can all cause similar symptoms and are reasonable to rule out. But a normal hormone test does not, by itself, rule out perimenopause as a contributing cause.

**Should I ask for repeat hormone testing?**
It can be reasonable, especially timed to a specific cycle day if your doctor suggests it, but be prepared for the result to still be inconclusive given how much hormones fluctuate. A detailed symptom history usually tells your doctor more than a second snapshot test.

**Is it normal to feel dismissed when a test comes back normal?**
Unfortunately, yes, many women describe exactly this experience. It reflects a gap in how perimenopause is diagnosed versus how it is often practised, not a reflection on how real your symptoms are.

**Can brain fog improve even without changing my hormone test results?**
Yes. Treatment, whether hormonal or through addressing sleep, stress, and other contributing factors, is guided by whether your symptoms improve, not by whether a specific lab number moves. Many women see real cognitive improvement well before, or without, any change in a repeat test.
