It is one of the quietest, most common regrets women describe once they finally learn about hormone therapy: "I wish someone had told me this existed years ago." Not because they were refused it outright, but because it was simply never mentioned. They were handed a painkiller for headaches, a sleeping tablet for insomnia, an antidepressant for low mood, each symptom treated separately, while the actual hormonal cause behind all of them went unnamed and untreated for years.

If this sounds familiar, you are not imagining a gap in your care. It is real, common, and worth understanding.

## The Cost of Not Knowing

Years of poor sleep, low mood, hot flashes, and brain fog are not a neutral waiting period; they affect work performance, relationships, and long-term health, including bone density and cardiovascular risk, both of which oestrogen helps protect. Many women describe an entire decade shaped around managing symptoms piecemeal that hormone therapy could have addressed at the root, had anyone raised it as an option at the start.

This is not to say hormone therapy is right for everyone, or that every symptom will resolve with it. It is to say that a woman cannot make an informed choice about a treatment she was never told existed, and that gap in information is the actual problem, regardless of what she eventually decides.

## What Hormone Therapy Actually Is, in Plain Terms

Hormone therapy, often called HRT, replaces the oestrogen, and usually progesterone, that decline during perimenopause and menopause. It is available in several forms, including tablets, patches, gels, and sprays, and can be adjusted in dose and delivery method depending on your symptoms and history. It is generally considered safe and often first-line for most women under 60 or within ten years of their last period, though it is not automatically right for every woman, particularly those with a personal history of certain cancers or blood clots, which is exactly why an individual conversation with a doctor matters.

**In the Indian context**, awareness of hormone therapy as an option remains limited even among educated, urban women, partly because menopause and perimenopause are rarely discussed openly, whether at home, at work, or in general practice. Many Indian women only hear about it from a friend, a workplace conversation, or social media, rather than from their own doctor, which is precisely the gap this article is trying to close.

## How to Bring It Up Yourself

You do not need to wait for a doctor to raise hormone therapy. You can simply ask: "Given my symptoms, is hormone therapy something that could help me, and am I a reasonable candidate for it?" This single, direct question shifts the appointment from symptom management to a proper treatment discussion.

**Name the pattern.** Mention your symptoms together rather than one at a time: irregular periods, sleep disruption, mood changes, hot flashes. Naming the cluster increases the chance perimenopause gets raised as the underlying cause.

**Ask about your personal risk factors.** Rather than accepting a general yes or no, ask specifically how your family history, age, and health background affect your suitability for hormone therapy.

**Ask what it would involve practically.** What form would be recommended, how soon you might notice a difference, what monitoring is needed, and what it is likely to cost, are all reasonable, practical questions worth getting clear answers to.

## If a Doctor Seems Unfamiliar With It

Not every doctor keeps up equally with current menopause care, through no fault of their own; it is a small part of a broad training. If a doctor seems uncertain or hesitant when you raise hormone therapy, it is reasonable to ask for a referral to a gynaecologist with a specific interest in menopause, sometimes attached to a dedicated menopause clinic in larger Indian cities. Seeking this out is not going over anyone's head; it is simply finding the right specialist for a specific question.

## You Are Allowed to Ask Now, Even Years In

If you are reading this years into perimenopause, already managing symptoms in a dozen small, separate ways, it is not too late to ask about hormone therapy. Many women start it well into their transition, sometimes even close to or after their final period, and still find meaningful relief. The years already passed without this information are not wasted, but they do not need to continue that way either.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it too late to start HRT if I am already several years into perimenopause?**
No. Many women start hormone therapy well into perimenopause or even after reaching menopause, and it can still meaningfully help with symptoms like hot flashes, sleep disruption, and mood changes. Your doctor can advise on timing and suitability based on your individual history.

**Why didn't my doctor mention HRT to me?**
This can happen for several reasons: your symptoms may have been treated individually without perimenopause being named as the underlying cause, or the doctor may simply not have raised it during a short appointment. It does not necessarily mean you are unsuitable for it.

**How do I know if I am a good candidate for hormone therapy?**
Most women under 60 or within ten years of their last period are reasonable candidates, though a personal history of certain cancers, blood clots, or liver disease needs individual assessment. A direct conversation with your gynaecologist about your specific history is the only way to know for certain.

**Is hormone therapy expensive in India?**
Costs vary by formulation, but many women find monthly costs comparable to other routine prescriptions rather than prohibitively expensive. It is worth asking your doctor for exact pricing on the specific option being recommended for you.
