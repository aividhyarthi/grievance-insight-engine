If you have noticed that your knees complain more once the clouds roll in, that your mood dips along with the barometric pressure, or that the humidity makes everything from your skin to your sleep feel worse in June through September, you are not imagining a coincidence. Perimenopause already makes your body more sensitive to change, and the monsoon brings several changes at once: pressure shifts, humidity, reduced sunlight, and disrupted routines. Put together, they can make an already unpredictable season of life feel even less predictable.

## Why Do My Joints Hurt More When It Rains?

**The pressure-and-perimenopause combination is real, even if it sounds like an old wives' tale.** As barometric pressure drops before rainfall, the tissue around joints can expand slightly, which may press on already-sensitised nerve endings, especially if you are also dealing with the joint aches and stiffness that many women report starting in their 40s as oestrogen falls. This does not mean the rain is "causing" arthritis. It means an already more reactive joint system is responding to one more variable.

**What helps**: gentle, consistent movement rather than rest. Joints that are moved regularly, even a 20-minute walk between showers, or stretching indoors on a heavy-rain day, tend to stiffen less than joints left completely still. Warm (not hot) compresses, and keeping joints covered and dry rather than damp, both help too.

## Does Humidity Make Hot Flashes and Night Sweats Worse?

Yes, and this catches a lot of women off guard because they associate hot flashes with heat, and monsoon does not feel like the peak of summer. But it is humidity, not just temperature, that determines how efficiently your body can cool itself through sweat evaporation. In a humid room, sweat sits on the skin instead of evaporating, so a hot flash can feel more prolonged and more drenching during the monsoon than during a dry, hot month.

**What helps**: a dehumidifier or simply running the fan more than the air conditioner on humid nights (AC alone can leave a room cold but still damp), breathable cotton bedsheets rather than synthetic ones, and keeping a spare set of nightclothes within reach so a night sweat does not mean lying in damp clothes until morning.

## Does the Monsoon Affect Mood in Perimenopause?

**Reduced sunlight is a real factor, not a mood weakness.** Serotonin production is partly light-dependent, and perimenopause already involves a more volatile mood baseline because oestrogen influences serotonin regulation. Stack overcast weeks on top of that, and a low, flat, or irritable mood during monsoon is a genuine physiological pattern for many women, not a character issue.

**Confined routines add to this.** Monsoon often means fewer outdoor walks, more time indoors, cancelled plans, and children stuck at home, all of which reduce the small daily resets (fresh air, a walk, a solo errand) that many women rely on without realising it. If your mood consistently dips every monsoon and lifts once the season passes, that pattern itself is useful information to bring to a doctor, particularly if it is layered on other perimenopausal mood symptoms.

## In the Indian Context

Monsoon in most of India is not a light drizzle, it is weeks of sustained humidity, waterlogging, power cuts that knock out fans and AC, and a genuine disruption to daily routines, especially for women managing a household, commuting, or caring for children whose school and activity schedules get cancelled unpredictably. If you live in a coastal or high-humidity region (Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, Kerala), the humidity effect on hot flashes and sleep is often more pronounced than in drier northern cities during monsoon, where the relief from summer heat can partly offset it.

**Vitamin D deficiency is already extremely common among Indian women even outside monsoon**, given clothing norms, indoor lifestyles, and limited sun exposure in many cities. Monsoon compounds this further by cutting available sunlight even more. If fatigue and low mood are a recurring monsoon pattern for you, ask your doctor for a vitamin D test rather than assuming it is "just the weather" or "just perimenopause" without checking.

**Monsoon illnesses (dengue, viral fevers, waterborne infections) are also worth flagging honestly**: a body already managing hormonal fluctuation has less reserve to fight these off comfortably, and fevers or infections can temporarily worsen hot flashes, fatigue, and brain fog on top of the illness itself. Basic monsoon precautions (avoiding stagnant water exposure, drinking boiled or filtered water, prompt medical attention for persistent fever) matter more, not less, during this life stage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it actually true that joints hurt more before it rains, or is that a myth?**
There is genuine evidence that barometric pressure changes affect joint tissue and nerve sensitivity, though the effect size varies between people. In perimenopause, joints are already more reactive due to falling oestrogen, so many women notice the pattern more clearly during this life stage than they did in their 20s or 30s.

**Should I take vitamin D supplements every monsoon as a precaution?**
It is better to get a blood test first rather than supplementing blindly, since both deficiency and excess vitamin D cause problems, and your doctor can recommend an appropriate dose based on your actual levels. Many Indian women do turn out to be deficient, so it is a reasonable thing to check, especially if fatigue and mood dip noticeably every rainy season.

**Can monsoon humidity make perimenopausal skin issues worse?**
Yes, the combination of hormonal skin changes and high humidity can worsen oiliness, breakouts, or fungal skin issues in body folds, while paradoxically some women also experience dryness from AC use indoors. Lightweight, breathable fabrics and keeping skin thoroughly dry after rain exposure both help.

**Is it normal for my mood to lift as soon as monsoon ends?**
Yes, this is a common and recognisable pattern, and if it happens consistently, it is worth mentioning to a doctor as useful seasonal information rather than dismissing it, particularly if you are also managing other perimenopausal mood symptoms that could benefit from a broader treatment conversation.
