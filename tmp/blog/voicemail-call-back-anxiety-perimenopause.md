There's a little red notification sitting on your phone. A missed call, maybe even a voicemail, from someone ordinary: your sister, your child's school, a colleague. You've seen it. You know you need to deal with it. And yet, for reasons you can't fully explain, you haven't pressed play, and you haven't called back. It's been two days. Maybe longer. If this has become a quiet, slightly shameful pattern in the last year or two, you're describing something real, and it has a name: perimenopause-linked call-back anxiety, a specific cousin of the more general phone-call aversion many women in this stage describe.

## How this is different from just preferring to text

**Many women in perimenopause notice a general shift toward texting over calling**, simply because a text is easier to process at your own pace than a live conversation. That's a real and separate pattern (we've covered it in detail elsewhere on this site). Call-back anxiety specifically is narrower and often more distressing: it isn't a preference, it's an active avoidance that comes with guilt, procrastination, and sometimes real consequences, like a missed appointment reminder or a worried family member who's now called three more times wondering why you haven't responded.

**It often targets voicemails more than the calls themselves.** A missed call alone is relatively low-stakes; you can see who it was and decide when to deal with it. A voicemail, though, contains unknown content you have to actively choose to receive, which is why some women describe a visceral reluctance to even press play, sometimes letting messages pile up for a week or more until the guilt of ignoring them outweighs the dread of hearing them.

## What tends to help

| Strategy | Why it works |
|---|---|
| Text back first: "Saw your call, bit swamped, can I call you back at [time]?" | Converts an open-ended dread into a scheduled, bounded task |
| Check voicemails at a set time each day, rather than randomly through the day | Removes the repeated low-grade dread of "should I check now?" |
| Listen with a pen and paper ready | Reduces the fear of missing a detail you'll need to remember, a common underlying worry |
| Batch callbacks into one block of time | Avoids the start-stop dread of fitting in calls between other tasks |
| Tell close family or colleagues you're working on this | Reduces the chance of repeated anxious follow-up calls that make the pattern worse |
| Address underlying anxiety directly with a doctor if it's persistent | Treats the root cause rather than only managing the behaviour |

**In the Indian context**, phone culture here often assumes a call will be answered or returned quickly, especially within families, and an unreturned call can be read as rudeness, worry, or even a sign something is wrong with you, which adds social pressure on top of the anxiety itself. A short explanatory text, even something as simple as "Been having a rough patch with energy and focus lately, will call you properly this evening," tends to go a long way with people who care about you, and takes far less effort than the callback itself often feels like it will.

**This pattern can also intersect with joint-family dynamics**, where missed calls from in-laws or extended family can carry extra emotional weight. If a particular relationship is the main source of your call-dread (rather than calls in general), that's worth naming to yourself honestly, since it may be more about that specific relationship's dynamics than about perimenopause at all, even if perimenopause is lowering your capacity to manage it right now.

## When it's more than a phone habit

If avoiding calls and voicemails has started affecting your work (missed client calls, delayed responses that are costing you professionally), your close relationships (family members who feel actively hurt or alarmed by the pattern), or if the anxiety extends well beyond phones into general avoidance of responsibilities, it's worth talking to a doctor about anxiety more broadly rather than treating this as a standalone phone quirk. Generalised anxiety and perimenopause overlap heavily, and addressing the underlying anxiety, whether through lifestyle changes, therapy, or in some cases medication or hormone therapy, often eases this specific symptom along with several others. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is avoiding voicemails a recognised perimenopause symptom?**
It isn't formally listed as a named symptom in medical literature the way hot flashes are, but it fits a well-documented pattern of heightened anticipatory anxiety and reduced tolerance for unplanned cognitive demands during perimenopause, which many women describe in almost identical terms.

**Why does a voicemail feel worse than a text with the same information?**
A voicemail is a closed, unknown package you have to actively open, which can trigger more anticipatory dread than a text, which you can scan quickly and process at your own pace without committing to anything in the moment.

**Is this the same as the general phone-call aversion other articles mention?**
It's closely related but narrower. General phone aversion is often about the cognitive load of live conversation and a preference for texting. Call-back anxiety specifically centres on avoidance, guilt, and dread tied to unknown or pending messages.

**Will this get better once I'm through perimenopause?**
Many women find it eases as hormones stabilise post-menopause, but if it's significantly affecting your life now, it's worth addressing directly rather than simply waiting it out, since anxiety patterns can also become more entrenched habits over time.

**What's a simple first step if this resonates with me?**
Try texting back before calling: a short message acknowledging the missed call and proposing a specific time to talk removes the open-ended uncertainty that tends to fuel the avoidance in the first place.
