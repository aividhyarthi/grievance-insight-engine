## Why Does This Happen in Perimenopause?

**Oestrogen influences the olfactory system directly.** Oestrogen receptors are present in the olfactory bulb and related brain regions responsible for processing smell. As oestrogen fluctuates through perimenopause, rather than declining in a smooth, predictable line, smell sensitivity can shift along with it, sometimes becoming sharper, sometimes duller, often varying week to week.

**This mirrors what happens in early pregnancy for a reason.** Many women recognise this heightened smell sensitivity as eerily similar to how they felt in the first trimester of pregnancy, and that recognition is not a coincidence: both states involve significant oestrogen fluctuation, and heightened smell sensitivity linked to nausea is a documented feature of both.

**It often travels with nausea and headaches.** Because smell and taste are closely linked, and because certain smells can directly trigger nausea or a migraine-type headache in sensitive individuals, hyperosmia rarely shows up as an isolated, neutral experience. It tends to bring these secondary symptoms along with it, which is part of what makes it feel so disruptive rather than just mildly annoying.

**Stress and poor sleep can turn the volume up further.** Heightened baseline stress and cortisol, common in perimenopause, can lower the threshold at which a smell tips from noticeable into genuinely unbearable, which is part of why the same smell might be fine on a calm day and overwhelming on a stressful, exhausted one.

## What This Actually Looks Like

Women describe suddenly being unable to tolerate a partner's cologne or natural body odour after years of it being unremarkable, cooking smells lingering in the house causing nausea long after the food is ready, needing to leave a room when someone wears strong perfume, a heightened reaction to cigarette smoke, petrol, or cleaning products that did not bother them before, and occasionally, oddly, an increased sensitivity to their own cooking or even certain foods they previously enjoyed.

## What Helps

**Don't dismiss it as "just being fussy."** Naming it to yourself as a real, hormonally driven symptom, rather than an overreaction, reduces the frustration and self-doubt that often comes with a symptom nobody warned you about.

**Adjust your home environment where practical.** Better kitchen ventilation, switching to unscented or lightly scented cleaning and personal care products, and airing out rooms after cooking can meaningfully reduce your daily trigger exposure.

**Carry a simple portable remedy.** Some women find relief from a strong, pleasant counter-smell, a dab of peppermint oil, a citrus-scented tissue, or simply stepping into fresh air, when caught somewhere with a trigger smell they cannot leave immediately.

**Have honest, specific conversations with people close to you.** "I've become really sensitive to strong smells lately, it's a hormone thing, would you mind switching to an unscented version" is a request most partners and colleagues can accommodate without needing a full explanation.

**Track the pattern alongside your cycle if you still have one.** Some women notice hyperosmia intensifies at particular points in their cycle, which can help you anticipate and plan around especially sensitive days rather than being caught off guard.

**Mention it to your doctor if it's severe or paired with other symptoms.** While usually benign and hormonal, a sudden, dramatic change in smell perception occasionally warrants ruling out other causes, so it is worth a mention at your next appointment rather than assuming it must always be hormonal.

| Common trigger | Why it's often worse now | Practical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Cooking oil and spices | Heightened olfactory sensitivity plus nausea link | Exhaust fan, open windows, cook in shorter bursts |
| Perfume and deodorant | Strong, concentrated synthetic scent | Ask household to switch to unscented options |
| Cigarette smoke | Highly volatile, easily overwhelming odour | Avoid enclosed smoking areas; step outside if exposed |
| Cleaning products | Sharp, chemical smell in enclosed spaces | Ventilate during use; consider milder alternatives |
| Your partner's natural scent | Heightened sensitivity to familiar, close-range smells | Honest conversation; more distance during flare periods is reasonable |

## In the Indian Context

In many Indian households, the kitchen is a daily, hours-long commitment, and being unable to tolerate the very smells you are responsible for producing, tempering spices, frying, the lingering smell of a full day's cooking, can feel like a genuinely disorienting problem, on top of the confusion of not knowing why it is happening at all. It can also create real friction in joint families, where a daughter-in-law reacting strongly to cooking smells or a mother-in-law's perfume may be misread as fussiness or disrespect rather than understood as a hormonal symptom. Naming it plainly, even briefly, as a perimenopause-related sensitivity can help reframe the conversation at home. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is hyperosmia dangerous, or just uncomfortable?**
For most women, it is uncomfortable and disruptive rather than dangerous. It is worth mentioning to a doctor if it is severe, sudden, or paired with other unexplained symptoms, mainly to rule out other causes and to discuss management options, not because it typically signals something serious on its own.

**Will my sense of smell go back to normal after menopause?**
For many women, smell sensitivity settles back toward its previous baseline once hormone levels stabilise after menopause, though the timeline varies. Some women do notice a lasting, milder change in smell sensitivity even afterward.

**Could this actually be a symptom of something else, like a sinus problem?**
It is possible, and if you have other symptoms like congestion, facial pain, or a sudden loss rather than increase in smell, it is worth having that checked separately. Hyperosmia specifically, an increase in sensitivity rather than a loss, paired with your broader perimenopause symptoms, is more likely hormonal.

**Why does this feel so similar to how I felt during pregnancy?**
Because the underlying mechanism is genuinely similar: both early pregnancy and perimenopause involve significant fluctuation in oestrogen, and heightened smell sensitivity linked to nausea is a documented feature of both states, which is why the resemblance you're noticing is not a coincidence.
