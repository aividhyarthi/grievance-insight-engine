Ragi, bajra, dals, chana, til, almonds, walnuts, the earthy, brown-toned staples of a traditional Indian kitchen carry some of the most concentrated nutritional value on your plate: fibre, protein, and, in several cases, gentle plant oestrogens. This is the final piece in our short series on eating by colour through perimenopause, following our guides to green leafy foods and white and aromatic foods, and in many ways the most foundational group of the three.

## Why Brown and Whole Foods Matter Now

**Protein for muscle and metabolism.** As covered in our guides to weight and exercise in perimenopause, protein is essential for preserving muscle, which is lost more easily as oestrogen falls. Dals, chana, rajma, and other legumes are among the most accessible plant protein sources in an Indian kitchen.

**Fibre for blood sugar and gut health.** Whole grains like ragi and bajra, and legumes generally, are rich in fibre, which steadies blood sugar (relevant to the cravings and energy crashes covered in our sugar cravings guide) and supports the gut microbiome, tying into the gut-hormone connection covered in our estrobolome guide.

**Calcium from ragi.** Ragi (finger millet) is unusually rich in calcium for a grain, making it a genuinely valuable, if under-appreciated, addition to the bone-protective habits covered in our guide to preparing your bones for perimenopause.

**Phytoestrogens from til and soya.** Sesame seeds (til) and soya both contain lignans and isoflavones, plant compounds with a mild, gentle oestrogen-like effect in the body, covered in more detail in our guide to oestrogen-rich foods, a genuinely useful, food-based way to support hormonal balance.

**Magnesium and B vitamins from nuts and seeds.** Almonds, walnuts, and flaxseed offer magnesium, supporting the sleep and muscle-relaxation benefits covered in our insomnia and restless legs guides, alongside healthy fats that support mood and joint comfort.

## The Staples Worth Prioritising

**Ragi (finger millet)** — as ragi dosa, roti, or porridge (ragi malt), one of the most calcium-rich grains available and a genuinely valuable daily habit.

**Bajra and jowar (millets)** — nutrient-dense alternatives to refined grains, rich in fibre and minerals, traditionally eaten as roti across much of India.

**Dals of all kinds** — moong, masoor, toor, chana, each bringing protein and fibre; varying your dals through the week offers a broader nutrient range than relying on just one.

**Rajma and other legumes** — protein-rich and versatile, a simple, filling way to build a meal around plant protein.

**Til (sesame seeds)** — a small daily addition, in laddoos, as a garnish, or ground into chutney, offers calcium and phytoestrogens in a genuinely traditional, easy form.

**Soya** — as chunks, tofu, or milk, one of the most studied phytoestrogen sources, worth including regularly rather than occasionally for a meaningful effect.

**Almonds, walnuts, and flaxseed (alsi)** — a small daily handful of nuts, and a spoon of ground flaxseed stirred into curd or a smoothie, are easy, low-effort habits with real nutritional value.

## How to Eat More Without It Feeling Like a Project

**Make dal a daily anchor, not an occasional side.** A bowl of dal at most meals is both a traditional habit and a genuinely strong nutritional choice, easy to build the rest of a meal around.

**Swap in millets gradually.** Rather than an abrupt switch, alternating bajra or jowar roti with wheat roti through the week eases the transition and broadens your nutrient intake.

**Start the day with ground flaxseed.** A tablespoon stirred into your morning curd or porridge is one of the simplest, most consistent phytoestrogen habits available.

**Keep a jar of roasted til or a few laddoos on hand.** A small, ready snack makes it easy to include sesame regularly rather than only when specifically cooking with it.

**Soak nuts overnight.** A small handful of soaked almonds or walnuts as a morning or evening snack is an easy, traditional habit that supports both nutrition and satiety.

## A Word on Choosing Whole, Less Processed Grains

Choosing whole grains over heavily refined ones (whole wheat over refined maida, brown or unpolished rice over heavily polished white rice where possible) is one of the more meaningful, evidence-backed food choices available, more impactful for most women than chasing specific organic certifications. Where organic whole grains and legumes are accessible and affordable, they are a reasonable choice, but the core habit that matters most is simply choosing the less processed, more traditional version of these staples, much as earlier generations generally ate.

## When to See a Doctor

**Routine appointment** to discuss your diet as part of your broader perimenopause care, particularly around protein intake, bone health, and how these habits fit alongside your individual health picture.

**Also worth checking** your iron and vitamin D levels, since legumes and whole grains support but do not fully replace addressing an existing deficiency, which is best identified with a simple blood test and treated directly if needed.

Ragi, dals, til, and nuts are some of the most quietly powerful foods already present in a traditional Indian kitchen. Building daily habits around this group, protein, fibre, calcium, and gentle phytoestrogens together, is one of the most genuinely useful, food-based things you can do to support your body through perimenopause.
