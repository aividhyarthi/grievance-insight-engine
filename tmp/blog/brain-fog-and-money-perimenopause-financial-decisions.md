A late fee on a credit card you always pay on time. A recurring subscription you meant to cancel two months ago and simply forgot. A larger, slightly impulsive purchase you cannot fully explain, made on a night you were exhausted and not thinking clearly. Individually, these look like ordinary lapses anyone might have. Strung together during perimenopause, alongside word-finding trouble and general forgetfulness, they can point to something more specific: brain fog quietly affecting your financial decisions, a consequence almost nobody warns women about.

## Why Would Brain Fog Affect Something Like Money?

**Financial decisions rely heavily on working memory.** Comparing two loan offers, remembering which bills are due when, holding a budget in your head while shopping, these all depend on the same working memory system that oestrogen fluctuations are known to affect during perimenopause, which is the same system responsible for the word-finding trouble and "why did I walk into this room" moments many women describe.

**Executive function governs impulse control, and it can weaken too.** The part of the brain responsible for pausing before a decision, weighing consequences, and resisting an immediate impulse is affected by the same hormonal shifts behind mood swings and reduced frustration tolerance. On a bad brain fog day, resisting an unplanned purchase can genuinely take more effort than it used to.

**Fatigue compounds the effect.** Financial decisions made late at night, after a poor night's sleep, or during a stressful week are more prone to error for anyone, and perimenopause frequently brings all three at once: disrupted sleep, elevated stress, and cognitive fog layered on top of each other.

## What This Can Actually Look Like

**Forgetting a bill you have paid reliably for years**, not because you do not have the money or do not care, but because the mental note simply did not stick the way it used to. This is one of the more common and confusing versions of financial brain fog, precisely because it breaks a pattern that felt automatic before.

**Losing track of small recurring subscriptions**, a streaming service, an app, a gym membership you meant to cancel, that quietly continue charging your card because remembering to actively cancel them requires a working memory step that brain fog interferes with.

**Making a larger purchase impulsively, then feeling confused by your own decision afterward.** This is not about irresponsibility with money in general; it is specifically about reduced impulse control in a particular hormonal window, often on a day when you were already exhausted or emotionally overwhelmed.

**Struggling to compare complex financial options**, insurance plans, investment choices, loan terms, that require holding several numbers in mind at once and comparing them, a task that genuinely becomes harder during a foggy stretch, even for women who previously handled this kind of comparison easily.

## How to Protect Your Finances While This Is Happening

**Automate everything that can be automated.** Set fixed bills, EMIs, insurance premiums, and utility payments to autopay wherever your bank or provider allows it, so a foggy week cannot cause a missed payment or a late fee, regardless of how your memory is functioning that day.

**Build in a single, fixed weekly check-in rather than relying on remembering spontaneously.** Pick one day and time, a Sunday evening, for instance, to glance over your accounts and upcoming payments. A scheduled habit is far more reliable during brain fog than an intention to "check when I think of it."

**Use a written or shared list for recurring subscriptions.** A simple note, checked every few months, catches the small charges that individually feel too minor to notice but add up meaningfully over a year.

**Give yourself a waiting period for non-essential purchases.** A 24 to 48 hour rule before any unplanned, non-essential spend gives your impulse control a chance to catch up, without requiring you to trust your in-the-moment judgement on a foggy day.

**Loop in a trusted person for major decisions, not daily ones.** For significant financial commitments, a large investment, a property decision, a loan, it is reasonable to ask a spouse, sibling, or financial advisor to review it with you during this period, not because you cannot manage your own money, but because a second set of eyes is a sensible safeguard during a known cognitive symptom, the same way you might double-check important emails.

## In the Indian Context

Many Indian women, particularly those managing household finances, joint family expenses, or supporting ageing parents financially, are already handling a heavier-than-average cognitive load around money, which makes brain fog's effect on financial decisions land harder. If household finances are managed jointly with a husband or family member, it can help to say plainly, "I'm having more trouble than usual keeping track of the small recurring payments right now, can we set up autopay for the fixed ones," rather than trying to quietly manage a growing sense of overwhelm alone. This is a practical request, not an admission of failure, and most partners respond better to a specific, solvable ask than to a vague sense that something feels harder than it used to.

| Financial task | Brain fog risk | Simple safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed monthly bills (rent, EMI, utilities) | Missed payments, late fees | Autopay wherever available |
| Recurring subscriptions | Forgotten cancellations, small ongoing charges | A written list, reviewed every few months |
| Unplanned purchases | Impulse buying on low-energy days | A 24 to 48 hour waiting rule |
| Complex comparisons (loans, insurance, investments) | Difficulty holding multiple numbers in mind | A second person to review with you, or spreading the decision over two sessions |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is forgetting to pay a bill really connected to perimenopause, or am I just being careless?**
For many women, it is a genuine cognitive symptom connected to the same working memory changes behind other brain fog symptoms, not a character flaw. If it is a new pattern that did not happen before, that timing itself is worth noticing.

**Should I mention this to my doctor along with my other symptoms?**
Yes. Financial slips are a less commonly discussed but real facet of perimenopause brain fog, and mentioning them alongside memory or concentration concerns gives your doctor a fuller picture of how significantly cognitive symptoms are affecting your daily life.

**Will this improve if I start hormone therapy for other symptoms?**
Many women notice improved concentration and memory on hormone therapy, which can indirectly help with this, though it is worth continuing practical safeguards like autopay regardless, since brain fog can fluctuate even with treatment.

**Is impulsive spending during brain fog a sign of a bigger problem, like a spending disorder?**
Usually not, if it is a new pattern specifically tied to this transition and responds to simple safeguards like a waiting period. If spending feels genuinely out of control regardless of these measures, that is worth discussing with a doctor or counsellor directly.
