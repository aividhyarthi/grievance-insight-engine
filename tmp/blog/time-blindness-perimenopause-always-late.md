## What exactly is "time blindness," and is it really a perimenopause thing?

Time blindness describes a genuine difficulty perceiving the passage of time accurately, both in the moment (losing track of how long you've been doing something) and in planning ahead (underestimating how long a task or journey will take). **It's a term that's been used for years in ADHD circles**, where it's a well-recognised symptom, and there's a growing, overlapping body of anecdotal and early clinical observation that perimenopause intensifies the same underlying cognitive mechanisms, attention regulation and working memory, that make time blindness happen in the first place.

This is part of why women who already live with ADHD often describe perimenopause as a dramatic worsening of symptoms they'd otherwise learned to manage, time blindness very much included. But it also shows up newly in women with no prior ADHD history, simply as one more flavour of the broader brain fog that oestrogen fluctuation brings.

## Why does it feel so specifically humiliating, compared to other brain fog symptoms?

**Lateness reads as a character judgement in a way that forgetting a word doesn't.** Society tends to interpret chronic lateness as disrespect, poor planning, or simply not caring enough, rather than what it often actually is: a genuine, in-the-moment failure of the brain's internal clock. This makes it one of the more emotionally loaded perimenopause symptoms to deal with, particularly for women who've spent decades building a reputation for reliability, now watching that reputation feel at risk over something that doesn't feel within their control the way it used to be.

**In Indian family and work culture specifically**, punctuality carries its own loaded meaning, missing the start of a puja, arriving late to collect children from school, or walking into a meeting after it's started can trigger disproportionate guilt or anxiety on top of the practical inconvenience, compounding an already frustrating symptom with a layer of shame that makes it harder to simply problem-solve.

## What actually helps, since willpower clearly isn't working?

**The core shift that helps most is moving time management outside your own head**, since the problem isn't effort or care, it's the internal sense of time itself becoming unreliable. External, non-negotiable cues consistently outperform internal intention.

- **Visible, multiple clocks or a watch**, rather than relying on glancing at a phone you may not check for twenty minutes at a stretch.
- **Alarms set for transitions, not just final deadlines**: one to start getting ready, a second as a hard leave-by cue, rather than a single alarm for the event itself.
- **Deliberately overestimating task duration** by 25-50% when planning your day, since perimenopausal time-blindness tends to consistently underestimate rather than overestimate.
- **Buffer time built into the calendar itself** between appointments, rather than scheduling back to back and assuming you'll transition instantly.
- **Saying the actual time out loud** when leaving the house, "it's 9:40, I need to leave by 9:55," turns an internal, unreliable sense into an external, spoken commitment.

| Strategy | Why it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple visible clocks/timers | Removes reliance on internal time sense | General daily transitions |
| "Leave-by" alarms, not just event alarms | Creates a hard external trigger before you're already late | Appointments, school pickups, meetings |
| Deliberate overestimation of task time | Corrects for the brain's tendency to underestimate during brain fog | Planning the day, cooking, getting ready |
| Calendar buffer time | Prevents one delay from cascading into the next commitment | Back-to-back work or family obligations |
| Saying the time out loud | Converts an unreliable internal cue into an explicit, spoken one | Leaving the house, ending a task |

## When does this need more than a time-management fix?

If lateness, missed appointments, or losing track of time is affecting your job performance, important family obligations, or your own sense of self significantly, **it's worth bringing up with your doctor alongside your other perimenopause symptoms**, not as a separate, unrelated complaint. It can be a useful, specific detail to mention: not just "brain fog" in general, but "I keep losing track of time and arriving late even when I plan carefully," since that specificity helps a doctor understand the pattern and consider it alongside options like hormone therapy, which some women find does meaningfully improve attention and working memory symptoms, in addition to addressing hot flashes and other better-known effects.

If you already have an ADHD diagnosis, it's particularly worth flagging that perimenopause may be intensifying existing symptoms, since this is an increasingly recognised overlap, and your doctor may want to reassess your management plan with that in mind rather than assuming nothing has changed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is time blindness a sign of early dementia?**
For most perimenopausal women, no. Time blindness tied to perimenopause tends to fluctuate alongside other classic brain fog symptoms and hormonal patterns, rather than progressively worsening in the way dementia-related cognitive decline typically does. If you're genuinely worried, or if the pattern feels different from typical brain fog, it's reasonable to raise it with your doctor for peace of mind.

**Why do I lose track of time more when I'm absorbed in something, like scrolling or a task I enjoy?**
This is actually a very typical pattern. Absorption in a task reduces the background attention needed to track passing time, which is already weaker during perimenopausal brain fog, so time slips by especially fast during focused or enjoyable activities.

**Does better sleep actually improve this symptom?**
For many women, yes, meaningfully. Poor sleep independently worsens attention and time estimation on top of the hormonal effect, so improving sleep quality (addressing night sweats, sleep hygiene, or underlying insomnia) often brings some improvement in time perception alongside other cognitive symptoms.

**Will this get better once I'm through menopause?**
Many women report that brain fog symptoms, including time blindness, improve somewhat once hormone levels stabilise post-menopause, though individual experiences vary considerably. In the meantime, building external systems (alarms, buffers, visible clocks) tends to help regardless of where you are in the transition.

**Should I mention this specific symptom to my doctor, or is it too minor?**
It's absolutely worth mentioning. Doctors increasingly recognise brain fog's many specific forms, word-finding, forgetfulness, time perception, and the more precisely you describe what's actually happening, the better they can tailor advice or treatment options to your actual experience rather than a generic symptom list.
