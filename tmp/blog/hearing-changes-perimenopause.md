Alongside the ringing sensation of tinnitus, some women notice a distinct and separate change during perimenopause: their actual hearing feels different. This might mean struggling to follow conversations in a noisy restaurant, needing the television slightly louder than before, or finding certain sounds oddly sharp or uncomfortable. This is a different experience from tinnitus, this is about how clearly and comfortably you hear, and there is a genuine hormonal link worth understanding.

## Why Hearing Can Change in Perimenopause

**Oestrogen supports the auditory system.** Oestrogen receptors are present in the inner ear and in the parts of the brain involved in processing sound. Oestrogen supports blood flow to the delicate structures of the inner ear and plays a role in maintaining healthy nerve function along the auditory pathway. As it fluctuates and falls in perimenopause, this support becomes less consistent, and some women notice subtle changes in hearing clarity as a result.

**Some age-related hearing change is simply expected.** As with vision, it is worth being honest that some hearing change in your 40s and 50s reflects the ordinary, gradual process of ageing in the ear, called presbycusis, which affects the ability to hear higher-pitched sounds and follow speech in noisy environments, and happens to some degree regardless of hormones. Perimenopause may add its own layer on top of this expected change, rather than being the sole cause.

**Fluid balance may play a role.** Hormonal fluctuations can affect fluid balance in the body, including subtly within the inner ear, which is a fluid-filled structure. Some women notice their hearing, or the sensation of ear fullness, seems to shift somewhat with their hormonal cycle during perimenopause.

## What This Looks Like

Women describe difficulty following conversations in noisy environments, like restaurants or gatherings, needing to ask people to repeat themselves more than before, needing higher volume on the television or phone, sounds occasionally feeling unusually sharp, loud, or uncomfortable (a heightened sensitivity rather than reduced hearing), and a sensation of fullness or pressure in the ears, sometimes fluctuating.

## What Helps

**Get a proper hearing assessment.** This is the essential first step, both to establish a clear baseline and to distinguish ordinary age-related change, hormonal fluctuation, and any other contributing factor, rather than guessing. A hearing test is simple, painless, and gives you real information to work with.

**Manage your listening environment where you can.** In noisy settings, positioning yourself to see the speaker's face, reducing background noise where possible (turning down music, choosing quieter venues), and asking others to speak slightly more clearly rather than louder, can all help you follow conversations more comfortably.

**Address ear health generally.** Avoid inserting anything into the ear canal, protect your ears from very loud noise exposure, and manage any nasal congestion or allergies (as covered in our guide to perimenopausal allergy flare-ups), since a blocked nose can contribute to a feeling of ear fullness or muffled hearing.

**Do not ignore gradual changes.** It is easy to adapt slowly to gradually declining hearing without quite noticing how much you have adjusted, turning the television up bit by bit, for instance. If in doubt, a hearing test settles the question clearly.

**Consider the broader hormonal picture.** As with several other perimenopausal symptoms, some women find hearing-related symptoms are part of a wider picture that eases somewhat with treatments that steady hormones. This is not typically a primary reason to consider HRT on its own, but worth mentioning to your gynaecologist if you notice a pattern.

**Hearing aids and support, if needed.** If a hearing test does identify meaningful hearing loss, modern hearing aids are far more discreet and effective than in the past, and addressing hearing loss properly is linked to better quality of life, mood, and even long-term brain health, well worth pursuing rather than simply adapting around it.

## When to See a Doctor

**Routine appointment** for a hearing assessment (with an audiologist or ENT specialist) if you have noticed changes in your hearing, particularly difficulty following conversations, needing higher volume, or a persistent feeling of fullness, so the cause can be properly identified and appropriate support offered if needed.

**Promptly** for any **sudden hearing loss, especially in one ear**, as this needs urgent assessment and is not typical of the gradual changes described here. Also see a doctor promptly for hearing changes that come with **significant dizziness or vertigo, ear pain, or discharge from the ear**, as these need their own evaluation.

Hearing changes in perimenopause are real, though often a blend of hormonal influence and ordinary ageing rather than one single cause. A proper hearing assessment gives you clarity, and there is a great deal of support available, whatever it reveals.
