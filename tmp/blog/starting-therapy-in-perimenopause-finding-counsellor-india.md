A close friend finally said it out loud: "Have you thought about talking to someone?" You laughed it off, because therapy is for "real problems," not for a woman who is just tired, irritable, and crying more easily than usual. Except that description, tired, irritable, crying more easily, is also a near-perfect summary of what a lot of women experience in perimenopause, and it is exactly the kind of thing therapy was built to help with, alongside, not instead of, whatever your gynaecologist is also doing for you.

## Is Therapy Enough on Its Own, or Do I Still Need a Doctor?

**Both can be true at once, and that is not a contradiction.** If your mood symptoms are substantially driven by hormonal fluctuation, therapy alone may help you cope and build skills, but it will not change the underlying hormonal pattern the way hormone therapy or other medical treatment can. Conversely, medical treatment alone will not necessarily give you tools for the relationship strain, identity questions, or long-standing thought patterns that therapy is specifically designed to work through.

**A reasonable way to think about it**: if your mood symptoms feel tightly linked to your cycle, come with vasomotor symptoms like hot flashes, or responded clearly to a change you have already tried, that points toward raising it with your gynaecologist first or alongside starting therapy. If the symptoms feel more like a broader shift in how you relate to yourself, your marriage, your children, or your sense of who you are, therapy may be doing work that no amount of hormone adjustment alone will reach. Many women benefit from both conversations happening around the same time, and a good therapist or doctor should be comfortable with that, not territorial about it.

## Who Actually Does What: Knowing the Difference

**This confusion stops a lot of women from starting**, so it is worth being specific.

**A counsellor or psychologist** typically offers talk-based therapy: structured conversation, coping strategies, and support, without prescribing medication. In India, look for someone with a recognised postgraduate qualification in psychology or counselling and, ideally, registration with the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) for clinical psychologists, which is a meaningful quality marker.

**A psychiatrist** is a medical doctor who can diagnose mental health conditions and prescribe medication when appropriate, including antidepressants that are sometimes used for perimenopausal mood symptoms, often alongside or instead of hormone therapy depending on your situation. A psychiatrist can also refer you onward for talk therapy with a psychologist or counsellor.

**Your gynaecologist** remains central to this picture too, since they are the one assessing whether your symptoms warrant hormone therapy, and ideally coordinating with whichever mental health professional you also see, rather than any one professional working in isolation from the others.

## How to Actually Find a Qualified Counsellor in India

**Start with a basic credential check.** Ask or look up whether a psychologist is RCI-registered, or whether a counsellor has a recognised postgraduate qualification from an accredited institution. This single step filters out a meaningful amount of the unregulated, self-styled "life coach" or "healer" territory that has grown alongside genuine mental health services.

**Teletherapy platforms** have made access considerably easier over the last several years, letting you consult a qualified professional by video call without needing one physically located in your city, which matters especially outside major metros where in-person specialist options are thinner.

**Hospital-based psychiatry and psychology departments**, particularly at larger government and private hospitals, often have structured mental health services, sometimes at a more accessible cost than private solo practice, and with the advantage of easy coordination if you also need a psychiatric medication review alongside your gynaecological care.

**Workplace employee assistance programmes (EAPs)** are increasingly offered by larger Indian employers and often include a set number of free or subsidised counselling sessions, confidentially, without your manager being informed that you used them. It is worth checking your company's HR policies quietly if this applies to you.

**Cost realistically ranges widely**: private sessions in major cities often fall somewhere between ₹1,000 and ₹3,000 or more per session, while hospital-based services, teletherapy platforms with sliding-scale options, and some NGO-run mental health helplines offer considerably lower-cost or free access.

## Addressing the Stigma, Honestly

**"What will people say" is a real pressure**, particularly in joint-family settings where therapy can be misread as either an admission of serious instability or an unnecessary, Western indulgence rather than ordinary healthcare. It can help to frame it to family, if you choose to mention it at all, in the same practical terms you would use for seeing a physiotherapist or a dietitian: a professional, confidential service for a specific concern, not a dramatic life event.

**You do not owe anyone a detailed explanation** for seeking therapy, and plenty of women navigate it privately without announcing it to their extended family at all. What matters is that you get support that actually helps, not how visibly you do it.

## When This Becomes Urgent, Not Just Important

**If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, or feel unsafe in any way**, this is not something to wait on until your next scheduled session. In an emergency, call 112. For mental health support specifically, iCall's helpline is available at 9152987821, Monday to Saturday, 8am to 10pm. Reaching out at that moment matters more than finding the "perfect" long-term therapist first.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is therapy only for serious mental illness?**
No. Therapy is widely used for everyday stress, mood changes, relationship strain, and life transitions, including perimenopause, without requiring a formal diagnosis to justify going.

**Will my therapist tell me to stop or start hormone therapy?**
A psychologist or counsellor does not prescribe or advise on hormone therapy directly; that conversation belongs with your gynaecologist or a psychiatrist for medication. A good therapist will support that medical process rather than compete with it.

**How many sessions will I need?**
This varies enormously by person and concern, from a handful of sessions focused on a specific issue to a longer-term ongoing relationship. Most therapists will discuss an initial plan with you after the first one or two sessions, and you can always reassess as you go.

**What if the first therapist I try doesn't feel right?**
That is common and completely acceptable to act on. Fit matters in therapy, and switching to someone else is a normal part of finding the right working relationship, not a failure on your part.

**How do I bring this up with my doctor if I want both hormonal and mental health support?**
You can simply say you would like your gynaecologist's input on whether your symptoms look hormonally driven, and separately ask whether they can recommend a psychologist or psychiatrist they coordinate with.
