You are genuinely happy for her. You have known this friend for fifteen years, you cried a little when she called with the news, and you would not miss this wedding for anything. And yet, sitting at the sangeet watching her dance with her sisters, something unexpected rises in you, a tightness in your throat that has nothing to do with the onions from the buffet, a flash of irritation at a relative's comment, a strange distance from a friend group you have always felt completely at home in. If a wedding that should feel purely joyful has started stirring up feelings you cannot fully name or explain, perimenopause is very likely part of the story, and it has very little to do with how much you love the bride.

## Is It Normal to Feel Sad or Tearful at a Happy Event Like This?

**Yes, and it's one of the more confusing perimenopause patterns because it seems to contradict how you actually feel about the occasion.** You can be completely, sincerely happy for your friend and still find tears arriving during a particular song, a father-daughter moment, or a toast, simply because your emotional threshold is lower right now and any strong feeling, positive or otherwise, has an easier path to the surface. This does not mean you are secretly unhappy or envious, it means your nervous system is processing big emotional moments with less of a buffer than it used to have.

## Why Do I Feel Irritable or Impatient Around Family at Weddings Now?

**Weddings concentrate exactly the kind of social friction perimenopause makes harder to absorb: personal questions, unsolicited comments, long waits, and constant social performance.** A relative asking why you look tired, a comment about your weight or your outfit, an aunt's opinion on your own marriage delivered uninvited, all of this used to roll off more easily and now lands with more weight. This is a tolerance change, not a character change, and it is one of the most common things women report finding harder at big family functions during this stage.

## Is It Okay to Feel a Little Envious or Wistful, Even Though I'm Happily Settled?

**Yes, and it does not cancel out your happiness for your friend.** Watching someone else's wedding, especially the excitement and attention around it, can genuinely stir up nostalgia for your own wedding day, or reflection on how your marriage and life have evolved since. These feelings are common and complicated, and they are allowed to exist alongside real joy for her, not instead of it. Complicated feelings at milestone events are a normal human response, not something specific to perimenopause, but perimenopause does tend to bring them closer to the surface than before.

## What Actually Helps Through the Functions?

**Pace yourself across a multi-day wedding rather than attending everything at full intensity.** Skipping one function, arriving late to another, or leaving before the very end are all reasonable choices that protect your energy for the parts that matter most to you, like the actual ceremony or time with the bride herself. **Have a low-key exit plan for overwhelming moments**, a quiet corner, a short walk outside, or a car waiting if you need to leave early, so you are not stuck riding out a wave of tears or irritation in the middle of a crowd. **Give yourself permission to feel whatever comes up without needing to explain it to anyone else in the moment.** Not every emotional wave needs an audience or a justification, sometimes stepping away to have a private cry and coming back composed is the whole solution.

## In the Indian Context

**Indian weddings are rarely a single event, they are three or four days of functions, each with its own dress, its own standing-and-socialising demands, and its own emotional register**, from the playful sangeet to the solemn pheras. Treating each function as its own commitment, rather than one continuous obligation, makes it easier to protect your energy for the ones you genuinely want to be present for. **Close friend groups from school or college carry decades of shared history**, and it's common at these reunions for old dynamics, old jokes, and old comparisons to resurface all at once, which can feel intense even in a good way. If a wedding coincides with a hard personal stretch, whether hormonal, financial, or relational, it is completely reasonable to attend selectively rather than push through the entire event out of obligation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to want to leave a wedding early even though I love the couple?**
Yes. Wanting to leave early reflects your current energy capacity, not your feelings about the couple or the event. Most hosts and friends understand a quieter exit far more than we assume they will.

**Why do I feel more emotional at other people's weddings than I did at my own age when I attended weddings in my twenties?**
Emotional reactivity generally increases during perimenopause due to fluctuating oestrogen, so events that once felt straightforwardly fun can now stir up more layered feelings, nostalgia, reflection, even grief for time passed, alongside genuine happiness.

**Could this be about something deeper than the wedding itself, like my own marriage or life choices?**
It can be, and that's worth sitting with gently rather than dismissing. If a wedding brings up real dissatisfaction with your own relationship or life direction that persists well beyond the event, that's worth exploring further, on its own terms, separate from the passing emotional intensity of the day itself.

**Should I mention this to my doctor if it feels like more than just a wedding being emotional?**
If big emotional swings are frequent, disproportionate, or distressing across many situations, not just weddings, it's worth discussing with your doctor as part of your broader perimenopause symptom picture, since mood regulation is a legitimate and treatable part of that picture.
