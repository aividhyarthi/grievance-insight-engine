Every Indian winter, sesame seeds show up everywhere: in til gud ladoos, in chikki, sprinkled over jaggery at Makar Sankranti and Pongal, pressed into the gingelly oil used across South Indian kitchens. It is such a familiar, old-fashioned ingredient that it rarely gets talked about the way flaxseed or fenugreek do in modern nutrition conversations. That is a bit of a missed opportunity, because sesame, or til, is genuinely one of the richest plant sources of calcium most Indian women already have in their kitchens, at a stage of life when bone health starts to matter more than it used to.

## What Makes Til Nutritionally Useful in Perimenopause?

**Sesame seeds are unusually calcium-dense for a plant food, with the unhulled, darker variety containing considerably more calcium per gram than hulled white sesame or most other seeds.** Alongside calcium, til provides magnesium, zinc, healthy monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats, and a reasonable amount of plant protein for such a small seed. This combination matters specifically in perimenopause, when declining oestrogen accelerates bone density loss, and when magnesium deficiency is already common enough among Indian women to be worth actively correcting through diet.

**Til also contains lignans, specifically sesamin, a plant compound with mild phytoestrogenic activity**, similar in concept to the lignans found in flaxseed, though less extensively studied specifically for menopausal symptoms. The theory is that these compounds can weakly interact with oestrogen receptors in the body, potentially offering a gentle buffering effect as the body's own oestrogen declines, though this remains a modest, partial mechanism rather than a proven treatment pathway.

## Does the Evidence Support Specific Benefits?

**For bone health, the calcium and magnesium case is solid and well established in general nutrition science, even without perimenopause-specific trials.** Getting enough dietary calcium and magnesium through whole foods is a well-supported strategy for supporting bone density at any life stage, and sesame seeds are a genuinely efficient way to contribute to that intake, especially for women who eat a largely plant-based or vegetarian diet and may have fewer dietary calcium sources than someone who regularly eats dairy in large amounts.

**For hot flashes, mood, or other vasomotor symptoms specifically, the evidence is thinner.** A small number of studies, often done with sesame seed supplementation rather than typical dietary amounts, have reported modest improvements in some menopausal markers, but these studies tend to be small, short, and not replicated at the scale needed to make a confident clinical claim. It is reasonable to eat sesame seeds as part of a generally supportive diet; it is not reasonable to expect them to meaningfully reduce hot flashes on their own.

## How Much, and in What Form?

**A tablespoon or two of sesame seeds a day, whether as til chikki in moderation, sprinkled over vegetables and salads, stirred into chutneys, or used as gingelly (sesame) oil for cooking, is a realistic, sustainable amount to incorporate without major dietary disruption.** Lightly roasting and crushing or grinding the seeds, rather than eating them whole, can improve how much of the calcium and other nutrients your body actually absorbs, since whole seeds sometimes pass through digestion largely intact.

**It is worth being mindful of the calorie density, particularly with sweetened preparations like til gud ladoos, which combine seeds with jaggery and often ghee.** These are a legitimate, traditional, and reasonably healthy seasonal treat in moderate amounts, but they are not the same as eating plain sesame seeds for their nutritional content, and relying on sweets as your main source risks adding more sugar than the calcium benefit justifies.

## In the Indian Context

**Sesame seeds and gingelly oil are inexpensive and already part of most Indian kitchens, typically costing well under ₹100 for enough seeds to last several weeks**, which makes this one of the more accessible nutrition changes available compared to supplements or specialty foods. Many households already eat til seasonally around Makar Sankranti and Pongal; the opportunity is simply eating it more consistently through the year rather than only in winter.

**If a bone density scan (DEXA) or blood test has flagged low calcium or vitamin D, food sources like til are a helpful addition but are unlikely to be sufficient on their own to correct a significant deficiency**, and your doctor may still recommend supplementation. Dietary changes and supplements are not an either-or choice; they work best discussed together with your doctor, especially once you are past 40 and bone density becomes a more active consideration.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can eating sesame seeds replace a calcium supplement?**
For most women, dietary sesame seeds are a helpful contributor to calcium intake but not a guaranteed replacement for supplementation if a doctor has identified a specific deficiency. Food and supplements can work together rather than as a strict either-or.

**Is black (unhulled) sesame better than white (hulled) sesame?**
For calcium content specifically, yes, unhulled sesame generally retains more calcium than hulled sesame, since much of the calcium sits in the outer hull that gets removed in processing. Both are nutritionally useful, but if bone health is your main goal, the darker, unhulled variety is the better choice.

**Can sesame seeds cause any problems in perimenopause?**
Sesame allergy, while uncommon, does exist and should be taken seriously if you notice a reaction. Beyond that, the main practical caution is overeating sweetened preparations like ladoos and chikki, which adds more sugar and calories than most women need regularly.

**Should I tell my doctor I eat sesame seeds regularly?**
It is not usually necessary to mention ordinary dietary amounts, but it is worth mentioning if you are taking a concentrated sesame seed supplement or sesame oil in therapeutic amounts, simply so your doctor has a complete picture of what you are consuming alongside any medication or hormone therapy.
