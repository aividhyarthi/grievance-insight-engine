Chukandar finds its way into Indian kitchens as a salad, a raita, a juice, or a sabzi, usually praised by an aunty or a well-meaning relative as "good for your blood." During perimenopause, when fatigue, cardiovascular risk, and bone health all become more pressing concerns, it is worth asking what beetroot actually offers, separate from the general folk wisdom around it.

## What Does Beetroot Actually Do for Perimenopausal Bodies?

**Beetroot's best-supported benefit relates to dietary nitrates, compounds the body converts into nitric oxide, a molecule that helps blood vessels relax and improves blood flow.** Several controlled studies, including some using beetroot juice specifically, have shown modest reductions in blood pressure and improved exercise performance in various populations. This is genuinely relevant to perimenopause, a stage of life when cardiovascular risk begins rising as oestrogen's protective effect on blood vessels declines, making foods that support vascular health worth including as part of a broader approach, not as a replacement for medical management of blood pressure.

**Beetroot also provides folate, potassium, and manganese, along with betalains, the pigments responsible for its deep red colour, which have antioxidant properties in laboratory studies.** The practical significance of betalains specifically for perimenopausal symptoms has not been well studied in humans, so this should be understood as a plausible, not proven, benefit.

## Is Beetroot Actually a Good Source of Iron for Perimenopausal Fatigue?

**Beetroot has a strong folk reputation as a blood-building food, but its iron content is more modest than this reputation suggests, and it is non-haem iron, the plant-based form that the body absorbs less efficiently than the haem iron found in meat, poultry, and fish.** It can still contribute meaningfully to overall iron intake, particularly when eaten alongside a vitamin C source such as citrus, tomato, or amla, which significantly improves non-haem iron absorption.

**If you are experiencing persistent fatigue, especially alongside heavy perimenopausal bleeding, beetroot alone is not an adequate response.** Iron deficiency significant enough to cause real fatigue needs proper testing, usually a ferritin blood test, and may need supplementation or treatment of the underlying heavy bleeding rather than relying on dietary measures alone.

## How Should You Actually Include Beetroot in Your Diet?

**Raw, grated beetroot in a salad or raita preserves more of its nitrate and vitamin content than prolonged boiling, though lightly cooked beetroot, as in a sabzi or soup, is still a reasonable way to include it regularly.** Beetroot juice, now widely available in India, concentrates the nitrate content and has been the form used in much of the blood pressure research, though whole beetroot provides fibre that juice does not.

**A few practical cautions are worth knowing before you start eating it regularly.** Pink or reddish urine and stool after eating beetroot, sometimes called beeturia, is completely harmless and simply reflects how your individual body metabolises its pigments, not a sign of bleeding or a medical problem. Beetroot is also relatively high in oxalates, compounds that can contribute to certain types of kidney stones in susceptible people, so anyone with a history of oxalate kidney stones should moderate intake and mention it to their doctor.

## In the Indian Context

**Beetroot is inexpensive and widely available across Indian markets year-round, making it one of the more accessible vegetables to add regularly to a perimenopausal diet, usually at a fraction of the cost of imported supplements marketed for similar purposes.** It fits naturally into existing meal patterns, whether as a salad alongside roti and dal, blended into a juice, or added to a mixed vegetable sabzi, without requiring any special preparation or sourcing.

**If high blood pressure runs in your family or you have noticed your own readings creeping up since your periods became irregular, mention this to your doctor rather than relying on beetroot as a stand-in for proper monitoring and, where needed, medication.** Dietary measures like regular beetroot intake work best as a genuine complement to medical care, not a substitute for it, particularly once cardiovascular risk factors have actually been identified through testing. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can beetroot help with perimenopausal hot flashes?**
There is no direct evidence linking beetroot to hot flash relief. Its best-supported benefits relate to blood pressure and vascular function, not vasomotor symptoms specifically.

**Is beetroot juice better than eating whole beetroot?**
Juice concentrates the nitrate content studied in blood pressure research, but whole beetroot retains fibre that supports digestion and blood sugar stability, so both have a place depending on your goal.

**Can I eat beetroot every day?**
For most people, yes, in normal dietary amounts, though anyone with a history of oxalate kidney stones or on blood pressure medication should discuss regular intake with their doctor first.

**Does beetroot interact with any medications?**
It can have an additive blood-pressure-lowering effect alongside blood pressure medication, so mention regular beetroot or beetroot juice consumption to your doctor, especially if your blood pressure readings have been unusually low.
