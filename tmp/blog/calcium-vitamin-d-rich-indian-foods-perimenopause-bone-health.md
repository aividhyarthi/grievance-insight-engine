Nobody tells you that the years right before menopause are quietly the most important years for your bones, not the years after. By the time you notice a fracture or a low bone density scan result, a good chunk of the loss has already happened. Oestrogen protects bone, and as it starts falling through perimenopause, bone breakdown outpaces bone rebuilding faster than most women realise, often years before the periods actually stop. The good news is that food is one of the few levers you have real, daily control over, and Indian kitchens already hold more bone-friendly ingredients than most people give them credit for. The trick is knowing which ones, and how much of them you actually need.

## Which Indian Foods Are Actually High in Calcium?

**Ragi (finger millet) is one of the best calcium sources available in an ordinary Indian kitchen.** A 100-gram serving carries roughly 300 to 350 mg of calcium, more than an equivalent amount of cow's milk, which makes ragi mudde, ragi dosa, or a simple ragi porridge a genuinely useful daily habit rather than a "health food" gimmick. **Sesame seeds (til) are even more concentrated**, a tablespoon of roasted til or a small piece of til chikki adds a meaningful calcium boost, and til is already woven into many regional winter and festival foods. **Dairy remains the most reliable everyday source** for households that consume it: a glass of milk, a cup of dahi, or 100 grams of paneer each carry roughly 250 to 300 mg. **Leafy greens like methi, moringa (drumstick leaves), and amaranth (chaulai) add calcium alongside other bone-supporting nutrients** like vitamin K and magnesium, though the calcium in greens is somewhat less easily absorbed than dairy calcium because of naturally occurring oxalates.

## Why Is Vitamin D the Harder Part to Fix?

**Very few foods naturally contain meaningful vitamin D**, which is why deficiency is so common even among women who eat well. Fatty fish (like mackerel or sardines), egg yolks, and fortified foods like some milks and cereals provide some, but for most vegetarian Indian households, food alone realistically covers only a fraction of daily needs. **Sunlight is the body's main vitamin D factory**, roughly 15 to 20 minutes of midday sun on bare arms and legs, a few times a week, triggers your skin to produce it. **In the Indian context, this is harder than it sounds**: long working hours indoors, pollution in many cities, sun avoidance for skin-tone reasons, and clothing that covers most skin all reduce actual exposure, which is part of why vitamin D deficiency shows up so often on blood tests even in a famously sunny country. A simple blood test (25-hydroxy vitamin D) can tell you where you stand, and if levels are low, a doctor can guide a supplement dose rather than guessing.

## How Much Do You Actually Need Each Day?

| Nutrient | Daily target (perimenopause) | Good Indian food sources |
|---|---|---|
| Calcium | 1,000-1,200 mg | Ragi, sesame seeds, dairy, moringa leaves, amaranth, dried figs |
| Vitamin D | 600-800 IU (higher if deficient, per doctor) | Egg yolk, fatty fish, fortified milk, sunlight exposure |
| Magnesium | 310-320 mg | Almonds, pumpkin seeds, whole grains, leafy greens |
| Vitamin K | Supports calcium use in bone | Methi, spinach, coriander, cabbage |
| Protein | 1-1.2 g/kg body weight | Dal, paneer, dahi, eggs, fish, soy |

## Does It Matter How You Spread Calcium Across the Day?

**Yes, somewhat.** The body absorbs calcium more efficiently in doses of around 500 mg or less at a time, so a single very large calcium-heavy meal is used less efficiently than the same total spread across breakfast, lunch, and dinner. In practice, this usually means it's more useful to add a small calcium source at each meal (a glass of milk at breakfast, a til-based snack mid-morning, dahi with lunch, greens with dinner) than to try to load it all into one dish.

## In the Indian Context

**Vegetarian households can meet calcium targets fairly comfortably** through dairy, ragi, sesame, and greens, but vitamin D remains the tougher nutrient for anyone avoiding fish and eggs, which makes sun exposure and, often, a doctor-recommended supplement genuinely important rather than optional. **Cost is rarely the barrier here**: ragi, til, and seasonal greens are among the more affordable staples in most Indian markets, so this is less about budget and more about building the habit into a routine that already leans on rice, wheat, and dal. **If you've never had a bone density check and are in your 40s, especially with a family history of osteoporosis, early menopause, or a slight build, it's worth asking your gynaecologist about a DEXA scan** rather than waiting for a fracture to raise the question. `/quiz` can help you get a clearer picture of where your other perimenopause symptoms stand, and `/guide` has more on preparing your bones for this stage more broadly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I get enough calcium without eating dairy?**
Yes, though it takes more deliberate planning. Ragi, sesame seeds, moringa leaves, amaranth, and dried figs together can meet most of a vegan or dairy-avoiding woman's calcium needs, it just requires including a few of these most days rather than relying on one large source.

**Is it too late to improve bone health if I'm already in my late 40s?**
No. Diet, weight-bearing exercise, and, where needed, medical treatment can all still meaningfully slow bone loss at this stage. The earlier you start, the more you preserve, but there is no point at which it stops being worthwhile.

**Should I take a calcium or vitamin D supplement without testing first?**
It's better to test first where possible, particularly for vitamin D, since both deficiency and excess (from over-supplementing) carry risks. A simple blood test and a conversation with your doctor can guide the right dose for you, rather than guessing.

**Does hormone therapy affect how much calcium or vitamin D I need?**
Hormone therapy helps protect bone density but doesn't replace the need for adequate calcium and vitamin D, the two work together. If you're on or considering hormone therapy, it's worth discussing your bone health and nutrient levels with your doctor as part of the same conversation.
