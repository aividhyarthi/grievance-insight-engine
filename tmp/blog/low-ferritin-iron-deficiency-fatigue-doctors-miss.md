"Your blood test is normal, it's probably just perimenopause." It's a sentence a lot of exhausted women hear, and sometimes it's true. But sometimes the blood test that came back "normal" only checked haemoglobin, not ferritin, and those are not the same thing. A woman can have perfectly normal haemoglobin and still be running on empty iron stores, tired in a way that no amount of sleep fixes.

Given how common heavy periods are in perimenopause, and how common iron deficiency already is among Indian women generally, this is one of the more fixable causes of fatigue that gets missed simply because the right test was never ordered.

## Why perimenopause makes low ferritin so common

**Heavier and more frequent bleeding drains iron stores faster than your diet can replace them.** As progesterone drops relative to oestrogen in many perimenopausal cycles, the uterine lining can build up more than usual before shedding, leading to heavier periods, longer periods, or periods that arrive more often than every month. Each of these means more iron lost through blood, month after month, often for years before menopause finally stops it.

**Most women underestimate how much heavier their periods have actually gotten.** Because the change is gradual, many don't register that what used to be two heavy days is now four or five, or that they're now going through pads or tampons twice as fast. That gradual creep is exactly the pattern that quietly depletes ferritin without ever producing a single dramatic, obviously-alarming period.

**Dietary iron absorption can also dip with age and gut changes**, and vegetarian diets, common across India, provide iron in a form (non-haem iron) that the body absorbs less efficiently than the iron in meat and fish, making the balance even harder to maintain during a heavier-bleeding stretch.

## Why the symptoms get blamed on "just perimenopause"

**Low ferritin and perimenopause share an almost identical symptom list**: fatigue that doesn't improve with rest, brain fog, difficulty concentrating, hair thinning or shedding, feeling cold, restless legs at night, and even mood changes. Because perimenopause is already expected to cause most of these, it becomes the default explanation, and ferritin never gets specifically checked.

**A standard blood panel often stops at haemoglobin.** Many routine check-ups order a complete blood count, which includes haemoglobin, but ferritin is a separate test that has to be specifically requested. If nobody asks for it by name, it frequently doesn't get run, even when fatigue is the main complaint.

**Doctors under time pressure default to the most obvious explanation.** In a short consultation, "your haemoglobin is fine, this is likely perimenopause fatigue" is a faster conclusion to reach than digging into whether ferritin specifically has been tested, especially if heavy periods weren't mentioned directly.

## What low ferritin actually feels like

**Fatigue that sleep doesn't fix.** This is the hallmark difference many women describe: perimenopausal tiredness from disrupted sleep tends to improve somewhat with a good night's rest, while low-ferritin fatigue often persists even after adequate sleep.

**Hair shedding that feels more than "normal" thinning.** Handfuls in the shower or on the pillow, beyond the gradual thinning texture change many women notice in perimenopause anyway.

**Restless legs, especially at night**, along with a craving for ice or a strange urge to chew on it (a classic, specific sign of iron deficiency called pica).

**Getting breathless or your heart racing with mild exertion**, like climbing a flight of stairs that never used to bother you.

| Ferritin level (approximate, labs vary) | What it typically means | What to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Above 50 ng/mL | Generally adequate stores | Unlikely to be the main fatigue cause |
| 20 to 50 ng/mL | Low-normal, borderline depleted | Worth discussing with a doctor if fatigue is significant |
| Below 20 ng/mL | Depleted iron stores | Usually warrants dietary changes plus supplementation |
| Below 10 to 12 ng/mL | Often associated with iron-deficiency symptoms even without anaemia | Should be addressed with a doctor's guidance |

## In the Indian context

Iron deficiency is already widespread among Indian women even outside perimenopause, national health survey data has repeatedly shown a large share of women of reproductive age have some degree of iron deficiency or anaemia, often linked to diet, earlier pregnancies, and heavy periods that were never properly evaluated. Perimenopause adds a second wave of risk on top of an already-common baseline.

A ferritin test is inexpensive and widely available, typically costing around ₹400 to ₹900 at most diagnostic labs and hospitals across Indian cities, and can often be added on to a routine blood panel with a single extra request. If your doctor has only ordered a CBC, it is reasonable to specifically ask, "can we also check my ferritin," particularly if your periods have gotten heavier.

Dietary iron sources accessible in most Indian kitchens include dark leafy greens like spinach and methi, jaggery, dates, pumpkin seeds, and lean meat or fish where eaten, paired with a source of vitamin C (amla, citrus, tomatoes) to improve absorption, while tea or coffee close to meals can reduce iron absorption and is worth spacing out. For confirmed deficiency, doctors commonly prescribe iron supplementation for a few months with a repeat ferritin test to confirm levels have recovered.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can my haemoglobin be normal but my ferritin still be low?**
Yes, and this is actually common. Ferritin, your stored iron, typically drops before haemoglobin does, so you can have normal haemoglobin and still be significantly iron deficient with real symptoms.

**How do I know if I should ask for a ferritin test?**
If you have persistent fatigue that doesn't improve with rest, heavier or more frequent periods than before, hair shedding, restless legs, or cravings for ice, it's reasonable to specifically request a ferritin test rather than relying on a routine blood count alone.

**Can low ferritin cause brain fog, not just tiredness?**
Yes, iron plays a role in oxygen delivery to the brain and in neurotransmitter production, so low ferritin can contribute to difficulty concentrating and mental fogginess, symptoms that overlap heavily with perimenopause itself.

**How long does it take to fix low ferritin?**
With dietary changes and supplementation where needed, ferritin levels typically start improving within 6 to 12 weeks, though it can take a few months of consistent treatment to fully rebuild stores, with a repeat blood test to confirm.

**Is it safe to just start taking iron supplements without a blood test?**
It's best to confirm low ferritin with a blood test first, since taking iron supplements without a genuine deficiency can cause side effects and, in rare cases, iron overload. A doctor can guide the right dose based on your actual levels.
