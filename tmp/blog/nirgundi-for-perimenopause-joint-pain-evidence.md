If your knees have started aching in a way they never did in your thirties, or your shoulder stiffens up for no obvious reason some mornings, you may have been pointed toward "nirgundi" by an Ayurvedic practitioner, a neighbour, or a well-meaning relative who swears by the oil. It's one of the most commonly recommended Ayurvedic herbs for joint and muscle complaints in India, sold everywhere from classical pharmacies to the shelf of your local kirana store as an oil, a paste, or a tablet. But it's also one of the most confused herbs in this space, because its name sounds similar to, and is sometimes mixed up with, a completely different plant used in the West for hormonal symptoms.

## What Is Nirgundi, Traditionally?

**In classical Ayurvedic texts**, nirgundi is classified as a herb that pacifies vata, the dosha most associated with pain, stiffness, and movement-related complaints. It appears in formulations for arthritis-type joint pain, sprains, and general musculoskeletal discomfort, and is one of the more widely stocked herbs at Ayurvedic pharmacies across India precisely because joint complaints are such a common reason people seek Ayurvedic treatment in the first place.

**The leaves and the oil extracted from them** are the two most common forms. The oil is typically warmed slightly and massaged into painful joints, sometimes alongside other warming oils, while the leaves themselves may be used in a poultice applied directly to a swollen area, or taken internally as part of a broader formulation for pain and inflammation.

## Don't Confuse It With Chasteberry

**This is the single most important clarification to make.** Vitex negundo (nirgundi) and Vitex agnus-castus (chasteberry, sometimes sold as "vitex" in Western supplement aisles and already covered in its own right for its effect on PMS and cyclical mood symptoms) belong to the same genus but are different species, used traditionally for entirely different purposes. Nirgundi's traditional and research focus is joint and musculoskeletal pain; chasteberry's is a pituitary and prolactin-related effect relevant to the menstrual cycle. Seeing "Vitex" on a label is not enough information, the species matters, and it's worth checking exactly which one you're being offered before assuming it does what a friend's "vitex supplement" did for her.

## What Does the Evidence Actually Show?

**Laboratory and animal research** on Vitex negundo extracts has reasonably consistently found anti-inflammatory and pain-reducing activity, with proposed mechanisms including reduced production of inflammatory compounds in the body. This gives a plausible biological basis for its traditional use in joint pain.

**Human clinical trials** are far fewer and generally smaller in scale than what exists for better-studied anti-inflammatory options. Some small studies on topical nirgundi oil preparations for joint pain and swelling have shown encouraging results, but the overall body of rigorous, well-controlled human research remains limited compared to options with a longer global research history.

**Evidence specific to perimenopausal joint pain** does not exist as its own dedicated research category. Perimenopausal joint pain is a real, biologically plausible phenomenon, oestrogen has anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective effects, and its decline is increasingly recognised as a genuine contributor to new or worsening joint symptoms in this life stage. But no study has specifically tested nirgundi against this particular cause of joint pain, as opposed to joint pain and inflammation more broadly.

## Topical Use Versus Oral Use

**Topical oil application** is generally considered the lower-risk way to try nirgundi, since it limits systemic absorption compared to swallowing a tablet or extract, and this is also the most traditional and most studied form of use.

**Oral tablets or extracts** carry more uncertainty, both in terms of how much active compound you're actually getting (since standardisation varies considerably between brands) and in terms of potential interaction with other medication you may be taking, particularly anti-inflammatory drugs or blood thinners, where combining effects isn't well mapped out.

## In the Indian Context: Quality and Access

Nirgundi oil and tablets are widely and inexpensively available across India, at Ayurvedic pharmacies, general stores, and increasingly online, often marketed simply as a "joint pain oil" without much detail on standardisation or concentration.

**India's herbal product market is loosely regulated** compared to pharmaceutical medication, meaning the actual concentration of active compounds in a bottle of nirgundi oil can vary considerably between brands, and even between batches from the same manufacturer. This matters less for a gentle topical massage oil than it would for a concentrated oral extract, but it's still worth buying from a known, established brand rather than an unlabelled or unusually cheap product.

**A qualified Ayurvedic practitioner or your doctor** can help you figure out whether your joint pain genuinely fits a pattern nirgundi is traditionally suited for, or whether it needs a different kind of evaluation first, since joint pain in your forties can have several causes beyond hormonal change, including early osteoarthritis, thyroid dysfunction, or an autoimmune process, each of which needs its own management approach.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is nirgundi the same as the "vitex" supplement sold online for hormonal balance?**
No. Nirgundi is Vitex negundo, traditionally used for joint pain. The Western "vitex" or chasteberry supplement is Vitex agnus-castus, a different species used for PMS and cyclical mood symptoms. Check the species name, not just the word "Vitex," before assuming they do the same thing.

**Can nirgundi oil be used alongside a doctor-prescribed anti-inflammatory medication?**
Possibly, but this is exactly the kind of combination to mention to your doctor first, since topical use is generally lower-risk but not risk-free, and your doctor can advise based on your specific medication and joint condition.

**Does it actually treat the hormonal cause of perimenopausal joint pain?**
No. Nirgundi may help with the pain and inflammation symptom itself, based on its traditional use and some laboratory evidence, but it does not address declining oestrogen, which is the underlying driver for many women's perimenopausal joint symptoms. For that, a conversation with a gynaecologist about your broader symptom picture, including whether hormone therapy is appropriate for you, is the more direct route.

**Where can I get reliable guidance on using it?**
A registered Ayurvedic practitioner (BAMS or equivalent) can advise on correct use, and your doctor should be kept informed, particularly to rule out other causes of joint pain first.
