Most supplement conversations in perimenopause circles are about things like magnesium, vitamin D, or omega-3s, nutrients your body needs and may be short of. DHEA (dehydroepiandrosterone) is a different category of thing entirely, and it gets lumped in with "supplements" in a way that can be genuinely misleading. It is not a vitamin or mineral your body is simply missing. It is a hormone precursor, a substance your adrenal glands produce that your body then converts into oestrogen and testosterone. That distinction matters enormously for how carefully it should be approached.

## What DHEA actually is, and why it is not like your other supplements

**DHEA is produced naturally by the adrenal glands**, and levels decline with age in both men and women, which is part of why it is marketed as an "anti-ageing" supplement in some markets. Once absorbed, the body converts DHEA into other hormones, principally oestrogen and testosterone, depending on the tissue and the person. This is fundamentally different from something like vitamin D, which your body uses directly without converting it into a different, more powerful hormone along the way.

**Because of that conversion, taking DHEA is, functionally, a way of raising your own oestrogen and testosterone levels**, even though it is sold in some countries without a prescription and marketed alongside shelf-stable vitamins. The casual framing around it in wellness content does not match how it actually behaves physiologically.

## What the evidence says for perimenopausal symptoms specifically

**The strongest and most consistent evidence is for vaginal DHEA**, used as a local, low-dose insert specifically for vaginal dryness and painful intercourse related to declining oestrogen, often in women who are further along in the menopause transition. Several clinical trials support this specific, localised use, and it is used in some countries as a recognised treatment for this particular symptom, with much lower systemic absorption than an oral tablet.

**Oral DHEA for broader symptoms like mood, energy, or libido has a much weaker and more mixed evidence base.** Some smaller studies suggest modest benefits for mood or sexual function in certain groups of women, particularly those with notably low baseline DHEA or adrenal insufficiency, but results across studies are inconsistent, and oral dosing raises systemic hormone levels in a way that is harder to predict and control than the localised vaginal form.

## The real risks that make this different from "just another supplement"

**Because DHEA raises oestrogen and testosterone, its side effects are hormonal, not just digestive or mild.** These can include acne, oily skin, unwanted facial or body hair growth, scalp hair thinning (the opposite effect you might want), voice changes, mood shifts, and in theory, effects on hormone-sensitive tissue that make it a genuine concern for anyone with a personal or strong family history of breast, ovarian, or uterine cancer. It can also interact with hormone therapy itself, with blood thinners, and with certain other medications, in ways that need a doctor's input to anticipate.

**This is also why DHEA is treated very differently across countries.** In the United States it has historically been sold over the counter as a dietary supplement, a regulatory quirk more than a safety endorsement. In India, it is generally not available as a casual over-the-counter wellness product the way magnesium or vitamin D is, and sourcing it informally, through unregulated online sellers or trips abroad, removes any quality control or medical oversight entirely, which is a genuinely risky combination for a hormonally active substance.

## In the Indian context

**India's broader supplement market is already loosely regulated**, with wide variation in purity and labelling accuracy even for straightforward vitamins, so the same caution applies doubly to something as hormonally active as DHEA. If a doctor does recommend it, usually for vaginal dryness specifically or in a specialist setting for diagnosed low DHEA, getting it through a proper prescription channel rather than an informal import or online source matters more here than it would for a basic vitamin.

**If you are on hormone therapy already, do not add DHEA on your own initiative.** The combination needs a doctor who understands both your current regimen and your symptom pattern, because stacking two hormonally active approaches without coordination raises the risk of side effects without necessarily improving symptoms further.

| Form | Strongest evidence for | Key caution |
|---|---|---|
| Vaginal DHEA (local insert) | Vaginal dryness, painful intercourse | Lower systemic absorption, still needs a prescription |
| Oral DHEA | Mixed evidence for mood, energy, libido | Raises systemic oestrogen/testosterone, more side effects |
| Any DHEA alongside hormone therapy | Not established as routine practice | Needs direct doctor supervision and monitoring |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is DHEA the same as taking HRT?**
No, but it is related. HRT typically supplies oestrogen and/or progesterone directly, while DHEA is a precursor your body converts into these hormones itself, with less predictable and controllable blood levels than direct hormone therapy.

**Can I buy DHEA supplements online if they are not easily available in India?**
This is not advisable. Sourcing a hormonally active substance through unregulated channels removes quality control, correct dosing, and medical oversight, all of which matter significantly more for DHEA than for a basic vitamin.

**Will DHEA help with hot flashes?**
Evidence for hot flash relief specifically is weak and inconsistent. It is far better supported for vaginal dryness in its local form, and hormone therapy or other evidence-based options are generally considered first for hot flashes.

**What should I ask my doctor if I am curious about trying it?**
Ask whether your specific symptoms, and any existing conditions or medications including hormone therapy, make DHEA a reasonable option, what form and monitoring would be involved, and whether a simpler, better-studied option should be tried first.
