And there she is: a school friend's glowing gym selfie, a cousin's effortless-looking skincare routine, an influencer in her mid-forties who looks a decade younger and talks about "thriving" through menopause with a supplement you have never heard of. This specific kind of comparison, not general social media use but the particular sting of watching other women seem to sail through the same decade you are struggling through, deserves its own honest conversation.

## Why Does This Comparison Feel Worse Now Than It Used To?

**Perimenopausal hormonal fluctuation genuinely affects mood regulation and emotional resilience, which means a thought that you might have shrugged off at thirty can land much harder at forty-five.** Fluctuating oestrogen affects serotonin and other mood-related brain chemistry, and when you are already running on poor sleep and a shorter emotional fuse, a single scroll through someone else's highlight reel has more room to lodge itself and spiral than it would on a well-rested, hormonally stable day.

**There is also a timing problem specific to this decade.** Many of the physical and emotional symptoms of perimenopause are invisible from the outside, a hot flash does not show up in a photo, brain fog does not appear in a caption, so the comparison is structurally unfair in a way it was not earlier in life: you are comparing your full, often difficult internal experience against someone else's carefully selected external image, often without realising that is exactly what you are doing.

## Is Everyone Else Actually Doing Fine, or Does It Just Look That Way?

**Almost certainly, a meaningful number of the women whose posts trigger this comparison are also perimenopausal and also struggling, just not posting about it.** Social media rewards curated, positive, aspirational content, and most people, understandably, do not post their 3am wake-up or their worst brain fog day. The gym selfie does not show the three days of joint pain that followed, and the glowing skincare routine does not show the sleep that made the skin look that way possible, or the editing that happened before posting.

**A specific category worth naming directly: wellness and anti-ageing content aimed at midlife women is often, quite deliberately, designed to make you feel slightly inadequate, because that feeling is what sells supplements, skincare, and programmes.** Recognising the business model behind a lot of this content does not eliminate the feeling, but it does change how much authority you give it.

## What Actually Helps, Beyond "Just Log Off"?

**Being deliberate about your feed, rather than passively consuming whatever the algorithm serves you, makes a real difference over time.** This does not have to mean quitting social media entirely, which is rarely realistic or necessary. It means actively curating: unfollowing or muting specific accounts that consistently leave you feeling worse, regardless of their intention, and following at least a few voices who talk honestly about the harder parts of this decade, so your feed reflects a more complete, more accurate picture of what forty-something actually looks like for most women.

**Timing matters too.** Scrolling during the hours when your mood is already most fragile, late at night when you cannot sleep, or first thing in the morning before you have fully woken up, tends to amplify the comparison effect. Simply moving your main scrolling window to a steadier part of the day, even without reducing total time spent, can soften how much it affects you.

## In the Indian Context

**Comparison in Indian social circles often has an added layer: extended family WhatsApp groups and relatives' social media posts that implicitly compare you not just to strangers but to cousins, sisters-in-law, and childhood friends whose lives you have followed for decades, adding a familial weight general social media comparison does not carry.** A relative's effortless-seeming fiftieth birthday party post can trigger a very specific, personal version of this comparison that a stranger's post never would.

**If scrolling consistently leaves you feeling low, anxious, or inadequate, and this has been going on for weeks rather than being an occasional bad night, it is worth mentioning to your doctor as part of your broader mood picture, not dismissing as "just social media."** Persistent low mood deserves a proper conversation, and a free symptom check through the site's /quiz, or connecting with others going through the same stage through the /community space, can also help you feel less alone in it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to feel worse about my own perimenopause after seeing other women's posts about theirs?**
Yes, this is a common experience, especially when other women's content is framed positively ("thriving through menopause") while your own day feels anything but. The gap between someone else's framing and your lived reality can feel like a personal shortfall, when it usually is not.

**Should I just quit social media entirely?**
That is a reasonable choice for some women, but it is not necessary for everyone. Deliberately curating who you follow, and being mindful of when and how much you scroll, often achieves most of the benefit without requiring you to give it up entirely.

**Why does this bother me more now than it did in my thirties?**
Hormonal fluctuation during perimenopause genuinely affects mood regulation and emotional resilience, which can make comparison, criticism, and other emotional triggers land harder than they used to, independent of anything actually changing about your circumstances.

**Could this be a sign of depression rather than just comparison fatigue?**
If low mood, loss of interest in things you normally enjoy, or persistent sadness is accompanying the comparison and lasting most days for two weeks or more, that moves beyond ordinary comparison fatigue and is worth raising directly with your doctor.
