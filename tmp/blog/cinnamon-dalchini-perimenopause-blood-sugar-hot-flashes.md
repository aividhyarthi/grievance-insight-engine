You probably already have dalchini sitting in your masala dabba, right next to the cardamom and cloves, mostly used for chai and biryani. What you may not have heard is that cinnamon has a real, if modest, body of research behind it for blood sugar control, which matters more than it used to once perimenopause starts making your blood sugar swing in ways it never did in your twenties. The hot flash claims attached to cinnamon online are a different story, and the two deserve to be separated clearly.

## Why does cinnamon come up so much for perimenopause?

**The blood sugar connection is the real one.** As oestrogen declines through perimenopause, insulin sensitivity tends to drop too, which is part of why many women notice sugar cravings, energy crashes after meals, and weight that settles more stubbornly around the middle even without eating differently than before. Several randomised trials, done mostly in people with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes, show that cinnamon in doses of around one to six grams a day can modestly lower fasting blood glucose and improve insulin sensitivity. The effect size is not dramatic, usually a small single-digit percentage improvement, but it is a real, reproducible finding across multiple studies, not a single outlier trial.

**The hot flash connection is much weaker.** Cinnamon shows up constantly in home-remedy lists for hot flashes, often lumped in alongside fennel, flaxseed, and other "cooling" or "balancing" foods. There is no good clinical trial evidence that cinnamon reduces hot flash frequency or intensity. The confusion likely comes from cinnamon's general metabolic benefits getting generalised into a broader "good for hormones" reputation it has not actually earned for this specific symptom.

**Where it probably helps most** is in the indirect way: steadier blood sugar through the day can mean fewer energy crashes, less reactive hunger, and potentially gentler mood swings for women whose symptoms are tangled up with blood sugar instability, which is a meaningfully large number of us in our forties.

## How much is safe, and does the variety matter?

This is where the two varieties matter. Cassia cinnamon, the kind sold loose by weight in most Indian kirana stores and used in garam masala, contains a compound called coumarin, which in large, concentrated amounts over time can stress the liver. For ordinary cooking, a teaspoon or so sprinkled into your chai, oats, or dal now and then, this is not a practical concern for most healthy adults. The concern becomes real with concentrated cinnamon supplement capsules taken daily over months, where coumarin can accumulate to levels that matter, particularly for anyone with existing liver disease.

**Ceylon cinnamon**, sometimes labelled "true cinnamon" and increasingly available in Indian supermarkets and online, contains far less coumarin and is the safer choice if you want to use cinnamon more concentratedly or daily, rather than just as an occasional spice.

**A practical middle ground**: using cinnamon generously in cooking, in your morning chai, sprinkled over fruit, stirred into dal, is a low-risk, genuinely pleasant way to work it into your week without needing to think about supplement dosing at all.

## Who should be more careful

Cinnamon in supplement form, not kitchen amounts, is where the caution really applies. If you have liver disease, talk to your doctor before taking concentrated cinnamon supplements. If you are on blood sugar medication, cinnamon's own mild blood-sugar-lowering effect means the combination could occasionally push levels lower than expected, so mention it to your doctor if you are using both. Cinnamon can also mildly thin the blood in high doses, so if you are on anticoagulants, stick to culinary amounts and ask before adding a supplement.

**In the Indian context**, dalchini is sold loose, pre-ground, or as whole sticks in virtually every local market, typically costing somewhere between ₹300 and ₹600 per kilogram depending on quality and whether it is cassia or Ceylon, making it one of the cheapest and most accessible items on this entire pillar of posts. Cinnamon-based supplement capsules are also widely available online in India, but given that India's supplement market is loosely regulated, with quality and actual cinnamon content varying meaningfully by brand, it is worth telling your doctor before starting any concentrated supplement, especially if you are also on hormone therapy or diabetes medication, since the interaction risk, while small, is real.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can cinnamon replace metformin or other diabetes medication?**
No. Cinnamon's effect on blood sugar is real but modest, nowhere near strong enough to substitute for prescribed diabetes medication. It can be a reasonable addition alongside medical treatment, with your doctor aware you are using it, but never a replacement.

**Does cinnamon tea actually help hot flashes?**
There is no solid clinical evidence that it does, despite how often this specific remedy circulates. Cinnamon tea is a pleasant, low-risk drink, but it should not be relied on as your main hot flash strategy if flashes are frequent or disruptive.

**Is Ceylon cinnamon worth the extra cost over cassia?**
If you only use cinnamon occasionally in cooking, the difference does not matter much. If you want to use it more concentratedly or daily for blood sugar support, Ceylon's much lower coumarin content makes it the more sensible long-term choice.

**How long does it take to notice any blood sugar benefit?**
Most clinical trials measuring cinnamon's effect on blood sugar ran for eight to twelve weeks before showing measurable improvement, so this is not a same-day or same-week effect; think of it as a slow, steady addition rather than a quick fix.

**Can I just add more cinnamon to everything instead of changing my overall diet?**
Cinnamon works best as one small piece of a broader approach to blood sugar, alongside reduced refined sugar, regular meal timing, and movement, not as a substitute for those bigger changes.
