Feet rarely get attention until something goes wrong with them, a cracked heel, a nagging ache, or worse, a diabetic foot complication that could have been caught early. Yet feet carry your entire body weight through tens of thousands of steps a year, and foot health has a quiet but real relationship with balance, mobility, and how comfortably you move through daily life as you age.

India also has a centuries-old tradition of dedicated foot care in the form of padabhyanga, Ayurvedic foot oil massage, practised long before "self-care" became a wellness buzzword. This article looks at what genuinely matters for foot health after 40, drawing on both modern podiatry and what padabhyanga actually offers.

## Why do feet deserve more attention after 40 specifically?

**Circulation to the feet naturally becomes somewhat less efficient with age, and this effect is significantly amplified in anyone with diabetes, a condition affecting a large and growing share of Indian adults over 40.** Reduced blood flow combined with diabetic nerve damage (neuropathy) means minor foot injuries, a blister, a small cut, a corn left untreated, can go unnoticed and progress to serious infection far more easily than most people expect. This is precisely why doctors treating diabetes emphasise foot checks as seriously as they do blood sugar monitoring, even though patients often find the advice easy to dismiss until a problem actually appears.

**Balance also has a genuine connection to foot health that is easy to overlook.** The soles of the feet contain dense nerve endings that feed your brain constant information about the ground beneath you, information your balance system depends on. Thickened calluses, poorly fitting footwear, or reduced sensation in the feet from age or nerve changes can all subtly degrade this input, contributing to the kind of balance uncertainty that raises fall risk, particularly on uneven Indian pavements and stairs.

## What does padabhyanga actually involve, and does the evidence support it?

**Padabhyanga is the Ayurvedic practice of massaging the feet with warm oil, traditionally done before sleep, using specific pressure applied to the sole, heel, and toes.** In Ayurvedic tradition, it is described as calming the nervous system, improving circulation, and supporting better sleep, and it has been practised as part of daily or weekly self-care routines in Indian households for generations, often as something elders did for children or that adult children now do for ageing parents.

**Modern research on foot massage specifically, separate from general Ayurvedic theory, does show some genuine support for a few of these claims.** Small studies on foot massage before bed have found improvements in sleep quality and reduced anxiety in some populations, plausibly through relaxation and increased parasympathetic nervous system activity, the body's "rest and digest" response. The evidence base is smaller and less rigorous than for many other health practices, so claims should stay modest, but a warm oil foot massage before bed is a low-risk, pleasant habit with at least some genuine physiological plausibility behind the relaxation benefit, independent of any specific therapeutic claim.

### Padabhyanga versus reflexology: are they the same thing?

They are related but distinct. **Padabhyanga is primarily a relaxation and circulation-focused oil massage rooted in Ayurvedic tradition**, while reflexology, a separate practice with origins in East Asian and Western alternative medicine traditions, is based on the specific claim that distinct points on the foot correspond to and can treat specific internal organs. **This organ-mapping claim in reflexology has not been supported by rigorous scientific evidence**, and it should not be relied upon to diagnose or treat any specific medical condition. The general relaxation and circulation benefits of foot massage, common to both traditions, are the part with genuine, if modest, evidence behind them.

| Practice | What it claims | Evidence quality |
|---|---|---|
| General foot massage (relaxation-focused) | Improves relaxation, circulation, possibly sleep | Modest but genuine supporting evidence |
| Padabhyanga (Ayurvedic oil massage) | Traditional benefits for sleep, nervous system, circulation | Supported by tradition; modest modern evidence for relaxation effect |
| Reflexology (organ-point mapping) | Specific foot points treat specific internal organs | Not supported by rigorous scientific evidence |
| Daily foot inspection for diabetes | Catches early skin, nerve, or circulation problems | Strongly supported medical practice |

## Does footwear actually matter as much as people say?

**Yes, and Indian footwear habits, a lot of flat chappals, unsupported sandals, and long periods of barefoot floor-sitting, deserve a closer, honest look after 40.** Footwear with minimal arch support or cushioning is fine for short periods but can contribute to joint strain over years of daily wear, particularly for anyone who is on their feet for long stretches or has existing knee or hip discomfort. **Choosing footwear with reasonable arch support and cushioning for daily wear, saving minimal or purely traditional footwear for shorter periods**, is a reasonable, evidence-informed adjustment rather than an all-or-nothing change.

**For anyone with diabetes specifically, footwear choice moves from a comfort issue to a genuine medical precaution.** Well-fitted, protective footwear that avoids pressure points and reduces injury risk is a standard part of diabetic foot care recommended by doctors, and going barefoot or wearing minimal footwear outdoors carries meaningfully higher injury risk for anyone with reduced foot sensation from neuropathy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How often should I do a foot self-check if I have diabetes?**
Daily is the standard recommendation from most doctors treating diabetes, checking for cuts, blisters, redness, swelling, or changes in skin colour or temperature, since diabetic nerve damage can mean an injury goes unnoticed without an active visual check.

**Is any cooking oil fine for padabhyanga, or does the type matter?**
Ayurvedic tradition often recommends specific oils based on individual constitution and season, and a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner can offer personalised guidance here. For general relaxation purposes without a specific therapeutic goal, a mild, unscented oil that does not irritate your skin is a reasonable, low-risk choice.

**Can reflexology actually treat conditions like digestive problems or headaches?**
There is no rigorous scientific evidence supporting the specific claim that particular foot points correspond to and can treat specific internal organs or conditions. Foot massage generally may offer genuine relaxation benefit, but it should not replace appropriate medical evaluation and treatment for any specific health condition.

**What foot symptoms should prompt a doctor's visit rather than home care?**
Any wound that is not healing within a couple of weeks, spreading redness, numbness or tingling that is new or worsening, or any foot symptom in someone with diabetes should be seen by a doctor promptly rather than managed at home, since these can indicate infection or nerve and circulation problems that need timely treatment.

Feet do a lot of quiet, unglamorous work over a lifetime, and giving them a few minutes of real attention each day, whether that looks like a diabetic foot check, a warm oil massage before bed, or simply better footwear, is a small habit with a genuinely outsized payoff for comfort, balance, and mobility as you age.
