A burning sensation that climbs up from the chest after a heavy dinner used to be an occasional nuisance. Somewhere past 40, for many people, it becomes a near-nightly visitor, sometimes bad enough to interrupt sleep or make you sit up in bed. This is acid reflux, and when it happens often enough, doctors call it GERD (gastroesophageal reflux disease). It is extremely common in India, driven partly by diet, partly by body changes that come with age, and it is worth understanding properly rather than just reaching for an antacid every night.

## Why does this seem to get worse after 40?

**The lower esophageal sphincter, the ring of muscle that is supposed to keep stomach contents from traveling back up, tends to lose some of its tone with age.** This is a gradual, mechanical change, not a disease in itself, but it means acid that would have stayed down at 30 now has an easier path upward at 45 or 50.

Several other age-related changes compound this. Stomach emptying can slow slightly, meaning food and acid sit longer than before. Weight gain, especially around the abdomen, increases pressure on the stomach, pushing contents upward. And certain medications commonly started in midlife, including some blood pressure drugs, pain relievers, and calcium channel blockers, can relax that same valve further as a side effect.

### The Indian dinner pattern problem

**Late, large, and spice- or oil-heavy dinners, a common pattern in many Indian households, are a near-perfect recipe for nighttime reflux.** Eating a substantial meal within two to three hours of lying down gives the stomach little time to empty before gravity stops helping. Add in chilli, excess oil, and fried snacks, all of which relax the valve and slow digestion, and the combination explains why so many people report reflux specifically after dinner rather than after breakfast or lunch.

## What actually helps, beyond avoiding spicy food?

**Meal timing matters more than most people expect.** Finishing dinner at least three hours before lying down gives gravity and digestion a real chance to work in your favour. If a late dinner is unavoidable due to work or family schedules, keeping that particular meal smaller and lighter helps considerably.

**Elevating the head of the bed, not just stacking extra pillows, makes a genuine difference for nighttime symptoms.** A wedge or blocks under the bed's legs at the head end keeps the whole upper body raised at a gentle angle, using gravity to help keep acid down through the night. Propping only the head on pillows tends to just bend the body at the waist, which can make reflux worse, not better.

**Weight around the midsection is one of the most modifiable risk factors for reflux.** Even a moderate reduction in abdominal weight can meaningfully reduce how often symptoms occur, independent of any dietary change.

## When is it more than just occasional heartburn?

Occasional reflux after a particularly heavy or spicy meal is common and not usually a concern. It becomes GERD, worth a doctor's attention, when it happens more than twice a week, when it disturbs sleep regularly, when swallowing becomes difficult or painful, or when there is unexplained weight loss alongside it.

**Long-standing, untreated GERD can lead to inflammation and changes in the lining of the food pipe over years**, which is why persistent symptoms deserve proper evaluation rather than years of self-managed antacid use. A doctor may recommend acid-suppressing medication for a defined period, and in some cases an endoscopy to look at the food pipe directly, particularly if symptoms are severe, long-standing, or accompanied by alarm signs.

## Reflux versus a heart problem: why it matters to know the difference

This is genuinely important after 40. Both acid reflux and early heart trouble can cause a burning or pressure sensation in the chest, and people sometimes dismiss serious cardiac symptoms as "just acidity," particularly men who are reluctant to seek care.

| Feature | More typical of reflux | More typical of a cardiac event |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Often related to meals, worse lying down | Can occur with exertion or at rest, unrelated to food |
| Nature of sensation | Burning, rising from stomach toward throat | Pressure, tightness, or heaviness, may spread to arm, jaw, or back |
| Associated symptoms | Sour taste, belching | Sweating, breathlessness, nausea, dizziness |
| Response to antacid | Often eases within minutes | Usually does not improve with antacids |
| What to do | Try reflux measures; see a doctor if frequent | Treat as an emergency; call 112 |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it safe to take antacids every single night?**
Occasional use is generally fine, but regular nightly reliance on antacids or acid-suppressing tablets for weeks or months without a doctor's input is a sign the underlying reflux needs proper evaluation rather than ongoing self-treatment.

**Does drinking milk help with acid reflux?**
It may give brief relief by temporarily coating the stomach lining, but milk also stimulates acid production afterward, so it is not a reliable or lasting fix, and full-fat milk late at night can sometimes make symptoms worse rather than better.

**Can stress make acid reflux worse?**
Yes. Stress does not directly cause excess acid in most people, but it can slow digestion, increase swallowing of air, and heighten sensitivity to the discomfort reflux causes, which is why reflux often feels worse during stressful periods.

**Is occasional reflux after a big festival meal something to worry about?**
Not usually, an isolated episode after an unusually heavy or rich meal is common and not a cause for concern on its own. It is frequent, regular reflux, several times a week over weeks, that is worth discussing with a doctor.

Acid reflux after 40 is rarely about willpower or giving up favourite foods entirely. It is usually a mechanical and timing issue that responds well to specific, practical changes, and when it doesn't, it deserves a proper medical look rather than years of nightly antacids.
