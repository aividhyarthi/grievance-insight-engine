A plate of dal, roti, vegetables, and rice looks complete, and in many ways it is. But "complete" on the plate does not always translate into "sufficient" in the bloodstream, and after 40, small shortfalls that the body once compensated for quietly start to show up as fatigue, brittle nails, slow-healing cuts, or a doctor raising an eyebrow at a routine blood report.

This isn't an argument for supplement shelves or imported superfoods. It's a look at which micronutrients actually tend to run low in Indian diets as people cross into their 40s, why that happens even when meals look balanced, and what a sensible, food-first response looks like.

## Why do micronutrient gaps become more common after 40?

**Several things shift at once around this age, and they compound rather than act alone.** Appetite and portion sizes often shrink gradually, absorption of certain nutrients, particularly B12 and calcium, becomes less efficient in the gut, and many people are simultaneously eating less of the foods that were their main source of a given nutrient without realising it, whether that is less milk, less red meat, or less time spent outdoors.

**Modern urban life adds its own layer.** Longer indoor working hours mean less sun exposure for vitamin D, highly processed or restaurant food tends to be lower in micronutrient density than home-cooked meals even when it looks similar in volume, and the sheer busyness of midlife can mean meals get smaller or more repetitive than they once were.

## Which deficiencies actually show up most often in Indian blood tests?

### Vitamin D

**Despite India's abundant sunshine, vitamin D deficiency is remarkably common across age groups, and it tends to get worse, not better, after 40.** Darker skin requires more sun exposure to produce the same amount of vitamin D, most people's exposed skin area has shrunk due to clothing and indoor lifestyles, and sunscreen use, while good for skin cancer prevention, further reduces synthesis. A blood test is the only reliable way to know where you stand.

### Vitamin B12

**B12 comes almost entirely from animal foods, which puts vegetarians and vegans at clear risk, but age itself is an independent risk factor too.** Stomach acid, which is needed to release B12 from food, tends to decline with age, so even people who eat meat, eggs, or dairy regularly can develop a deficiency later in life. Fatigue, tingling in the hands or feet, and memory complaints are sometimes dismissed as "just ageing" when a B12 deficiency is actually at play.

### Calcium and magnesium

**Many Indian households assume dairy intake automatically covers calcium, but portion sizes of milk, curd, and paneer are often smaller than people assume, and absorption also depends on adequate vitamin D.** Magnesium, found in whole grains, legumes, nuts, and leafy greens, is easy to under-eat once a diet leans more toward refined grains and less toward these whole foods.

### Iron and zinc

**Iron needs actually change after 40, particularly for women once menstrual blood loss patterns shift, but iron status should never be assumed in either direction.** Too little iron causes fatigue and poor concentration, while too much, taken without a genuine deficiency, can also cause problems, which is exactly why a blood test should guide any decision here rather than habit or marketing.

|---|---|---|---|
| Vitamin D | Very common | Sunlight exposure, fortified foods, fatty fish | Fatigue, bone or joint pain, low sun exposure |

## Can food alone close these gaps, or does everyone need a supplement?

**For most people, food and sunlight can close a meaningful part of the gap, but not always all of it, and that is exactly why testing matters more than assuming.** Someone with a confirmed significant vitamin D deficiency, for instance, is unlikely to correct it through diet and sun exposure alone within a reasonable time frame, and a doctor-guided supplement becomes genuinely useful there. Someone with only a mild or borderline reading may do perfectly well with food-first changes and a recheck in a few months.

**The honest answer is that it depends on the nutrient, the degree of deficiency, and individual factors like diet pattern and absorption**, which is precisely why a blanket "just take a multivitamin" approach misses the point. A multivitamin may provide a small buffer, but it rarely corrects an actual deficiency at the doses typically used, and it can give a false sense of the problem being handled when it is not.

## What about multivitamins, do they just solve this for everyone?

**A daily multivitamin feels like a reasonable insurance policy, but current evidence does not support it as a substitute for identifying and correcting an actual deficiency.** Large studies in healthy, well-nourished adults have generally not shown multivitamins to meaningfully reduce disease risk or extend life. They are not harmful for most people at standard doses, but they are also not a replacement for knowing your own numbers.

**India's supplement market is loosely regulated**, which means the actual content and quality of over-the-counter multivitamin and mineral tablets can vary between brands, so this is not a category to self-prescribe indefinitely without any medical input, particularly if you are already on other medication.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What blood tests should I ask for to check micronutrient levels after 40?**
A reasonable starting panel includes vitamin D, vitamin B12, a complete blood count (which flags iron-related anaemia), and calcium, with your doctor adding others based on your diet, symptoms, and history. This is a conversation to have directly with your doctor rather than a fixed checklist to request blindly.

**Can I just take a multivitamin instead of getting tested?**
A multivitamin may offer a small general buffer but is unlikely to correct an actual deficiency at typical doses, and it can mask the fact that a specific nutrient still needs proper attention. Testing first gives you and your doctor something concrete to act on.

**Are vegetarians more at risk of micronutrient gaps than non-vegetarians?**
Vegetarians, and especially vegans, face a higher risk specifically for vitamin B12 and sometimes iron and zinc, since these are more concentrated in animal foods. With deliberate food choices and, where needed, doctor-guided supplementation, these gaps are very manageable.

**Is it normal to feel more tired as you cross 40, or could it be a nutrient gap?**
Some reduction in energy can be part of normal ageing, but persistent, unexplained fatigue is also a classic sign of several micronutrient deficiencies, particularly B12, iron, and vitamin D. It is worth raising with your doctor rather than assuming it is simply age.

Most micronutrient gaps after 40 are quiet, common, and fixable once they are actually identified. A short conversation with your doctor and a basic blood panel will tell you far more than another round of guessing, and the fix, once you know what is actually low, is usually simpler than people expect.
