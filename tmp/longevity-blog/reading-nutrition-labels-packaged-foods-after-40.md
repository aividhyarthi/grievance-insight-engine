Most of us read a food label the way we read a terms-and-conditions page: a quick glance, maybe a check of the expiry date, and then straight into the trolley. After 40, with blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol all becoming genuinely more relevant, that quick glance starts costing more than it used to.

This article walks through how to actually read a packaged food label sold in India, where the misleading bits usually hide, and what is worth paying attention to without turning every trip to the kitchen into a research project.

## Why does label-reading matter more after 40?

**Blood pressure, blood sugar regulation, and cholesterol handling all tend to shift gradually after 40**, even in people who feel entirely well, and packaged food is where a surprising amount of sodium, added sugar, and refined carbohydrate enters an otherwise reasonable diet without anyone quite noticing. A samosa eaten occasionally is not the concern. A daily pattern of packaged snacks, sauces, and ready meals, each individually unremarkable, adding up across a week, is where the real effect tends to sit.

**This is not about eliminating packaged food entirely**, which is neither realistic nor necessary for most households balancing work, family, and limited time for cooking from scratch. It is about knowing which products are worth reaching for regularly and which are better kept occasional, and that distinction becomes much clearer once you know where to look on the label.

### What do front-of-pack claims actually mean?

**Terms like "healthy," "natural," "immunity-boosting," or "no added preservatives" printed prominently on the front of a package are largely unregulated marketing language in India**, not claims independently verified against a fixed standard the way a medicine's label would be. A product can be marketed as natural while still containing substantial added sugar, or labelled high in protein while also being high in sodium and saturated fat. None of this makes the product automatically unhealthy, but it does mean front-of-pack claims should prompt a look at the back of the pack, not replace it.

## How do you actually read the nutrition table and ingredient list?

**The ingredient list is ordered by weight, from most to least**, which is one of the most useful and most overlooked facts about food labels. If sugar, refined flour, or a vegetable oil appears within the first two or three ingredients, that tells you more about the product's actual composition than any claim on the front.

**The nutrition table itself is usually given per 100 grams or per serving, and it is worth noticing which one you are looking at**, since a package quoting "per serving" values for a snack with four servings inside can make the numbers look smaller than what you will likely eat in one sitting.

- **Added sugar** is the number to watch for anyone managing blood sugar, weight, or simply trying to reduce overall intake; Indian labelling increasingly separates "added sugar" from "total sugar," and the added figure is the more meaningful one for packaged snacks and beverages
- **Sodium** is listed in milligrams and adds up fast across namkeen, papad, instant noodles, ready-to-eat curries, and sauces; a single serving of some packaged snacks can account for a third or more of a reasonable daily total
- **Total fat and saturated fat** matter together with the ingredient list, since "vegetable oil" could mean several different oils with quite different effects on heart health
- **Fibre and protein** are genuinely useful numbers to compare between similar products, since higher fibre generally means a more filling, less blood-sugar-spiking option

## What are the common label traps worth knowing about?

**"No added sugar" is one of the most misread claims on Indian packaging.** It genuinely means no table sugar was added during manufacturing, but it does not account for naturally high-sugar ingredients like concentrated fruit juice, dates, or honey used as a sweetener, which can still push total sugar content fairly high. The total sugar figure in the nutrition table is the more honest number to compare.

**Multiple names for sugar scattered through a long ingredient list can also obscure how much sugar a product actually contains overall**, since sucrose, glucose syrup, fructose, and fruit concentrate are all forms of sugar that, added together, might rank much higher than any single one would suggest on its own.

| Label phrase | What it actually means | What to check instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Natural" / "healthy" | Largely unregulated marketing terms | Full ingredient list and nutrition table |
| "No added sugar" | No table sugar added during manufacturing | Total sugar figure; check for juice concentrate or similar ingredients |
| "Low fat" | Reduced fat, sometimes with added sugar to compensate for taste | Compare total sugar alongside the fat figure |
| "High protein" | Genuinely higher protein, but check the rest | Sodium and saturated fat on the same label |
| "Multigrain" | Contains more than one grain; not necessarily whole grain | Look for "whole wheat" or "whole grain" specifically listed first |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need to check every single packaged item I buy?**
Not realistically, and that would make grocery shopping unmanageable. It is more useful to check labels carefully for the items you buy and eat regularly, like a daily snack, cooking oil, or breakfast cereal, since those are where small differences compound over weeks and months.

**Is packaged food automatically worse than home-cooked food?**
Not automatically. Many packaged staples, like plain dals, whole grains, and frozen vegetables, are perfectly reasonable choices. The concern is mainly with ultra-processed snacks, sauces, and ready-to-eat meals high in added sugar, sodium, and refined carbohydrate, consumed as a regular daily pattern rather than occasionally.

**Why do some labels list sugar in grams and others in teaspoons?**
Indian labelling requirements have been evolving, and not all brands present information identically yet. If a label only gives grams, roughly four grams of sugar is equivalent to one teaspoon, which can make the number easier to picture in everyday terms.

**Should I avoid anything with preservatives listed on the label?**
Not necessarily. Many preservatives permitted in Indian packaged foods are considered safe at the approved levels used, and they serve a genuine purpose in preventing spoilage. The bigger concern for most people after 40 is usually added sugar and sodium content, not the presence of a preservative by itself.

Reading a food label properly takes perhaps thirty extra seconds once you know what to look for, which is a small price for the clearer picture it gives you about what is actually going into a daily habit. After 40, with more at stake in how your body handles sugar, salt, and fat, that half-minute spent at the shelf is a genuinely useful one to build into how you shop.
