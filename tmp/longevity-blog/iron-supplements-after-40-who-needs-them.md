Walk into almost any pharmacy in India and you will find iron tablets and tonics sold over the counter, often marketed as a general fix for tiredness. It is tempting, after 40, when fatigue becomes a more regular visitor, to simply start taking one. The trouble is that iron is one of the few common supplements where taking it without needing it can genuinely cause harm, not just waste money.

This article looks at who is actually likely to be low in iron after 40, why fatigue alone is a poor reason to self-start supplementation, and what a sensible, doctor-guided approach actually looks like.

## Why does iron deficiency get blamed for everything after 40?

**Low energy, pale skin, breathlessness on exertion, and brittle nails are all genuine signs that can point to iron deficiency**, and they also overlap heavily with dozens of other common conditions, from thyroid changes to poor sleep to simple overwork. Because iron deficiency is treatable and iron tablets are cheap and easy to buy, it becomes a convenient first guess, sometimes acted on before any blood test confirms it.

**The irony is that iron deficiency anaemia is a specific, diagnosable condition**, not a vague feeling. It means haemoglobin has dropped below a measurable threshold, usually alongside low ferritin, the protein that reflects the body's stored iron. Without that confirmation, starting iron is a guess, and in this particular case, a guess that can backfire.

### Who is genuinely at higher risk after 40?

A few groups do have a real, elevated risk of iron deficiency in this age range:

- **Women still menstruating, especially with heavy or prolonged periods**, lose iron every cycle and may not fully replace it through diet alone
- **People with largely vegetarian diets low in iron-rich foods**, since plant-based (non-haem) iron is absorbed less efficiently than iron from meat and fish, though pairing iron-rich foods with vitamin C sources like citrus or amla improves absorption meaningfully
- **Anyone with undiagnosed gut conditions affecting absorption**, such as coeliac disease or chronic gastritis, or anyone on long-term acid-reducing medication, which can reduce iron absorption
- **People with any source of ongoing, even minor, blood loss**, including certain gut conditions that deserve medical evaluation rather than assumption

## What are the real risks of taking iron you don't need?

This is the part that gets skipped in casual conversations about supplements. **Iron is not like most vitamins, where excess is simply passed out of the body.** The body has limited ability to excrete extra iron, so unnecessary supplementation, especially over months or years, can lead to iron building up in the liver, heart, and other organs.

- Short-term, unnecessary iron commonly causes constipation, stomach cramping, nausea, and dark stools, which are uncomfortable but usually reversible once stopped
- Longer-term, in people who are not actually deficient, it can contribute to oxidative stress and, in rarer cases, organ damage over years
- Men and postmenopausal women are at particular risk of quietly accumulating excess iron, since they lose far less of it through natural routes than menstruating women do
- A small proportion of people carry a genetic condition called haemochromatosis, which causes the body to absorb far more iron than it needs, making unsupervised supplementation genuinely dangerous for them

## What should a sensible approach actually look like?

**The right first step is always a simple blood test, not a tablet.** A complete blood count along with a serum ferritin test gives your doctor a much clearer picture than symptoms alone ever could, and these tests are widely available and reasonably affordable across India, including at many government diagnostic centres.

| Step | What it involves |
|---|---|
| 1. Get tested | Haemoglobin and serum ferritin, at minimum, before starting anything |
| 2. Identify the cause | Diet, menstrual blood loss, absorption issues, or another source should be discussed, not assumed |
| 3. Treat the confirmed deficiency | Dose, form, and duration should be set by your doctor, based on how low your levels actually are |
| 4. Recheck | Follow-up testing after a few months confirms whether levels have actually improved, and tells your doctor when to stop |
| 5. Reassess diet | Iron-rich foods and better absorption habits, like pairing with vitamin C, help maintain levels long after supplementation ends |

**If you do have confirmed deficiency, food still matters alongside any supplement your doctor prescribes.** Dark leafy greens, legumes, jaggery, dried fruits, and lean meats or fish for those who eat them, eaten alongside a vitamin C source such as lemon, tomato, or amla, meaningfully improve how much iron your body actually absorbs from a meal.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I just try an iron tonic for a few weeks to see if I feel better?**
This is not a reliable way to judge anything, since any improvement could be a placebo effect or simple rest, and you would have no way of knowing if you were pushing iron levels higher than they should go without a test. A blood test before and after is a far more honest way to know if iron was ever actually the problem.

**I feel constantly tired after 40. Does that mean I'm iron deficient?**
Not necessarily. Fatigue after 40 has many common causes, including poor sleep quality, thyroid changes, low vitamin D or B12, depression, and simply carrying too much on your plate day to day. Iron deficiency is one possibility among several, which is exactly why testing rather than guessing matters.

**Is it safe to take a multivitamin that contains iron if I'm not sure I need it?**
Most standard multivitamins contain a modest amount of iron that is unlikely to cause harm for an average healthy adult over the short term, but if you are a man or a postmenopausal woman taking one daily for years, it is still worth mentioning to your doctor at a routine checkup, particularly if you have any family history of iron overload conditions.

**What if my ferritin is low but my haemoglobin is normal?**
This pattern, sometimes called depleted iron stores without full anaemia, is still worth discussing with your doctor, since it can explain fatigue even before haemoglobin drops, and catching it early may mean a shorter, simpler course of treatment.

Iron deficiency after 40 is real for some people and worth taking seriously when it is confirmed. But the instinct to reach for an iron tablet whenever energy dips is exactly the habit worth resisting, since this is one supplement where the harm from taking it unnecessarily is just as real as the benefit from taking it when you genuinely need it. A simple blood test is the difference between the two.
