Ask a lifelong dog owner why they love having a pet and you will get an answer about companionship long before you get one about health. But a genuine, growing body of research has looked specifically at whether owning a pet, particularly a dog, is associated with better health outcomes as people age, and the findings are more interesting than a simple yes or no.

This is not a case for getting a pet purely as a health intervention, that would miss the point of what a pet actually is. But for people who are already considering it, or wondering whether their existing companion is doing more for them than they realised, the evidence is worth a closer look.

## What does the research actually say about pets and longevity?

**Multiple large observational studies, including a well-known analysis from Sweden covering millions of people, have found that dog ownership in particular is associated with lower rates of cardiovascular death and, in some studies, lower all-cause mortality**, especially among people living alone. These are observational studies, meaning they show an association rather than definitive proof that owning a pet directly causes a longer life, since people who choose to get a dog may also differ in other health-relevant ways from people who do not. Even accounting for that limitation, the consistency of the finding across multiple large studies in different countries makes it a genuinely interesting pattern, not a fluke.

### Why dogs specifically, more than other pets

**Dogs require regular walks, which appears to be a meaningful part of why dog ownership shows a stronger association with physical activity and cardiovascular benefit than pet ownership in general.** Cat ownership and other pets show more modest and less consistent associations with physical health outcomes in the research, though they are still linked to companionship and stress-related benefits in several studies. This suggests the mechanism is less about the specific animal and more about what having that animal actually requires of the owner's daily routine.

## Does having a pet actually reduce loneliness?

**Yes, this is one of the more consistently supported findings**, particularly for older adults living alone, a group with genuinely elevated health risks tied to social isolation. A pet provides routine, a sense of purpose and responsibility, and in the case of dogs, frequently leads to more casual social interaction during walks, whether with neighbours or other dog owners. This matters because loneliness and social isolation are themselves linked in research to worse cardiovascular health, cognitive decline, and higher mortality, making this an area where a pet's companionship benefit and its physical activity benefit likely reinforce each other.

## Is getting a pet a reasonable choice for every older adult?

**Not automatically, and this is where the honest caveats matter.** A pet, particularly a dog, is a real, ongoing commitment involving cost, physical care, and for larger or more active breeds, a level of physical activity that may not suit everyone's mobility or health situation. For someone who travels frequently, has significant mobility limitations, or genuinely does not want the daily responsibility, a pet is not the right health intervention, and other routes to physical activity and social connection are entirely reasonable alternatives. For those who do want a pet and can realistically care for one, choosing based on lifestyle fit, a lower-energy breed or an adult rather than a puppy, for instance, matters more than choosing for any presumed health benefit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do the health benefits apply to cats as well as dogs?**
Cat ownership shows more modest and less consistent associations with physical health benefits compared to dog ownership, though companionship and stress-related benefits appear in some research for cat owners too. The activity benefit specifically seems tied to the regular walking that dog ownership requires.

**Is it too late to get a pet's benefits if I am already in my 60s or 70s?**
No, the research does not suggest an age cutoff for benefiting from pet companionship, though practical considerations, such as choosing a pet whose care needs realistically match your own physical capacity and household situation, matter more at any age.

**What if I cannot commit to a pet but still want the social connection benefit?**
Volunteering at a local animal shelter, spending time with a family member's or neighbour's pet, or joining community walking groups are reasonable ways to get some of the companionship and routine benefits without full-time pet ownership.

**Can pet ownership genuinely help with anxiety or low mood?**
Several studies suggest pets are associated with lower stress and improved mood for many owners, likely through routine, companionship, and in some cases the physical act of petting an animal, which has been linked to modest reductions in stress hormones. This is a genuine benefit for many people, though it should not replace proper care for a diagnosed mental health condition.

The evidence does not prove that a pet adds years to your life on its own. What it does suggest, fairly consistently, is that the routine, movement, and companionship that come with caring for an animal are genuinely good for the things that matter for a long, healthy life, which may be reason enough.
