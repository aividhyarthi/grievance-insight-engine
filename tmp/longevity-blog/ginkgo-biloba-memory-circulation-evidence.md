Ginkgo biloba extract sits on pharmacy shelves across India under a handful of generic herbal-supplement labels, usually marketed for memory, concentration, and "poor circulation." It is one of the oldest surviving tree species on earth, and extracts of its leaves have been studied in more clinical trials than most herbal supplements sold today. That research history is exactly what makes ginkgo worth examining carefully rather than dismissing or endorsing on reputation alone.

This piece looks at what ginkgo biloba is actually used for, what clinical trials have found for memory and circulation specifically, and what matters if you are considering it after 40.

## What is ginkgo biloba actually supposed to do?

Ginkgo extract is typically standardised to contain specific compounds called flavone glycosides and terpene lactones, which researchers believe may improve blood flow in small vessels and act as antioxidants. **The traditional and modern marketing case for ginkgo rests on two separate claims: that it improves circulation, particularly in the legs and brain, and that this improved circulation translates into better memory and cognitive function.** These are related ideas but need to be evaluated as two separate questions, because the evidence for each is quite different.

## Does ginkgo actually help with memory and cognitive decline?

This is where the evidence is weakest, despite being the reason most people buy it.

**Large, well-designed trials in healthy older adults and in people with mild cognitive decline have generally not shown a clear, clinically meaningful benefit from ginkgo for memory or overall cognitive function.** One of the most cited large trials, which followed thousands of older adults over several years, found that ginkgo did not reduce the rate of progression to dementia compared to placebo. Smaller studies have occasionally reported modest benefits on specific cognitive tests, but these results have not been consistently replicated, and systematic reviews pooling the available trials have generally concluded the evidence is too inconsistent to recommend ginkgo as an effective treatment for preventing memory decline or dementia.

### Where the evidence is a little stronger

Ginkgo has shown more consistent, though still modest, benefit in people already diagnosed with dementia for easing some associated symptoms, such as mood and daily functioning, in certain trials, though results here too remain mixed across studies and are not considered a substitute for medically recognised dementia treatment and care planning.

## What about circulation specifically?

This is ginkgo's best-supported use. **For intermittent claudication, a condition where narrowed leg arteries cause pain or cramping during walking, several clinical trials and at least one meta-analysis have found that ginkgo produces a modest improvement in pain-free walking distance compared to placebo.** The effect size is generally small and less dramatic than supervised exercise therapy or standard medical treatment for the same condition, but it is one of the more consistently positive findings in the ginkgo research literature.

| Use being considered | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| Leg pain from poor peripheral circulation (claudication) | Modest, fairly consistent improvement across several trials |
| Preventing age-related memory decline in healthy adults | Weak and inconsistent; large trials have generally not shown a clear benefit |
| Slowing progression of diagnosed dementia | Mixed results on secondary symptoms; not a recognised primary treatment |
| Tinnitus (ringing in the ears) | Evidence does not support a meaningful benefit in most trials |
| General "brain fog" or everyday concentration | Essentially no reliable trial evidence to support this common marketing claim |

## What are the actual safety concerns?

Ginkgo is generally well tolerated by most healthy adults in typical doses, with mild headache or stomach upset being the most commonly reported side effects. **The more important safety issue is its genuine effect on blood clotting, since ginkgo has been shown to have mild antiplatelet activity, meaning it can increase bleeding risk, particularly when combined with blood-thinning medicines such as those prescribed after a stroke, heart attack, or for atrial fibrillation.**

**India's herbal supplement market is loosely regulated when it comes to verifying actual content against the label, so quality and active-compound concentration can vary meaningfully between brands.** Choosing a product from an established pharmacy rather than an unverified online seller, and discussing the specific brand and dose with your doctor, reduces this risk somewhat, though it does not eliminate the underlying regulatory gap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can ginkgo biloba prevent dementia?**
Current evidence does not support this. Large trials in older adults have not shown that ginkgo reduces the rate of progression to dementia compared to placebo, so it should not be relied on as a prevention strategy.

**Is ginkgo biloba safe to take with blood pressure medication?**
Ginkgo does not have a well-documented major interaction with most blood pressure medicines specifically, but its blood-thinning effect means you should still tell your doctor about all medicines and supplements you take together, since individual interactions can vary.

**How long does ginkgo take to show an effect on circulation symptoms?**
In the trials that showed benefit for leg pain from poor circulation, improvements were generally measured over several weeks to a few months of consistent use, not days.

**Should I stop taking ginkgo before surgery?**
Many doctors recommend stopping ginkgo and similar supplements with blood-thinning effects one to two weeks before a planned surgical or dental procedure, but you should confirm the exact timing with your own doctor or surgeon rather than guessing.

Ginkgo biloba is a genuinely well-studied supplement, which is precisely what makes its mixed results worth taking seriously rather than ignoring. If circulation symptoms or memory concerns are bothering you enough to consider a supplement, a conversation with your doctor about what is actually driving the symptom will usually serve you better than the bottle alone.
