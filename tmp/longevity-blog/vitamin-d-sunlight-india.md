It sounds like it should not be possible. India gets abundant sunlight across nearly the entire country for most of the year, and sunlight on skin is how the body makes most of its vitamin D. Yet multiple studies across Indian cities, spanning all age groups and both sexes, have found vitamin D deficiency in a large share of the population, in some surveys well over half. This mismatch between sunlight availability and actual vitamin D levels is not a contradiction, it reflects how modern urban life, indoor-heavy routines, and skin biology interact in ways that abundant sun alone cannot fix. Understanding why matters, because vitamin D plays a meaningful role in bone health, immune function, and, based on a growing body of research, broader healthy ageing.

## Why Living in a Sunny Country Does Not Guarantee Enough Vitamin D

The core issue is that sunlight exposure, not sunlight availability, is what produces vitamin D, and most people simply do not get direct sun on enough exposed skin for long enough. Urban working life keeps most adults indoors for the bulk of daylight hours, commuting in covered vehicles, working in offices, and spending leisure time indoors as well. When people are outside, cultural norms around modest dress, sun avoidance for skin tone reasons, and widespread use of sunscreen, all reasonable choices for other reasons, further reduce the skin surface area exposed to direct sunlight.

Air pollution in many Indian cities also filters out a portion of the ultraviolet B rays specifically responsible for vitamin D synthesis in skin, meaning even time spent outdoors in heavily polluted air produces less vitamin D than the same exposure would in cleaner air. And darker skin, which has more melanin acting as a natural sunscreen, requires meaningfully longer sun exposure to produce the same amount of vitamin D as lighter skin, a factor that applies to a large share of the Indian population and is rarely accounted for in general sun exposure advice.

## Why Vitamin D Matters for Long-Term Health

Vitamin D's best-established role is in calcium absorption and bone health, deficiency is linked to weaker bones, higher fracture risk, and conditions like osteomalacia and rickets in severe cases. This matters increasingly with age, since bone density naturally declines over the decades and adequate vitamin D is one factor supporting bone strength alongside weight-bearing exercise and adequate calcium and protein intake.

Beyond bone health, vitamin D receptors exist throughout the body, including in immune cells, and research links deficiency to higher rates of respiratory infections and, in some studies, associations with mood, muscle strength, and metabolic health, though these associations are generally less firmly established than the bone health connection and are still being actively researched. It is worth being cautious here, vitamin D has become something of a wellness buzzword with claims that outpace the evidence, and correcting a genuine deficiency is a more defensible goal than chasing very high supplemental doses in someone who is not actually deficient.

## What Actually Helps, Beyond Just Going Outside

Sensible sun exposure remains a reasonable first step, getting some direct sunlight on arms, legs, or back for a portion of the day, ideally earlier or later when the sun is less intense, rather than only brief exposure during a commute. But for many people, particularly those with predominantly indoor jobs, darker skin, or living in heavily polluted cities, sun exposure alone may not be enough to correct an existing deficiency, and this is where the science gets more individual rather than one-size-fits-all.

Dietary sources help but are limited, fatty fish, egg yolks, and fortified foods contain some vitamin D, though it is difficult to meet daily needs through diet alone in most Indian eating patterns. If you suspect a deficiency, based on fatigue, bone or muscle pain, or simply because you spend most of your day indoors, a simple blood test can confirm your actual levels rather than guessing. A doctor can then advise on whether supplementation is appropriate and at what dose, since inappropriate high-dose supplementation without testing carries its own risks and is not something to self-prescribe based on general advice.

## A Realistic Approach for Most Indian Adults

For most people, a reasonable approach combines a few things, some intentional sun exposure most days when practical, being mindful that a brief car-to-office commute does not count as meaningful sun exposure, including vitamin D-containing foods where possible, and getting a blood test if you have risk factors like predominantly indoor work, limited sun exposure, or symptoms that could relate to deficiency. This is a more useful framework than either assuming that living in a sunny country automatically protects you, or reflexively starting high-dose supplements without knowing your actual levels.

India's sunlight is abundant, but abundance does not automatically translate into what your body needs when daily life keeps most of that sunlight at arm's length. Closing that gap does not require anything dramatic, a bit more intentional time outdoors, some awareness of the factors working against you, and a blood test if you are unsure, are enough to know where you actually stand and what, if anything, needs correcting.
