Walk into almost any pharmacy or wellness store in India today and you will likely find shilajit, sold as a resin, a powder, or capsules, promising more energy, better stamina, and slower ageing. It is one of Ayurveda's oldest rasayanas (rejuvenative substances), described in classical texts as exuding from Himalayan rock crevices over centuries. It is also, right now, one of the fastest-growing supplement categories both in India and internationally.

That combination, ancient tradition plus a booming modern market, is exactly where this article tries to be useful. What is shilajit actually made of, what does current research say about it, and what should a cautious buyer in India actually check before trying it?

## What exactly is shilajit?

**Shilajit forms over long periods in high-altitude rock formations, largely in the Himalayas, from the slow decomposition of plant matter compressed under mineral-rich rock.** The result is a tar-like substance containing humic and fulvic acids, along with trace minerals and a compound called dibenzo-alpha-pyrones. In classical Ayurvedic texts, it is classified as a rasayana, a category of substances believed to support vitality, resilience, and healthy ageing when used over time, generally alongside diet, routine, and other lifestyle practices, not as a standalone cure.

Today's commercial shilajit is almost never gathered directly from a rock face. Most of what reaches Indian shelves is processed, purified, and standardised to some degree, though the extent of that processing varies enormously between brands and is not something a buyer can judge from the label alone.

## What does current research actually show?

**The honest answer is: promising in small studies, not yet established.** A handful of human trials, most involving a few dozen participants over eight to twelve weeks, have looked at purified shilajit and reported modest improvements in self-reported fatigue and some markers linked to testosterone in middle-aged men. Animal and cell-based research has explored possible effects on mitochondrial function and antioxidant activity, which is part of why it gets framed as a "longevity" compound in marketing copy.

### Where the evidence is weakest

None of this amounts to proof that shilajit slows ageing, reverses age-related decline, or treats any diagnosed condition. Trials have been small, short, often industry-funded, and have not been replicated at a scale that would satisfy a cautious reading of the evidence. There is no good human data on long-term safety at the doses typically sold, and no high-quality evidence for many of the broader claims (cognitive sharpness, skin ageing, general "detox") attached to it in retail marketing.

## Why purity is the real risk, not the herb itself

**The most serious concern with shilajit in India has less to do with the substance's traditional use and more to do with what actually ends up in the bottle.** Because shilajit originates from rock and soil, it can carry lead, arsenic, mercury, or other heavy metals if it is not properly purified and tested, a concern flagged repeatedly in independent lab analyses of commercially sold shilajit products worldwide. India's supplement market is loosely regulated: many products reach shelves without consistent third-party heavy-metal testing, and label claims about purity are not independently verified before sale.

## Who should be especially cautious

People with chronic kidney disease should avoid shilajit, since some of its mineral and acid content can place extra load on compromised kidneys. People with hereditary haemochromatosis or other iron-overload conditions should also steer clear, since shilajit can contain iron. Pregnant or breastfeeding women, and anyone on blood pressure, diabetes, or blood-thinning medication, should not start it without first checking with a doctor, since interaction data is essentially absent.

## The bottom line for someone considering it

**Shilajit sits in a familiar place for many Ayurvedic substances: genuinely interesting traditional use, early but incomplete modern evidence, and a market that has grown faster than regulation has kept up with.** If you are curious about trying it, the more useful conversation is not "does this work" but "is this specific product tested, and is it right for my health situation", and that conversation is best had with a doctor or a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner who knows your full medical history, not a product listing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is shilajit safe to take every day?**
There is no strong long-term human safety data on daily use at commercial doses. Traditional use in Ayurveda tends to be periodic and supervised rather than an indefinite daily habit, and a qualified practitioner would typically tailor use to the individual rather than recommend open-ended daily intake.

**Can shilajit replace my regular medication?**
No. Shilajit has never been shown to treat any diagnosed medical condition, and it should never be used as a substitute for prescribed medication for diabetes, blood pressure, thyroid disease, or anything else. Stopping or changing prescribed treatment without medical advice can be genuinely dangerous.

**Why does purity matter so much if the substance itself is traditional?**
Traditional use assumed a different supply chain, smaller scale, and often direct knowledge of the source. Today's mass-market shilajit travels through processing and packaging steps that are not independently verified for every brand, which is why heavy-metal testing matters regardless of how "natural" the product claims to be.

**Does Ayurveda itself recommend shilajit the way it is marketed today?**
Classical Ayurvedic use of shilajit is typically individualised, often combined with other substances, and supervised by a practitioner who adjusts it to the person's constitution and condition. The generic, one-size-fits-all capsule sold for general energy is a modern commercial adaptation, not a direct reflection of classical practice.

Shilajit is a genuinely old and interesting substance, but "ancient" does not automatically mean "evidence-based" or "safe at any dose for anyone." If you want to try it, start with a conversation with your doctor and look for a product that can actually show you its testing, not just its marketing.
