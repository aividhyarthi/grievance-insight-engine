Vitamin C has one of the oldest, most solid reputations in nutrition, and also one of the most exaggerated. Most people know it for fighting off colds, but after 40, a different conversation starts: whether it genuinely does anything for sagging skin, wrinkle depth, or the collagen loss that becomes more noticeable this decade. This article separates what vitamin C's role in the body actually is from the marketing built around it.

## What does vitamin C actually do in the body, beyond immunity?

**Vitamin C's best-established role in skin and connective tissue is as a required cofactor for collagen synthesis.** Your body cannot build stable collagen, the structural protein that keeps skin firm and joints supported, without adequate vitamin C present. This is not a marginal or theoretical function, it is a well-established biochemical requirement, which is why severe vitamin C deficiency, scurvy, causes visibly poor wound healing, bleeding gums, and weakened connective tissue. Beyond collagen, vitamin C is also a water-soluble antioxidant that helps neutralise the oxidative stress associated with sun exposure and pollution, both significant factors in visible skin ageing.

### Why this gets conflated with "more is better"

**The confusion starts because a genuinely essential nutrient gets marketed as though extra amounts produce extra benefit, which is not how it works.** Once your body's needs are met, through diet or a modest supplement, additional vitamin C is largely excreted rather than stored or put to further use. This is a common pattern across many vitamins: adequacy matters enormously, but exceeding it does not translate into a proportional improvement in skin firmness or wrinkle reduction.

## Do most Indian adults over 40 actually need a vitamin C supplement?

**For most people eating a reasonably varied diet, the answer is no.** India has genuinely excellent dietary sources of vitamin C: amla, guava, citrus fruits, bell peppers, and many green leafy vegetables all provide meaningful amounts, often exceeding what a basic supplement would add. People who may genuinely fall short include those with very restricted diets, heavy smokers, whose bodies use up vitamin C faster, people recovering from surgery or significant wounds, and those with malabsorption conditions. For everyone else, a supplement is unlikely to add visible benefit beyond what a varied diet already provides.

## What does the research actually say about vitamin C and visible skin ageing?

Oral vitamin C supplementation studies for skin ageing specifically are limited in number and often small, with modest, inconsistent findings on fine lines and skin texture. Topical vitamin C, applied directly to skin as a serum, has a somewhat stronger evidence base for improving skin brightness and modestly reducing the appearance of fine lines, likely because it delivers a locally higher concentration directly to the skin layer where collagen is synthesised, bypassing digestion and absorption limits entirely.

| Guava | High | Widely available and inexpensive across most of the year |

## What should you actually check before taking a vitamin C supplement?

**If your doctor confirms a genuine gap, a basic supplement is a reasonable, low-risk option, but the Indian supplement market is loosely regulated, and quality, purity, and even the actual stated content can vary meaningfully between brands.** Look for products that disclose their manufacturing standards and third-party testing where available, avoid unusually high-dose formulations marketed with dramatic anti-ageing claims, and always mention any supplement you take to your doctor, particularly if you are on blood thinners or have a history of kidney stones, since very high vitamin C intake has been linked to increased stone risk in susceptible individuals.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can vitamin C supplements reverse wrinkles or sagging skin after 40?**
No. Vitamin C supports your body's own collagen production and offers modest antioxidant benefit, but it does not reverse existing wrinkles or significant skin laxity. Sun protection, adequate protein, and not smoking remain far more influential for long-term skin ageing than any single supplement.

**Is it better to get vitamin C from food or from a supplement?**
For most people, food is the better source, since fruits and vegetables also provide fibre, other antioxidants, and no risk of excess intake. A supplement is reasonable when a doctor identifies a genuine dietary gap, not as a routine addition to an already adequate diet.

**Does cooking destroy the vitamin C in vegetables?**
Vitamin C is sensitive to heat and breaks down somewhat during cooking, especially prolonged boiling. Light steaming, stir-frying briefly, or eating some sources raw, like amla or citrus fruit, helps preserve more of the vitamin.

**Are vitamin C serums for skin actually worth using alongside a healthy diet?**
Topical vitamin C serums have reasonable evidence for modestly improving skin brightness and fine lines, and work through a different pathway than dietary intake, so the two are not interchangeable. They remain a cosmetic addition, not a substitute for sun protection, which has a far stronger evidence base for preventing skin ageing in the first place.

Vitamin C deserves its good reputation, but the honest picture is narrower than the marketing suggests: essential when you are short of it, genuinely useful at adequate levels, and not a dramatic anti-ageing solution once your needs are already met.
