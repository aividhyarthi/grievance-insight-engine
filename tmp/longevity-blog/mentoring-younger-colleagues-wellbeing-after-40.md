Somewhere in your 40s and 50s, workplaces start expecting something different from you. You are no longer the person being trained, and increasingly, you are the one younger colleagues turn to for guidance, whether or not anyone has formally called it mentoring. This shift is usually discussed purely in terms of what it does for the younger person's career. Less discussed is what it actually does for you, and the research on this is more interesting than it first appears.

## Why does guiding others matter for your own wellbeing?

**Psychologist Erik Erikson described a specific stage of adult psychological development centred on what he called generativity versus stagnation.** In simple terms, midlife brings a genuine psychological need to contribute to something beyond yourself, whether that is raising children, building something lasting, or guiding the next generation of colleagues. People who find healthy outlets for this drive tend to report greater life satisfaction, while those who feel stuck or without this kind of contribution often describe a sense of stagnation, even when their career is otherwise going well.

Mentoring at work taps directly into this need. **Several workplace studies have found that mentors report benefits including renewed enthusiasm for their own work, a clearer sense of their accumulated expertise's value, and improved relationships across the organisation,** benefits that are sometimes stronger for the mentor than for the person being mentored, who may simply be seeking practical career advice.

## Does this actually show up as a health benefit, or just a feel-good idea?

**The link is more concrete than it might sound.** A sense of purpose, which mentoring can meaningfully support, is associated in longitudinal research with better self-reported health, lower rates of depression, and in some studies, lower mortality risk over time. This does not mean mentoring alone extends your life, but it fits a broader, well-established pattern: people who feel they are contributing something meaningful tend to report better psychological and physical health outcomes than people who do not, independent of income or job title.

### What if your workplace does not have a formal mentoring programme?

**Most meaningful mentoring happens informally, not through official programmes.** It can be as simple as regularly making time for a newer colleague's questions, offering honest feedback when someone asks for it, or occasionally pulling a junior team member into a meeting specifically to build their visibility and confidence. Formal programmes help structure the relationship, but their absence is not a real barrier to mentoring meaningfully.

## What does mentoring look like in Indian workplaces specifically?

**Many Indian workplaces are genuinely multigenerational, often spanning three active generations on the same floor, which creates a distinctive two-way dynamic.** While a senior colleague might mentor a younger one on navigating office politics, client relationships, or domain expertise built over decades, the same younger colleague is often the one explaining a new software tool, an evolving industry trend, or a shift in client expectations around technology. This reverse mentoring is increasingly recognised as valuable in its own right, and embracing it, rather than resisting it as a loss of authority, tends to make the overall relationship work better for both people.

## Formal versus informal mentoring

| Factor | Formal mentoring programme | Informal mentoring |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Set meetings, often matched by HR | Flexible, as needed |
| Ease of starting | Depends on organisation offering one | Can start anytime, with anyone |
| Accountability | Built in through programme tracking | Depends on both people's initiative |
| Best suited for | Larger organisations with HR support | Any workplace, including small businesses and family firms |
| Risk of fading out | Lower, due to structure | Higher, needs deliberate consistency |

## When does mentoring become a burden instead of a benefit?

**Mentoring should add meaning to your work, not quietly become an unpaid second job.** Some people, particularly those seen as naturally generous with their time, end up repeatedly mentoring without any acknowledgement, promotion consideration, or even basic reciprocity, which can lead to resentment and burnout rather than the wellbeing benefits described above. Setting reasonable boundaries, being selective about how many people you actively mentor at once, and ensuring the relationship is not entirely one-directional all help keep mentoring sustainable rather than draining.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a formal title to mentor someone at work?**
No. Most valuable mentoring happens informally, through consistent, honest guidance offered over time, regardless of whether a company programme or title is attached to it.

**Does mentoring really benefit the mentor as much as research suggests?**
Multiple workplace studies report that mentors frequently describe benefits like renewed motivation, a clearer sense of their own expertise, and greater job satisfaction, sometimes rating these benefits as highly as the mentees do. It is not purely a one-way act of generosity.

**What if I feel like I have nothing valuable to offer a younger colleague?**
This feeling is common and usually inaccurate. Experience accumulated over years, including mistakes you have learned from, is often exactly what a newer colleague needs most, even if it does not feel like specialised expertise to you.

**How do I avoid being taken advantage of as the "go-to" person for advice?**
Set a few simple boundaries, such as limiting how many people you actively mentor at once, scheduling specific times rather than being available for constant interruptions, and being willing to say when you genuinely do not have the bandwidth for a request.

Guiding others at work is not just a professional nicety expected of senior staff. For many people, it is a genuine source of meaning in midlife, one that research suggests benefits the mentor's own wellbeing nearly as much as it helps the person being mentored.
