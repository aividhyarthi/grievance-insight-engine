Vitamin D has been the breakout supplement star in India for years, but increasingly it is showing up on pharmacy shelves paired with something less familiar: vitamin K2. The pitch sounds appealing and specific, that K2 helps direct calcium to your bones instead of your arteries, which is a more precise-sounding claim than most supplement marketing manages. The real question is whether the evidence behind it is as solid as the pitch.

This article looks at what vitamin K2 actually is, what it is genuinely different from vitamin K1, what the research supports for bone and heart health, and where most people can realistically get it from food before considering a supplement at all.

## What is vitamin K2, and how is it different from vitamin K1?

**Vitamin K is actually a family of related compounds, not one single nutrient**, and K1 and K2 are its two main forms. Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) is abundant in leafy green vegetables and is the form most people get easily through a typical diet. Vitamin K2 (menaquinone) is found in smaller amounts in animal products and fermented foods, and the body can also convert some K1 into K2, though this conversion is limited and varies between individuals.

**Both forms activate proteins involved in blood clotting**, which is the role vitamin K has been known for longest. Vitamin K2 has drawn separate attention because it also activates specific proteins involved in calcium regulation: one that helps bind calcium into bone matrix, and another that helps prevent calcium from depositing in soft tissue such as blood vessel walls. This is the biological basis for the "directs calcium to bones, not arteries" claim you may have seen in marketing.

### Why does this matter more after 40?

**Bone density naturally declines with age, and arterial stiffening tends to increase.** Both processes involve calcium handling in the body, which is why researchers became interested in whether a nutrient involved in calcium regulation might meaningfully influence either outcome. It is a genuinely reasonable hypothesis, which is different from it being a proven intervention.

## What does the evidence actually show for bone health?

**Several studies, mostly from Japan where a fermented soybean dish called natto provides very high natural K2 intake, have found associations between higher vitamin K2 levels and better bone density and fewer fractures.** Some clinical trials using high-dose K2 supplements in postmenopausal women have shown modest improvements in markers of bone health. However, the trial quality and sizes vary considerably, several studies come from manufacturers of K2 supplements, and results have not been consistently replicated outside these specific populations and dietary contexts.

**For Indian readers, an important caveat is that natto is not a food most people eat**, so the strongest evidence base comes from a dietary pattern quite different from a typical Indian diet. This does not invalidate the research, but it does mean the findings may not transfer cleanly to someone whose baseline K2 intake and diet look completely different.

## What about the heart and arterial calcium claims?

**A smaller number of studies have looked at vitamin K2 and arterial calcification**, the buildup of calcium deposits in blood vessel walls that contributes to arterial stiffening. Some observational studies suggest people with higher K2 intake have less arterial calcium buildup over time, and a few small trials using supplements have shown modest improvements in markers of arterial stiffness. This research area is younger and smaller than the bone health research, and it has not yet produced the kind of large, long-term outcome trials, actual heart attack or stroke reduction, that would make this a settled recommendation.

## Where do you get vitamin K2 from food in an Indian diet?

| Source | Vitamin K form | Notes for Indian readers |
|---|---|---|
| Leafy greens (spinach, methi, amaranth) | Mostly K1 | Easily included in most regional diets; supports overall vitamin K status |
| Curd and fermented dairy | Some K2 | Produced by fermentation bacteria, though amounts are modest compared to natto |
| Egg yolk and chicken | Small amounts of K2 | Relevant for non-vegetarian eaters |
| Ghee and full-fat dairy | Small amounts of K2 | One more reason moderate ghee use is not automatically harmful, as covered in our piece on cooking oils |
| Natto (fermented soybean) | Very high K2 | The food behind most strong research, but not part of typical Indian eating |

**Most people following a reasonably varied Indian diet, with regular leafy vegetables, some dairy, and eggs or meat for non-vegetarians, are unlikely to be severely deficient in vitamin K overall**, even without natto. A true deficiency is uncommon outside specific medical conditions affecting fat absorption, since vitamin K is fat-soluble.

## Who should actually consider a supplement, and who should be careful?

**The clearest case for discussing a K2 supplement with your doctor is if you already have diagnosed osteoporosis or osteopenia and are on long-term calcium and vitamin D supplementation**, since some doctors consider it a reasonable addition in this specific situation. Outside this, routine supplementation for a generally healthy adult is not currently backed by strong enough evidence to call it necessary.

**One serious safety point cannot be skipped: vitamin K, in either form, interferes with blood-thinning medications such as warfarin**, because these medicines work by blocking vitamin K's clotting function. Taking a vitamin K2 supplement without telling your doctor if you are on such medication can meaningfully change how well the medicine controls your blood clotting, with real risk either way. This is not a minor interaction, and it is one of the clearest reasons self-prescribing any vitamin K supplement is a bad idea.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I take vitamin K2 along with my vitamin D supplement?**
Not automatically. The pairing is marketed heavily, but routine combined supplementation is not supported by strong evidence for most healthy adults. Ask your doctor whether it is relevant to your specific bone health situation rather than assuming the pairing is always necessary.

**Can I get enough vitamin K2 without eating natto?**
You can get reasonable overall vitamin K status from leafy greens, curd, eggs, and moderate dairy and ghee intake, even though these provide less K2 specifically than natto. True deficiency in a varied diet is uncommon.

**Is vitamin K2 dangerous for people on blood thinners?**
It can meaningfully interfere with how well blood-thinning medication like warfarin works. If you are on any such medication, do not start a vitamin K2 supplement without your doctor's explicit knowledge and guidance.

**Does India's supplement market make quality a concern here too?**
Yes. Like other supplements, vitamin K2 products sold in India are not tightly regulated for purity and actual content, so buying from an established pharmacy and discussing the specific product with your doctor is more reliable than choosing based on online marketing alone.

Vitamin K2 has a genuinely interesting biological story behind it, and the calcium-regulation research is a real and active area of science, not pure marketing invention. What it is not yet, is a proven, necessary supplement for most healthy adults over 40. For the majority of people, a varied Indian diet covers baseline vitamin K needs reasonably well, and a supplement decision is best made with a doctor who knows your bone health status and your full medication list, not from a pharmacy counter recommendation alone.
