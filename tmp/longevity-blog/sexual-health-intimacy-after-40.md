It is one of the least talked-about parts of getting older in Indian households, even between couples who are otherwise comfortable discussing everything else. Desire, frequency, and physical ease around sex commonly shift after 40, for both men and women, for reasons that are largely biological rather than a sign that something has gone wrong in the relationship. This article looks at what actually changes, why, and what tends to genuinely help.

## Why does this change so much after 40, for both men and women?

**Hormones shift gradually for everyone past midlife**, not only during menopause. Testosterone, which plays a role in desire for both sexes, declines gradually in men from around the mid-30s onward, and oestrogen changes around perimenopause and menopause affect women's desire and physical comfort during sex. These are normal, expected biological shifts, not signs of a problem on their own.

**Blood flow matters as much as hormones.** Sexual arousal and function in both sexes depend heavily on healthy blood vessels and circulation. This is part of why conditions like high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and diabetes, all more common after 40, are so closely linked to sexual difficulties. In a meaningful number of cases, a change in sexual function is actually an early signal of a broader cardiovascular issue worth checking, which is one more reason not to simply ignore it.

## Could it be the medicines, not the ageing?

This is an underappreciated piece of the picture. **Several very commonly prescribed medicines can affect desire or physical function as a side effect**, including some blood pressure medicines, some antidepressants, and certain other common prescriptions. This does not mean stopping a needed medicine; it means mentioning the side effect to the prescribing doctor, who can often adjust the dose, timing, or switch to an alternative with less of this particular effect. This is part of why a broader medication review matters after 40, something this vertical has covered separately in the context of polypharmacy.

### Stress, sleep, and everyday life

Chronic stress, poor sleep, and simple exhaustion from running a household or caring for ageing parents and growing children at the same time (the so-called sandwich generation squeeze) all reduce desire independent of any hormonal or medical factor. For many couples, this is a bigger contributor than anything physical, and it is also the most fixable.

## What actually helps, practically?

| Common cause | What typically helps |
|---|---|
| Hormonal changes (both sexes) | Discuss with a doctor; several evidence-based options exist depending on the specific symptoms |
| Medication side effects | Ask the prescribing doctor about alternatives or timing changes, never stop a medicine on your own |
| Vaginal dryness or discomfort during sex | A doctor can suggest safe, appropriate options; this is a common and treatable issue, not something to simply tolerate |
| Erectile changes in men | A doctor can assess cardiovascular and other contributing causes before suggesting treatment |
| Stress, fatigue, and mismatched schedules | Protecting unhurried time together, even briefly, and open conversation about pressure points at home |
| Body image concerns after physical changes | Conversations with a partner, and sometimes a counsellor, tend to help more than anyone realises until they try it |

## Why does talking about it feel so hard, even for comfortable couples?

**Many Indian couples were never given language for this growing up**, and the discomfort can outlast the marriage itself, persisting decades in. But research on long-term relationships consistently finds that couples who can talk openly about changes in intimacy, rather than avoiding the topic or quietly assuming the worst about what their partner's reduced interest means, report more satisfaction overall, even when the frequency of sex itself has changed.

## When is it worth seeing a doctor specifically?

**A sudden or significant change, rather than a gradual one, deserves a medical conversation**, as does any change accompanied by pain, bleeding, or physical difficulty. Many people wait years before raising this with a doctor out of embarrassment, but doctors who treat midlife patients see this exact concern regularly and have practical, evidence-based options to offer. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a drop in desire after 40 always linked to a relationship problem?**
Not usually. Biological changes, hormones, medication side effects, stress, and fatigue, are the most common drivers, and many couples with otherwise strong, happy relationships experience this shift. It is worth exploring as a joint, practical issue rather than immediately assuming something is wrong between partners.

**Can blood pressure or cholesterol medicines really affect this?**
Yes, certain medicines in both categories can affect desire or function as a known side effect for some people. This is a reason to mention any changes to the prescribing doctor rather than simply stopping or reducing a dose on your own, since there are often alternatives that work just as well without the same side effect.

**Is it normal for women to experience physical discomfort during sex after menopause?**
It is common and has a clear physical explanation related to hormonal changes, and it is also genuinely treatable. Doctors who specialise in women's health after 40 see this regularly and can suggest safe, appropriate options tailored to individual health history.

**Should we see a counsellor, a doctor, or both?**
Many couples benefit from both, since intimacy after 40 often has overlapping physical and emotional threads. A doctor can rule out or treat medical contributors, while a counsellor, individually or as a couple, can help with communication and expectations. There is no need to pick just one.

Intimacy after 40 rarely disappears for good; it usually changes shape, and most of what drives that change responds well to a combination of honest conversation and appropriate medical care. Silence tends to make the gap feel bigger than it actually is.
