"I'm just tired all the time" is one of the most common things doctors hear from patients past 40, and one of the most frustrating to live with, precisely because it rarely comes with an obvious explanation. You slept eight hours and still feel like you didn't. You get through the workday but have nothing left by evening. The good news is that persistent fatigue almost always has a checkable, identifiable cause, even if it takes a bit of detective work to find it.

## Is this just normal ageing, or is something actually wrong?

**Some decline in energy with age is normal, but a dramatic, persistent change from your own usual baseline is not something to simply accept.** The useful question isn't "is this normal for someone my age," it's "is this normal for me." If you used to manage a full day without flagging and now struggle to get through an afternoon without needing to lie down, that change is worth investigating, regardless of your age.

**Fatigue that has lasted more than a few weeks, that doesn't improve with rest, or that is affecting your work and relationships is reason enough to see a doctor**, who can run a basic panel of tests that rules out, or identifies, most of the common causes below.

## The most common, checkable causes

### Thyroid changes

**An underactive thyroid is one of the most frequently missed causes of persistent fatigue, and it becomes considerably more common in both men and women after 40.** Along with tiredness, it often brings weight gain, feeling cold more easily, dry skin, and low mood, though it can also show up with fatigue as the only prominent symptom. A simple blood test checks for it, and treatment, when needed, is usually straightforward and highly effective.

### Low iron and anaemia

**Low iron levels reduce the blood's ability to carry oxygen efficiently, which translates directly into tiredness, breathlessness on exertion, and sometimes a pale appearance or brittle nails.** This is particularly worth checking in women who still have periods, people with restricted diets, and anyone who has had unexplained blood loss, including from piles or ulcers.

### Poor sleep quality, not just poor sleep quantity

**Sleeping for eight hours doesn't guarantee restorative sleep if that sleep is frequently interrupted**, whether by snoring and breathing pauses, nighttime trips to the bathroom, restless legs, or simply lighter, less efficient sleep that tends to come with age. Someone can technically be in bed for long enough and still wake up exhausted if the quality of that sleep was poor throughout the night.

### Mood and persistent stress

**Depression and chronic stress commonly present as physical fatigue rather than obvious sadness**, particularly in people, often men, who are less likely to describe their inner experience in emotional terms. Low motivation, disturbed sleep, reduced appetite, and a sense of flatness alongside the tiredness are worth paying attention to, and this is treatable, not something to simply push through indefinitely.

### Medication side effects

**Several commonly prescribed medicines for blood pressure, allergies, and sleep can cause daytime drowsiness or fatigue as a side effect.** If fatigue began or worsened around the time a new medicine was started, this is worth raising directly with your doctor rather than assuming it is unrelated.

### Blood sugar changes

**Undiagnosed or poorly controlled diabetes frequently presents with persistent fatigue**, often alongside increased thirst, more frequent urination, and slow healing of minor cuts. A simple blood sugar test can rule this in or out quickly.

| Possible cause | Other clues to look for | How it's usually checked |
|---|---|---|
| Underactive thyroid | Weight gain, feeling cold, dry skin, low mood | A blood test (TSH) |
| Low iron or anaemia | Breathlessness on exertion, pale skin, brittle nails | A blood test (haemoglobin, ferritin) |
| Poor sleep quality | Snoring, waking unrefreshed, restless legs | A conversation with your doctor, sometimes a sleep study |
| Low mood or chronic stress | Low motivation, disturbed sleep, reduced appetite | A conversation with your doctor |
| Medication side effect | Fatigue starting after a new prescription | A medication review with your doctor |
| Blood sugar changes | Increased thirst, frequent urination, slow-healing cuts | A blood sugar test |

## What you can try while you figure out the cause

**Regular, even moderate, physical activity is one of the most reliably effective fatigue-fighting habits**, somewhat counterintuitively, since it genuinely improves energy over weeks even though it feels like effort in the moment. A consistent sleep and wake time, limiting caffeine after early afternoon, and getting daylight exposure in the morning can all meaningfully improve sleep quality and daytime alertness, independent of whatever underlying cause is eventually identified.

## When fatigue needs urgent attention

**Sudden, severe fatigue accompanied by chest pain, breathlessness, confusion, or weakness on one side of the body is not ordinary tiredness, it needs emergency care.** Call 112 immediately rather than waiting to see if it passes. Similarly, fatigue alongside a racing or very irregular heartbeat, or fainting, deserves urgent medical evaluation rather than a wait-and-see approach.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can stress alone cause this level of tiredness?**
Yes, chronic stress is a genuinely common and often underestimated cause of persistent fatigue, through disturbed sleep, elevated stress hormones, and the mental effort of constant vigilance or worry, even when a person doesn't feel overtly anxious.

**How long should I wait before seeing a doctor about tiredness?**
If fatigue has lasted more than two to three weeks without an obvious cause like a recent illness or a particularly demanding period at work, it's reasonable to get it checked rather than waiting longer.

**Is feeling tired after lunch normal, or a sign of something wrong?**
A mild post-lunch dip is a normal part of the body's daily rhythm for most people and isn't usually a concern. Severe, disabling afternoon fatigue that interferes with work is worth mentioning to a doctor, particularly if it is a new pattern.

**Can low mood really cause physical tiredness without obvious sadness?**
Yes, this is well recognised in medicine. It is rarely something to simply accept as the price of getting older, and it is always worth a proper conversation rather than years of pushing through on caffeine alone.
