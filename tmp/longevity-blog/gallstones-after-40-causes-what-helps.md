Gallstones have a reputation for announcing themselves suddenly and painfully, often after a heavy meal, with a severe ache under the right ribs that sends people straight to a hospital. What is less widely known is that most gallstones sit silently for years, causing no symptoms at all, and that the odds of developing them rise steadily as people cross into their 40s and beyond.

This article covers why that happens, who is genuinely at higher risk, and what actually lowers the odds, alongside what to do if you ever get that classic attack of pain.

## What exactly is a gallstone, and why does it form?

**The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile, a fluid made by the liver that helps digest fat**, releasing it into the intestine after a meal, particularly a fatty one. Bile contains cholesterol, bile salts, and a pigment called bilirubin, and gallstones form when the balance between these components tips, usually toward too much cholesterol relative to the bile salts that normally keep it dissolved. The excess cholesterol crystallises, and over time these crystals can grow into stones ranging from sand-grain size to something closer to a marble.

A smaller proportion of stones form from excess bilirubin instead, more common in people with certain blood disorders or chronic liver conditions, but cholesterol stones are by far the more common type seen in general practice.

## Why does the risk actually rise after 40?

**Several age-related changes stack up to increase gallstone risk as people get older.** The liver's cholesterol output and the gallbladder's own muscular contraction, which normally empties it regularly and prevents bile from sitting still long enough to crystallise, both tend to change gradually with age, in a direction that favours stone formation. Hormonal shifts play a notable role too, particularly in women: oestrogen increases cholesterol levels in bile, which is part of why gallstones are roughly twice as common in women as in men, and why the perimenopausal and postmenopausal years often see risk climb further.

**Weight is another major factor, and this is where it gets counterintuitive.** Carrying excess weight, especially around the abdomen, increases risk by raising cholesterol levels in bile. But losing weight very quickly, through very low-calorie diets, certain crash diets, or in the months after weight-loss surgery, can also sharply increase gallstone risk, because rapid fat breakdown floods bile with extra cholesterol faster than the gallbladder can handle. **The safest approach to weight loss, for gallstone risk and for overall health, is a gradual, sustained pace rather than a rapid crash.**

## Who else is at higher risk, and what actually helps?

| Risk factor | Why it raises risk | What genuinely helps |
|---|---|---|
| Being over 40, and especially female | Age and oestrogen both raise bile cholesterol | Not modifiable, but worth knowing if symptoms appear |
| Excess body weight, especially abdominal | Raises cholesterol levels in bile | Gradual, sustained weight management rather than rapid loss |
| Very rapid weight loss or crash dieting | Floods bile with cholesterol faster than it can be processed | Losing weight slowly, around half a kilogram to a kilogram a week, under medical guidance |
| Skipping meals or prolonged fasting | Gallbladder empties less often, letting bile sit and concentrate | Regular meal timing, avoiding very long gaps between meals |
| Family history of gallstones | Genetic influence on cholesterol metabolism in bile | Not modifiable, but worth mentioning to your doctor |
| Diabetes and high triglycerides | Both affect cholesterol handling in bile | Managing blood sugar and lipid levels with your doctor |

**Regular physical activity has also been linked to lower gallstone risk in several studies**, likely through its broader effects on weight, insulin sensitivity, and lipid levels rather than any direct action on the gallbladder itself. This fits with the general pattern in longevity research where movement helps far more systems than the ones it obviously targets.

## What does a gallstone attack actually feel like, and what should you do?

**A classic gallstone attack, sometimes called biliary colic, usually involves sudden, intense pain in the upper right or central abdomen**, often starting after a fatty meal, that can spread to the right shoulder blade or back. It typically lasts anywhere from thirty minutes to several hours and may come with nausea or vomiting. If the pain settles on its own and does not recur for a while, it is still worth getting checked, since recurrence is common once a stone has caused one episode.

If the pain is severe, does not settle, or comes with fever, chills, vomiting that will not stop, or yellowing of the skin or eyes (a sign bile flow may be blocked), this is a medical emergency. Go to a hospital or call 112 immediately rather than waiting it out at home, since a blocked bile duct or an infected gallbladder can become serious quickly without treatment.

## Do all gallstones need surgery?

**Not necessarily.** Silent gallstones that cause no symptoms are often simply monitored rather than treated, since the risk of complications from leaving them alone is generally lower than the risk of unnecessary surgery for most people. Once gallstones start causing repeated symptomatic attacks, however, doctors commonly recommend removing the gallbladder, a well-established and generally safe procedure, since the odds of further attacks and complications rise once symptoms have started. This decision depends on your specific situation, and your doctor is best placed to weigh the options with you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can diet alone dissolve existing gallstones?**
No reliable dietary approach dissolves existing stones on its own. Some medications can slowly dissolve certain small cholesterol stones over many months in specific cases, but this is decided and monitored by a doctor, not attempted through diet or supplements alone.

**Does cutting out all fat from my diet prevent gallstones?**
Not quite, and very low-fat diets can sometimes be unhelpful since the gallbladder needs some fat stimulus to contract and empty regularly. A balanced diet with regular meals and moderate, not excessive, fat intake is generally more protective than extreme fat avoidance.

**I had one painful attack years ago but nothing since. Should I still see a doctor?**
Yes, it is worth getting an ultrasound and a doctor's opinion even if symptoms have not recurred, since gallstone attacks commonly repeat, and knowing your situation helps you and your doctor plan ahead rather than being caught off guard later.

**Are gallstones linked to anything more serious, like cancer?**
Long-standing gallstones are linked to a modestly increased risk of gallbladder cancer, though this cancer remains rare overall. This is one more reason doctors sometimes recommend removing a symptomatic or very large gallstone-affected gallbladder rather than simply managing symptoms indefinitely.

Gallstones rarely make it onto anyone's list of organs or conditions to think about proactively after 40, which is exactly why they deserve a mention here. Staying at a steady, healthy weight, avoiding crash diets, eating at regular intervals, and staying physically active are all genuinely protective, and knowing the warning signs of a true attack means you will recognise it quickly rather than waiting it out if it ever happens.
