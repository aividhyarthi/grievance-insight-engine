"Drink eight glasses of water a day" is one of the most repeated pieces of health advice in existence, and also one of the least specifically evidence-based. Actual hydration needs vary considerably by body size, climate, activity level, and age, which matters a great deal in a country where summer temperatures regularly cross 40 degrees Celsius in large parts of the country.

How much water do you actually need for healthy ageing, and does that change after 40? There is no single magic number that applies to everyone, but hydration needs generally rise with heat and activity, thirst signals become less reliable with age, and mild chronic dehydration is common enough in older adults to be a genuine, under-recognised health concern.

## Is the "eight glasses a day" rule actually based on evidence?

**The eight glasses rule is a rough, memorable approximation, not a precisely researched requirement.** Actual fluid needs vary by body size, activity level, climate, and diet, since a substantial portion of daily fluid intake comes from food, particularly water-rich foods like fruits, vegetables, dal, and curd, not only from drinking water directly. Most health guidelines now describe a range, commonly around 2 to 3 litres of total fluid a day for adults, rather than a fixed universal number, and this range shifts upward meaningfully in hot weather, with physical activity, or during fever and illness.

## Why does hydration actually matter for healthy ageing specifically?

Water is involved in nearly every basic bodily function, regulating body temperature, supporting kidney function, cushioning joints, and maintaining blood volume and circulation. **Even mild dehydration, well before the point of feeling obviously thirsty, is linked to reduced concentration, fatigue, headaches, and constipation**, and in older adults, chronic mild dehydration has been associated with a higher risk of urinary tract infections, kidney stones, and, in some research, faster biological ageing markers, though this research area is still developing. Kidney function also naturally declines somewhat with age, which makes consistent, adequate hydration more important, not less, for maintaining kidney health over the following decades.

### Why thirst becomes a less reliable guide as you age

The body's thirst-signalling mechanism becomes less sensitive with age, meaning many older adults do not feel thirsty until they are already meaningfully dehydrated. This is one reason dehydration-related hospital admissions, particularly during Indian summers, disproportionately affect older adults. **Waiting until you feel thirsty is a less reliable strategy after roughly 60**, which is why building a habit of drinking water at regular points through the day, rather than only in response to thirst, matters more with age.

## How does India's climate change the picture?

For much of the country, several months a year involve high heat and humidity, which substantially increases fluid loss through sweat, even without noticeable physical exertion. **People working outdoors, older adults in homes without adequate cooling, and anyone exercising during warmer parts of the day need meaningfully more fluid** than the baseline range suggests, along with attention to electrolyte balance, since heavy sweating also depletes sodium and other minerals. Traditional Indian drinks and foods such as buttermilk (chaas), coconut water, and watery dals are genuinely useful tools here, providing both fluid and some electrolyte replacement in a culturally familiar form, rather than needing a specialised sports drink.

| Situation | Approximate daily fluid guidance |
|---|---|
| Sedentary adult, mild weather | Around 2 to 2.5 litres total fluid |
| Active adult or hot weather | 3 litres or more, adjusted for sweat loss |
| Older adult (60+), any weather | Regular scheduled drinking, not reliance on thirst |
| During fever, diarrhoea, or vomiting | Increased fluids, oral rehydration solution if advised by a doctor |
| Outdoor workers in Indian summer | Significantly higher fluid and electrolyte needs, frequent breaks |

## Can you actually drink too much water?

Yes, though this is far less common than under-hydration for most people. **Drinking extremely large amounts of water in a short period can dilute sodium levels in the blood**, a condition called hyponatremia, which is genuinely dangerous but occurs mainly in specific situations, such as endurance athletes drinking excessive plain water without electrolyte replacement, or rare cases of compulsive overdrinking. For the average person managing daily hydration sensibly, drinking too much is not a realistic day-to-day concern. People with kidney disease, heart failure, or on specific fluid-restricted medical advice should follow their doctor's specific fluid guidance rather than general population recommendations.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does tea or coffee count towards daily fluid intake, or does it dehydrate you?**
Tea and coffee do count towards fluid intake for most people. The mild diuretic effect of caffeine is generally outweighed by the fluid volume consumed, though very high caffeine intake can have a modestly greater diuretic effect worth being aware of.

**How can I tell if I am dehydrated without measuring anything?**
Simple signs include dark yellow urine, infrequent urination, a dry mouth, fatigue, and headache. In older adults, confusion or dizziness can also be a sign of dehydration and should be taken seriously rather than dismissed as normal ageing.

**Do I need to drink more water if I have kidney stones or a history of them?**
Generally yes, higher fluid intake is commonly recommended for people prone to certain types of kidney stones, since it helps dilute the substances that form them, but the specific target should be set with a doctor based on your particular stone type and kidney function.

**Is coconut water or an electrolyte drink better than plain water after sweating heavily?**
For significant sweat loss, such as after intense exercise or a very hot day, replacing some electrolytes alongside water is genuinely useful, and coconut water, buttermilk, or a proper oral rehydration solution are reasonable choices. For everyday mild activity, plain water combined with normal meals is usually sufficient.

Hydration is one of the simplest, most overlooked levers in healthy ageing, not because the science is complicated, but because it is easy to underestimate how much thirst alone fails as a guide over time. Building a steady, climate-appropriate drinking habit, rather than chasing an exact number, is the more useful goal for most people.
