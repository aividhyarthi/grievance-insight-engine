Walk into any pharmacy or scroll through any wellness app in India today and you will find shelves and pop-ups promising that one more capsule is the missing piece of your longevity plan. Some of that promise is backed by decades of solid research. A lot of it is not. The honest answer sits somewhere between "supplements are useless" and "you need a dozen bottles a day," and getting that answer right matters, both for your health and your wallet.

## Why does the supplement conversation get so confusing?

**The supplement industry runs on hope, not always on evidence.** Research on a nutrient in a lab dish or a small short-term trial often gets marketed as if it were proven to add years to your life. In reality, very few supplements have strong evidence from large, long-term human studies showing they extend healthspan or lifespan in people who are already eating adequately. That does not mean supplements are worthless, it means the claims usually run far ahead of the science.

There is also a real gap between what a supplement can do for someone with a genuine deficiency versus what it does for someone who already gets enough of a nutrient from food. Correcting a deficiency can meaningfully improve energy, bone health, or mood. Adding more of something you already have enough of rarely helps, and in some cases (high-dose fat-soluble vitamins, for instance) can cause harm.

## Which supplements have the strongest evidence for Indian adults over 40?

**Vitamin D deserves attention first**, because Indian studies consistently find deficiency rates well above 50 percent in urban populations, despite the country's abundant sunlight. Indoor working hours, pollution, skin covering, and sunscreen use all play a role. A simple blood test (25-hydroxy vitamin D) can confirm whether you actually need supplementation, and if you do, your doctor can guide the right dose and duration rather than a fixed over-the-counter amount.

**Vitamin B12 is the second clear case.** It comes almost entirely from animal foods, which makes deficiency common in people following vegetarian or vegan diets, a large share of India's population. B12 deficiency can cause fatigue, nerve tingling, memory issues, and in older adults can be mistaken for normal ageing. A blood test again settles the question, and correction is straightforward once identified.

**Omega-3 fatty acids** have reasonable evidence for heart and possibly brain health, particularly for people who eat little oily fish. Options include fatty fish such as sardines or mackerel a couple of times a week, walnuts, and flaxseed, with a supplement considered mainly when diet alone cannot cover it or for specific cardiac risk profiles, best discussed with a doctor.

**Calcium** matters for bone health, especially for women after menopause, but the preference is to get it from food (dairy, sesame seeds, ragi, leafy greens) where possible, since very high-dose calcium supplementation has been linked to cardiovascular concerns in some studies. Protein powder can be reasonable for people who genuinely struggle to meet protein needs through food, particularly older adults with reduced appetite, but it is a convenience tool, not a magic ingredient.

## Which supplements are usually not worth the money?

**General multivitamins for otherwise healthy, well-fed adults have surprisingly weak evidence.** Several large long-term studies have failed to show that daily multivitamins reduce the risk of heart disease, cancer, or death in people without specific deficiencies. They are not dangerous for most people at standard doses, but they are also not doing the heavy lifting the marketing suggests.

**High-dose antioxidant supplements** (vitamin E, beta-carotene, and similar) have actually shown neutral or, in some trial populations, worse outcomes compared to placebo, particularly for lung cancer risk in smokers taking beta-carotene. The idea that "more antioxidants equals less ageing" does not hold up in controlled trials the way it does in theory.

**Trendy longevity compounds** marketed with terms like cellular rejuvenation or anti-ageing molecules usually have promising animal or lab data and very little large-scale human safety or efficacy data. It is reasonable to stay curious about ongoing research here while being cautious about paying a premium for early-stage science.

## What about Ayurvedic herbs and formulations?

**Several herbs used in Ayurvedic tradition, such as ashwagandha and certain adaptogenic formulations, have growing modern research interest**, with some smaller trials suggesting benefits for stress and sleep. Ayurveda offers a genuinely thoughtful, centuries-old framework for wellbeing that deserves respect rather than dismissal. That said, it works best as a complement to, not a replacement for, modern medical care, particularly for anyone managing a diagnosed condition or taking prescription medication. Herbal products in India are not always standardised for dosage or purity the way pharmaceutical drugs are, so it is worth consulting a qualified, registered Ayurvedic practitioner for personalised guidance rather than self-prescribing based on packaging claims or online reviews.

## How should you actually decide?

**Start with food, not a bottle.** A varied Indian diet with dals, vegetables, whole grains, dairy or fortified alternatives, and some nuts and seeds covers most nutrient needs for most people. Supplements are best thought of as filling a specific, tested gap, not as insurance against an unclear risk. If you are considering a supplement because of a symptom, tiredness, low mood, hair thinning, get that symptom properly evaluated first rather than treating the supplement as the diagnosis and the cure in one step.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a multivitamin after 40?**
Most healthy adults eating a reasonably varied diet do not need a daily multivitamin, based on current large-scale evidence. It is more useful to test for specific deficiencies, particularly vitamin D and B12, and address those directly with your doctor's guidance.

**Is it safe to take multiple supplements together?**
Not always. Some supplements interact with each other or with prescription medication, and fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) can build up to harmful levels if overused. Always tell your doctor about every supplement you take, including herbal ones.

**Are Ayurvedic supplements safer because they are natural?**
Not necessarily. Natural does not automatically mean risk-free, and quality control can vary between products. Ayurvedic formulations can be valuable as part of a personalised plan from a qualified practitioner, but they still deserve the same scrutiny as any other supplement, especially around sourcing and dosage.

**What is the single most useful thing I can do before buying any supplement?**
Get a basic blood panel done. It turns a guessing game into an informed decision, and it is far cheaper than months of supplements that may not be addressing an actual deficiency.

Supplements can genuinely help when they are targeted at a real, tested gap, but they are not a shortcut past food, sleep, movement, and regular medical checkups. The most useful longevity habit here is curiosity paired with a blood test, not a shopping cart.
