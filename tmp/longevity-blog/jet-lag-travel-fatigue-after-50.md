If a long-haul flight used to mean a rough day or two before you felt normal again, and now it means the better part of a week, you are not imagining things. Recovering from jet lag genuinely does take longer as you get older, and this matters more than it used to for a very Indian reason: a large number of parents in their 50s, 60s, and beyond now travel frequently, often long-haul, to visit children who have settled abroad.

Understanding why jet lag hits harder with age, and what actually helps versus what is folk wisdom with no real effect, can turn a trip that used to derail an entire week into one that costs you a day or two at most.

## Why does jet lag actually get worse with age?

**The body's circadian clock, the internal system that governs when you feel sleepy and alert, becomes measurably less flexible with age.** In younger adults, the clock resets to a new time zone relatively efficiently, sometimes within two or three days. In older adults, that same reset can take a week or longer, partly because the master clock in the brain responds more sluggishly to the light and darkness cues that normally recalibrate it.

There is a second factor that compounds the first. Sleep quality naturally becomes lighter and more fragmented with age even at home, with less of the deep sleep that feels most restorative. That leaves less of a buffer to absorb a few nights of poor sleep from travel, so the same disruption that a 30-year-old shrugs off within days can leave someone in their 60s feeling foggy and exhausted for the better part of a week.

## Does direction of travel actually make a difference?

Yes, and this is one of the more consistently proven aspects of jet lag research. **Travelling east, for instance from India towards Europe or North America, is generally harder on the body than travelling west**, because eastward travel requires the internal clock to shift forward, which goes against its natural tendency to drift later rather than earlier. This is worth knowing simply so you can set realistic expectations and plan a lighter schedule for the days immediately after an eastward flight in particular.

## What actually helps, versus what is just a travel ritual?

This is where it is worth separating evidence from habit. **Strategic light exposure is the single most powerful tool for resetting the circadian clock**, more effective than anything else on this list. Getting bright light, ideally outdoors, at the right time for your destination's day-night cycle, and avoiding light at the wrong time, speeds up adjustment measurably. A gradual shift of your sleep and meal times by an hour or so per day in the two to three days before a long trip also gives the body a head start rather than asking it to adjust all at once on arrival.

**Melatonin, taken in a low dose at the appropriate local bedtime for a few nights after arrival, has genuine evidence behind it specifically for jet lag**, though it should be discussed with a doctor first, particularly if you take other medications or have a condition where melatonin's mild sedative and hormonal effects need consideration. Caffeine, used to stay alert during the local daytime rather than continuously, can help, but late-day caffeine will only compound the sleep disruption you are trying to recover from.

### What has weaker or no evidence?

Many popular jet lag remedies, specific in-flight stretching routines beyond basic movement, certain hydration "protocols" beyond simply drinking enough water, and various travel supplements marketed for jet lag specifically, have little to no rigorous evidence behind them. That does not make them harmful, but they should not be relied on in place of the light exposure, sleep timing, and gradual adjustment strategies that actually work.

| Strategy | Evidence strength | Practical note |
|---|---|---|
| Timed light exposure | Strong | Outdoor light at the right time for your destination is most effective |
| Gradual pre-trip schedule shift | Strong | Shift sleep and meals by about an hour a day for 2-3 days before departure |
| Melatonin, low dose, short term | Moderate to strong | Discuss timing and dose with a doctor, especially with existing medications |
| Daytime caffeine, avoided late | Moderate | Helps daytime alertness, but late use worsens the underlying problem |
| In-flight rituals, supplements | Weak or unproven | Not harmful generally, but not a substitute for the above |

## Should chronic conditions change how you plan a trip?

**If you manage diabetes, high blood pressure, or take blood-thinning medication, long-haul travel across time zones deserves a conversation with your doctor before you go**, not because travel is unsafe, but because medication timing may need adjusting across the time change, and prolonged sitting on long flights raises the risk of blood clots for anyone with reduced mobility or certain existing conditions. Wearing compression stockings, walking periodically during the flight, and staying well hydrated are simple precautions worth taking regardless of age.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does jet lag typically last after 50?**
It varies with the number of time zones crossed and direction of travel, but many people over 50 find noticeable jet lag effects lasting five to seven days after a long eastward flight, compared to two to four days for a younger traveller on the same route. Westward travel is generally quicker to recover from.

**Is it worth taking melatonin for every long-haul trip?**
For occasional long-haul travel, low-dose melatonin at the destination's local bedtime for a few nights can genuinely help, but it is worth discussing with a doctor first, especially if you take other medications regularly or have never used it before.

**Does staying hydrated actually reduce jet lag?**
Adequate hydration supports general wellbeing during travel and can reduce some of the fatigue and headache that come with cabin air and prolonged sitting, but it does not directly reset your circadian clock the way timed light exposure does. Both matter, but they are not interchangeable.

**Should older travellers avoid long-haul flights altogether?**
Not at all, for most healthy older adults long-haul travel is entirely manageable with some planning. The main precautions are reviewing medications with a doctor beforehand, moving periodically during the flight, and building in a lighter schedule for the first day or two after arrival rather than diving straight into a packed itinerary.

Travel does not need to become harder to justify as you get older, it needs a slightly different approach. A little pre-trip planning around light, sleep timing, and medication schedules can turn what used to be a week of feeling off into a day or two, leaving more of the trip for the reason you took it in the first place.
