Vitamin B12 deficiency has a habit of hiding behind other explanations. Fatigue gets blamed on stress or poor sleep, tingling in the feet gets dismissed as "just sitting too long," and memory slips get waved away as normal ageing. Sometimes those explanations are correct. Often enough, in India, a simple blood test reveals B12 has quietly dropped low enough to explain a good part of it.

This article looks at why B12 deficiency is so common in India specifically, who is genuinely at higher risk after 40, what the evidence says about testing and correcting it, and why this is one supplement category where self-diagnosis is a particularly bad idea.

## Why is vitamin B12 deficiency particularly common in India?

**Vitamin B12 is found almost exclusively in animal foods, including meat, fish, eggs, and dairy, with essentially no reliable natural source in plant foods.** Since a very large share of India's population follows a vegetarian or vegan diet, often for cultural, religious, or personal reasons, this alone makes B12 one of the more predictable nutrient gaps in the country compared to many other parts of the world.

**But diet is only part of the story, and age adds a second, independent risk factor that applies even to people who eat meat, eggs, and dairy regularly.** Absorbing B12 from food requires adequate stomach acid and a protein called intrinsic factor, and both tend to decline somewhat with age, meaning older adults across all diet patterns absorb B12 from food less efficiently than they did decades earlier.

## Who is actually at higher risk after 40?

**Vegetarians and vegans remain the clearest risk group, simply because their dietary intake is inherently lower**, but several other factors raise risk specifically after 40, regardless of diet. These include long-term use of certain acid-reducing medications for reflux, since stomach acid is needed to release B12 from food, long-term use of the diabetes medication metformin, which is known to reduce B12 absorption over time, and any history of gut surgery or conditions affecting the small intestine.

**People who have had weight-loss surgery, have a diagnosed autoimmune digestive condition, or drink alcohol heavily are also at meaningfully higher risk**, since all of these can interfere with absorption independent of how much B12 is actually in the diet. Anyone in these groups has a reasonable case for periodic testing rather than waiting for symptoms to appear.

## What actually happens in the body when B12 runs low?

**B12 plays a central role in nerve function and in making red blood cells, which is why its deficiency shows up through two main pathways: anaemia and nerve-related symptoms.** On the blood side, low B12 can cause a specific type of anaemia that leads to fatigue, pale skin, and breathlessness on exertion. On the nerve side, it can cause tingling or numbness in the hands and feet, balance problems, and in more advanced cases, memory and concentration difficulties.

**This is exactly why B12 deficiency is so easy to mistake for ordinary ageing or general tiredness.** Fatigue, mild memory lapses, and some loss of steadiness on your feet can all have many causes, and B12 deficiency is simply one of the more fixable ones, which makes it worth ruling out through a blood test rather than assuming the worst, or assuming nothing at all.

## How is B12 deficiency actually diagnosed and corrected?

**Diagnosis is straightforward: a blood test measuring B12 levels, sometimes alongside other markers if the result is borderline, gives a clear answer rather than leaving it to guesswork.** This is one area where self-diagnosing from symptoms alone is particularly unreliable, since the symptoms overlap heavily with several other common conditions, including thyroid problems and other nutrient deficiencies.

**Correction, once confirmed, is generally straightforward and depends on the cause and severity.** Mild dietary deficiency often responds well to oral supplementation and dietary changes, while deficiency caused by an absorption problem, such as reduced intrinsic factor, may need injectable B12 instead, since oral supplements rely on the same absorption pathway that is impaired in that situation. This decision belongs with your doctor, not a pharmacy counter guess.

**Because the dose, form, and duration of correction depend heavily on the underlying cause, self-supplementing with high-dose B12 tablets without a confirmed diagnosis is not a sensible shortcut**, even though B12 itself is considered low-risk at typical supplement doses. Getting the actual cause right matters more than simply adding a supplement and hoping it covers the gap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a vegetarian diet alone meet B12 needs through fortified foods?**
It is possible with deliberate effort through fortified foods, but many vegetarians in India do not consistently consume enough fortified products to fully close the gap, which is why periodic testing is still reasonable even with a thoughtfully planned vegetarian diet. Your doctor can advise on whether supplementation makes sense for you specifically.

**Is B12 deficiency reversible if caught early?**
Yes, in most cases, especially when caught before significant nerve damage has set in. Blood-related symptoms like anaemia typically improve quickly with correction, while nerve-related symptoms can take longer to resolve and, in some longstanding cases, may not fully reverse, which is part of why earlier detection matters.

**Can I just take a B12 supplement without getting tested first?**
It is reasonable to ask your doctor about this, particularly if you are vegetarian or vegan, but testing first is still the more reliable approach, especially if you have symptoms, since it tells you and your doctor whether the dose and form being considered actually matches your situation.

**Does B12 deficiency only affect older adults?**
No, it can occur at any age, particularly in vegetarians, vegans, and people with certain digestive conditions, but age adds an additional, independent risk factor through reduced absorption, which is why testing becomes more relevant as a routine consideration after 40 regardless of diet.

Vitamin B12 deficiency is one of the more fixable contributors to fatigue and nerve symptoms that often get written off as simply getting older. A straightforward blood test, discussed with your doctor, settles the question far more reliably than guessing, and correction, once the cause is clear, is usually simpler than people expect.
