A plate of paneer that never bothered you in your twenties suddenly leaves you bloated in your forties. A glass of milk that was a daily habit now means a trip to the bathroom an hour later. If this sounds familiar, you're not imagining it. Food intolerances genuinely can appear, or worsen, in midlife, even for foods you've eaten your entire life without trouble.

This piece looks at why that happens, which foods tend to be the usual suspects, and how to tell an intolerance that just needs managing from something that needs a doctor's attention.

## Why would a food you've always eaten suddenly cause problems?

**Digestive capacity isn't fixed for life; it gradually shifts with age, and several changes tend to happen around the same time in midlife.** Lactase, the enzyme that breaks down milk sugar, can decline further after your 30s and 40s even in people who tolerated dairy fine for decades. Stomach acid production can also reduce gradually with age, affecting how efficiently protein and certain nutrients are broken down.

**Your gut microbiome also shifts with age,** and changes in that bacterial balance can affect how well you tolerate certain carbohydrates found in wheat, onion, garlic, and legumes, foods that make up a large share of typical Indian cooking.

### Lactose intolerance: the most common new arrival

Lactose intolerance, the inability to properly digest milk sugar, is one of the most frequently reported new food issues in midlife, and it genuinely increases with age in a large share of adults globally, including in India, where lactase decline after childhood is the more typical pattern rather than the exception. Someone who drank milk and ate curd for years can develop bloating, cramping, or loose stools as lactase production drops further, often gradually enough to be mistaken for a stomach bug at first.

## Is it an intolerance, or could it be something more serious?

**A food intolerance is a digestive reaction, usually bloating, gas, cramping, or loose stools, and it is distinct from a food allergy, which involves the immune system and can include symptoms like hives, swelling, or breathing difficulty.** An intolerance, while uncomfortable, is generally not dangerous. A true allergy can occasionally be medically urgent, and anyone with swelling of the face or throat, difficulty breathing, or a sudden widespread rash after eating should treat it as an emergency and call 112 or get to a hospital immediately.

Beyond allergy versus intolerance, some symptoms that look like "just a food intolerance" can also be early signs of irritable bowel syndrome, celiac disease (a genuine immune reaction to gluten, distinct from simple wheat sensitivity), gallbladder issues, or rarer, more serious conditions. **Persistent symptoms, unexplained weight loss, blood in the stool, or symptoms worsening despite diet changes are reasons to see a doctor rather than cutting out more foods on your own.**

| Symptom pattern | Likely to be |
|---|---|
| Bloating or loose stools after dairy, improves when you cut back | Lactose intolerance, common and manageable |
| Mild discomfort after large or spicy meals | Normal digestive slowing with age |
| Swelling, hives, or breathing trouble after a specific food | Possible allergy, needs urgent medical attention |
| Weight loss, blood in stool, or symptoms worsening over months | Needs a doctor's evaluation, not self-management |

### What foods tend to become harder to tolerate after 40?

Besides dairy, the most commonly reported new intolerances involve wheat and gluten (not necessarily celiac disease, often just general sensitivity), gas-forming legumes, and spicy or oily food, which can aggravate reflux more than it used to. This doesn't mean these foods are bad; your tolerance threshold may have changed, and quantity and preparation often matter as much as the food itself.

## What actually helps, practically?

**Start by tracking rather than guessing.** A simple food and symptom diary for two to three weeks often reveals the pattern more clearly than trying to remember after the fact. For suspected lactose intolerance specifically, many people find that smaller portions, having dairy with other food rather than alone, or choosing curd and aged products (which are naturally lower in lactose than milk) are tolerated better than cutting dairy out completely. For other suspected intolerances, a doctor or dietitian can guide a structured elimination and reintroduction process, which is more reliable than permanently avoiding entire food groups based on a guess.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to become lactose intolerant in your 40s if you've never had an issue before?**
Yes, this is a genuinely common pattern. Lactase production can decline gradually with age in many adults, and symptoms can appear or worsen in midlife even after decades of tolerating dairy without any problem.

**Should I get tested before cutting out foods on my own?**
It's worth discussing persistent or worsening symptoms with a doctor before broadly eliminating food groups, partly to rule out other causes and partly because unnecessary long-term restriction can create its own nutritional gaps.

**Can stress make food intolerances worse?**
It can contribute. Chronic stress affects gut motility and can heighten digestive sensitivity generally, so persistent high stress alongside new food symptoms is worth mentioning to your doctor as part of the full picture.

**Is wheat sensitivity the same as celiac disease?**
No. Celiac disease is a specific immune reaction to gluten that requires medical diagnosis, usually through blood tests and sometimes a biopsy, while general wheat or gluten sensitivity is a different and less severe pattern. If you suspect either, a doctor can help clarify which one you're actually dealing with before you make major dietary changes.

A changing relationship with certain foods after 40 is common and usually manageable once you understand what's actually shifting in your digestion. The goal isn't to fear food, it's to notice the pattern, adjust sensibly, and get a doctor involved when something doesn't add up.
