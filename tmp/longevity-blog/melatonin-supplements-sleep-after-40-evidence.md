Sleep tends to get lighter and more fragmented after 40, and melatonin has become one of the first things many people reach for when a few bad nights turn into a persistent pattern. It is sold over the counter in India as a supplement, often marketed with phrases like "natural sleep hormone," which makes it sound both effortless and risk-free. Neither assumption is entirely accurate.

Melatonin is a real hormone your own body produces, and the supplement version does have genuine evidence behind certain specific uses. It is not, however, a general-purpose sleeping pill, and its effectiveness depends heavily on what is actually causing your sleep trouble in the first place. Understanding the difference matters, especially since India's supplement market is loosely regulated, and not every bottle on a pharmacy shelf has been through the same quality scrutiny.

## What does melatonin actually do in the body?

**Melatonin is a hormone produced by a small gland in the brain, released in response to darkness, that signals to your body that it is time to prepare for sleep.** It does not force sleep onset the way a sedative does, it nudges your internal body clock, your circadian rhythm, toward a sleep-ready state. Levels naturally rise in the evening, peak in the middle of the night, and fall toward morning, and this rhythm can genuinely shift and weaken somewhat with age.

This distinction matters enormously for how well the supplement actually works. **A sedative makes you drowsy regardless of what time it is. Melatonin works best when the problem is about timing, your body's internal clock being out of sync with when you actually need or want to sleep**, rather than an inability to feel sleepy at all.

### Why does natural melatonin production change after 40?

Research suggests melatonin production can decline somewhat with age, and the timing of its release can also shift, which may partly explain why many people notice they wake earlier, fall asleep less easily at their usual bedtime, or feel their sleep has become lighter and more easily disrupted compared to their twenties and thirties. This is one plausible, though not the only, contributor to the sleep changes common in midlife.

## When does the evidence actually support using it?

**The strongest, most consistent evidence for melatonin exists for circadian timing problems, not general poor sleep.** Jet lag, where your body clock is out of sync with a new time zone, is one of the better-studied uses, with reasonable evidence that correctly timed melatonin at the destination's bedtime can speed adjustment. Shift workers trying to sleep during daylight hours, and people with delayed sleep phase, where the body's clock runs persistently later than desired, also show genuine benefit in research.

**For general age-related insomnia, the picture is considerably weaker and more mixed.** Some studies show a modest reduction in the time it takes to fall asleep, often by a matter of minutes rather than a dramatic change, while effects on sleep quality, total sleep time, and staying asleep through the night are inconsistent across trials. This does not mean it never helps, some individuals do report meaningful benefit, but it means expectations should be calibrated to "might help somewhat with falling asleep," not "will fix fragmented or poor-quality sleep."

| Use case | Strength of evidence | What to realistically expect |
|---|---|---|
| Jet lag, especially eastward travel | Reasonably strong | Faster adjustment to new time zone when timed correctly |
| Shift work sleep disruption | Moderate | Some improvement in daytime sleep quality |
| Delayed sleep phase (body clock runs late) | Moderate to strong | Can help shift bedtime earlier over time |
| General age-related insomnia | Weak to mixed | Minor reduction in time to fall asleep for some, inconsistent otherwise |
| Staying asleep through the night | Weak | Not well supported as a primary benefit |

## Is it safe, and what should you check before buying?

**Melatonin is generally considered safe for short-term use in healthy adults at the doses commonly sold, but it is not entirely without interactions or side effects.** Morning grogginess, headache, and mild dizziness are the most commonly reported side effects. More importantly, it can interact with blood thinners, diabetes medications by affecting blood sugar, blood pressure medication, and immunosuppressants, which is precisely why telling your doctor before starting it matters, especially for the common pattern of taking several medications together after 40.

**India's supplement market, like many countries without the tightest pharmaceutical-grade regulation, means actual melatonin content can vary noticeably between brands and batches.** Studies testing commercially available melatonin supplements in various markets have found some products containing considerably more or less melatonin than labelled, occasionally by a wide margin. Choosing products from established, reputable manufacturers, checking for any third-party quality testing mentioned on the label, and starting with a lower amount rather than assuming more is better are all reasonable precautions.

**Long-term daily use over months or years has not been as thoroughly studied as short-term use**, which is another reason this fits better as an occasional tool for specific situations, jet lag, a temporarily shifted schedule, than as an indefinite nightly habit without medical guidance.

## What actually helps more for everyday poor sleep after 40?

**For the common pattern of lighter, more easily disrupted sleep that comes with age, the habits with the strongest evidence are less exciting than a supplement but considerably more reliable.** Consistent wake times, morning light exposure, limiting screens and heavy meals close to bedtime, and addressing specific disruptors like needing to use the bathroom at night, pain, or anxiety tend to move the needle more than a melatonin tablet alone, for most people experiencing general age-related sleep changes rather than a true circadian timing problem.

**If poor sleep persists despite reasonable habits, it is worth raising with your doctor rather than cycling through supplements on your own**, since conditions like sleep apnoea, restless legs, thyroid problems, or medication side effects are common, treatable causes that a supplement will not address.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I take melatonin every night long-term?**
It is better used for specific, time-limited situations like jet lag or a temporarily shifted schedule rather than as an indefinite nightly habit, since long-term daily use has less research behind it and ongoing poor sleep deserves a doctor's evaluation to rule out a treatable underlying cause.

**Does melatonin interact with blood pressure or diabetes medication?**
Yes, it can affect blood sugar control and may interact with some blood pressure medications, which is why telling your doctor before starting it is important, particularly if you already manage one or more chronic conditions with regular medication.

**Is melatonin the same as a sleeping pill?**
No. Sleeping pills generally sedate you directly, while melatonin signals timing information to your body clock. This is why melatonin tends to help more with falling asleep at the wrong time than with genuinely poor-quality or fragmented sleep, and why it should not be assumed to work the same way a prescribed sleep medication does.

**Why does melatonin content vary so much between brands in India?**
Supplements in India, as in many markets, are regulated less strictly than prescription medicines, meaning manufacturing quality control varies between brands. Choosing well-established manufacturers and discussing the specific product with your doctor or pharmacist is a reasonable way to reduce this uncertainty.

Melatonin genuinely has a place for specific sleep-timing problems, but it is not the all-purpose fix its marketing often implies. If your sleep has changed since your 40s began, the first useful step is figuring out whether this is truly a timing issue or something else entirely, ideally with your doctor's help, rather than assuming a supplement bottle has the answer.
