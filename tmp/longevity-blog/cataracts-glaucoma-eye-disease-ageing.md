Most people notice their vision changing gradually: reading in dim light gets harder, headlights seem glarier at night, or fine print needs to be held further away. Some of that is ordinary ageing. Some of it is the early signal of a condition that is extremely common after 40 and, if caught in time, very manageable.

Cataracts and glaucoma are the two age-related eye conditions doctors in India see most often, and they behave very differently. One tends to announce itself with blurring you can actually notice. The other can progress for years with no symptoms at all until damage is already done. Knowing the difference is what makes early screening worth the trip to the eye doctor.

## Why do cataracts and glaucoma deserve attention after 40?

Vision loss is one of the more overlooked parts of healthy ageing, often mentioned only after it starts limiting daily life, driving at night, reading a prescription label, recognising a face across a room. **Both cataracts and glaucoma become significantly more common with each decade after 40**, and India carries a notably high burden of both, partly due to genetics, partly due to under-screening, particularly outside major cities where an ophthalmologist may not be nearby.

The encouraging part is that neither condition needs to end in serious vision loss. Cataract surgery is one of the most effective and commonly performed procedures in modern medicine. Glaucoma, while it cannot be reversed once damage occurs, can usually be stopped from progressing further with early treatment. The problem is almost never the treatment, it is the delay in noticing there is something to treat.

## What is actually different between a cataract and glaucoma?

### Cataracts: cloudy, but treatable

A cataract is a clouding of the eye's natural lens, usually developing slowly over years. **Vision with a cataract typically becomes blurry, hazy, or glare-sensitive**, especially with oncoming headlights at night, and colours can look faded or yellowed. It is not dangerous to the rest of the eye and does not cause pain. Cataract surgery, replacing the clouded lens with a clear artificial one, is quick, has a high success rate, and is widely available across India, including through government eye care schemes in many states.

### Glaucoma: the "silent thief of sight"

Glaucoma is different, and more concerning, because of how quietly it progresses. It involves rising pressure inside the eye that gradually damages the optic nerve, usually starting with peripheral, side vision, which most people do not notice losing until a significant portion is already gone. **By the time glaucoma causes symptoms you'd notice yourself, meaningful and permanent vision loss has often already occurred.** This is precisely why eye pressure checks matter even when your vision feels completely normal.

|---|---|---|
| What happens | Lens becomes cloudy | Eye pressure damages the optic nerve |

## What actually raises the risk, and does Ayurveda have a role?

**Diabetes and high blood pressure raise the risk of both cataracts and glaucoma**, adding one more reason blood sugar and blood pressure control matter well beyond the organs they are usually associated with. A family history of glaucoma specifically roughly doubles individual risk, which is why doctors often ask about it directly during a checkup. Prolonged, unprotected sun exposure and smoking are additional risk factors for cataracts.

Ayurvedic eye care, including practices like netra tarpana (a supervised eye treatment using medicated ghee) and general dietary emphasis on foods considered supportive of eye tissue, has a long tradition in Indian medicine and can be genuinely soothing for eye strain and dryness when done under a qualified practitioner. It is not, however, a treatment for cataracts or glaucoma themselves, and should be pursued alongside, never instead of, regular ophthalmological care once either condition is suspected or diagnosed.

## What does eye screening after 40 actually involve?

A comprehensive eye exam is not the same as a quick vision chart check for glasses. It typically includes a check of visual sharpness, an eye pressure measurement, and an examination of the lens and optic nerve, usually with the pupils dilated so the doctor can see the back of the eye clearly. None of it is painful, though dilation temporarily blurs near vision and brightens light sensitivity for a few hours afterward.

## When should you actually see a doctor rather than wait it out?

Sudden vision changes, eye pain, seeing halos around lights, or a sudden increase in floaters or flashes of light are reasons to see a doctor promptly rather than waiting for a routine appointment. Gradual blurring that is affecting daily tasks, reading, driving, recognising faces, is also worth booking an exam for even without any of those warning signs. If you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of glaucoma, mention this specifically when booking so the clinic knows to include a full pressure and nerve check, not just a vision chart.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**At what age should I start regular eye exams for cataracts and glaucoma?**
Most doctors recommend a baseline comprehensive eye exam by 40, then every one to two years after that, more frequently if you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of glaucoma. Earlier and more frequent screening is reasonable if any of these apply to you.

**Can cataracts be prevented, or only treated?**
Cataracts cannot be entirely prevented, but wearing UV-protective sunglasses, not smoking, and managing blood sugar well can slow how quickly they develop. Once a cataract significantly affects vision, surgery is the only effective treatment, and it is a well-established, generally low-risk procedure.

**Is glaucoma only a concern if it runs in my family?**
Family history roughly doubles your risk, but glaucoma can and does occur without any family history, particularly with age, diabetes, or high eye pressure detected incidentally during a routine exam. This is why screening matters for everyone after 40, not only those with a known family history.

**Can Ayurvedic eye treatments cure cataracts or glaucoma?**
No. Ayurvedic eye care can support general eye comfort and is worth discussing with a qualified practitioner for issues like dryness or strain, but neither cataracts nor glaucoma resolve with these treatments alone. Both require standard medical evaluation and treatment, alongside which complementary care can have its place.

Vision loss from cataracts or glaucoma is, in the great majority of cases, either fully correctable or largely preventable with early attention. The single highest-leverage thing you can do is treat an eye exam the same way you treat a blood pressure check, a routine part of ageing well, not something you wait to need.
