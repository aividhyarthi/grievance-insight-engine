Somewhere after 40, many people notice a new little flap of skin under an arm, a mole that was not there before, or a small, dark, slightly raised patch appearing out of nowhere. The instinct is often to either ignore it completely or worry about it far more than necessary, and both responses are understandable when nobody has actually explained what these changes usually mean.

This article walks through the common new skin growths that show up with age, what causes them, and the specific features that are actually worth getting a doctor to look at, rather than leaving you to guess.

## What are skin tags, and why do they suddenly show up after 40?

**Skin tags are small, soft, usually skin-coloured growths of excess skin, typically appearing where skin rubs against skin or clothing**, such as the neck, armpits, under the breasts, groin folds, and eyelids. They are made of normal skin tissue and blood vessels and are not a type of cancer or a sign of disease in the vast majority of cases.

They become more common after 40 for a mix of reasons: skin loses some of its elasticity and structural support with age, repeated friction over decades leaves its mark, and factors like excess weight and insulin resistance are linked to a higher number of skin tags, though plenty of people with no metabolic issues at all get them too. **Skin tags do not need to be removed for medical reasons**, but many people choose to have a bothersome or frequently irritated one removed by a doctor using a simple, quick in-clinic procedure, rather than attempting to tie or cut one off at home, which risks bleeding and infection.

## Are new moles after 40 something to worry about?

**Most new moles that appear in midlife are still ordinary, benign moles, and having moles in general is extremely common.** That said, the pattern of when moles typically appear does shift with age: most moles form in childhood and early adulthood, so a genuinely new mole appearing well into your 40s, 50s, or beyond is slightly more worth paying attention to than one that has clearly been there for decades without change, simply because the baseline rate of new mole formation slows down with age.

### The ABCDE check for any mole that concerns you

Dermatologists commonly use a simple checklist, often called the ABCDE rule, to flag moles that deserve a closer professional look:

- **A**symmetry: one half of the mole looks noticeably different from the other half
- **B**order: edges are irregular, notched, or blurred rather than smooth
- **C**olour: uneven colour, or multiple shades of brown, black, red, or blue within one spot
- **D**iameter: larger than about 6 millimetres, roughly the size of a pencil eraser, though some concerning moles can be smaller
- **E**volving: any change in size, shape, colour, or symptoms (itching, bleeding) over weeks to months

**A mole that ticks even one or two of these boxes is not an automatic diagnosis of anything serious, but it is a clear signal to get it looked at by a doctor rather than monitoring it yourself indefinitely.**

## What about other new marks, like dark patches or rough spots?

Beyond tags and moles, a few other common age-related skin changes are worth knowing by name, mainly so an unfamiliar spot does not feel alarming by default.

**Seborrheic keratoses** are rough, slightly raised, waxy or scaly patches, often brown or black, that become increasingly common from the 40s onward. They can look concerning because of their dark colour and raised texture, but they are benign growths related to skin ageing, not skin cancer, though a doctor may still want to confirm this on first appearance since they can superficially resemble other, less common conditions.

**Cherry angiomas** are small, bright red or purple round spots, usually just a few millimetres across, made of tiny clustered blood vessels. They are extremely common after 40, harmless, and tend to simply increase in number with age.

## How should you actually keep an eye on your skin?

| Habit | How often | What it catches |
|---|---|---|
| Full-body skin self-check, including back and scalp with a mirror or a family member's help | Every 2 to 3 months | New or changing moles, unusual patches |
| Photograph any mole you want to track | Once, then re-photograph if it seems different | Makes genuine change easier to spot than memory alone |
| Professional skin check by a doctor | Yearly after 40, sooner if you have many moles or a family history of skin cancer | A trained eye catches subtle signs a self-check can miss |
| Sun protection: shade, covering up, sunscreen on exposed skin | Daily, especially given India's high UV exposure for much of the year | Reduces the ongoing risk of new concerning growths over time |

**India's intense sun exposure for much of the year makes sun protection genuinely relevant here**, not just for wrinkles and tanning but because cumulative sun exposure is a known risk factor for skin cancers, even though rates in Indian populations with more melanin are lower than in fair-skinned populations. Covering exposed skin, seeking shade during peak sun hours, and using sunscreen on exposed areas like the face, neck, and hands remain sensible habits regardless of skin tone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I remove a skin tag myself at home?**
It is best not to. Tying off, cutting, or burning a skin tag at home risks bleeding, infection, and scarring, and a doctor can remove one safely and quickly in a routine clinic visit if it bothers you.

**I have dozens of moles. Do I need to get every single one checked?**
Not individually, but it is worth having a doctor do a full skin check periodically, especially if you have many moles, since they are better placed to scan for any one spot that looks different from the rest, a pattern doctors sometimes call the "ugly duckling" sign.

**Are dark-skinned people at lower risk of skin cancer and can skip these checks?**
Risk is generally lower with more skin melanin, but it is not zero, and skin cancers in darker skin are sometimes caught later because they are less expected, which can make outcomes worse. A yearly check and basic self-awareness are still worthwhile regardless of skin tone.

**Does a mole that suddenly starts itching always mean something serious?**
Not always, itching alone is a fairly common and usually harmless occurrence in ordinary moles. But itching combined with other changes, such as the mole also growing, changing colour, or bleeding, is a stronger signal to get it checked.

Most of what shows up on your skin after 40 is simply a visible record of time passing, nothing more concerning than that. The habit worth building is not anxiety about every new spot, but a simple, periodic look at your own skin and a willingness to let a doctor settle any genuine doubt quickly, rather than carrying that uncertainty around for months.
