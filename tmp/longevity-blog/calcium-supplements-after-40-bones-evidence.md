Calcium is probably the first supplement most Indians over 40 think of when bone health comes up, usually after a doctor mentions bone density or a relative breaks a hip. But calcium supplements are not as simple as "more is better." The evidence on who actually needs them, and what can go wrong if you overdo it, is more nuanced than most pharmacy counters let on.

This article looks at what calcium supplements can realistically do for your bones after 40, who genuinely needs one, and why the current medical advice has shifted away from routine calcium pills for everyone.

## Do most Indian adults over 40 actually need a calcium supplement?

**Not automatically.** A large share of the Indian diet, when it includes dairy, sesame (til), ragi, and leafy vegetables, can supply reasonable amounts of calcium without any pill. The bigger and more common gap in Indian adults is actually vitamin D, not calcium itself, because without enough vitamin D, the body cannot absorb the calcium you already eat efficiently.

Doctors generally consider a calcium supplement when someone's diet is genuinely low in calcium-rich foods (someone who avoids dairy, eats very little greens or sesame, and has limited sun exposure), or when a bone scan or blood test flags an actual deficiency. Taking a calcium tablet "just in case" is not what current medical guidance supports.

## What changed in the medical advice on calcium supplements?

For years, calcium pills were almost a reflex recommendation for anyone over 40, especially women heading toward or past menopause. That advice has shifted meaningfully over the past decade.

**Large reviews of trial data found that routine calcium supplementation in healthy adults produced only a small reduction in fracture risk, and in some analyses, none at all**, once food-based calcium intake was already adequate. At the same time, several studies linked higher-dose calcium supplements (especially without food) to a measurably increased risk of kidney stones, and some researchers have raised questions about a possible association with cardiovascular events, though this remains debated and not conclusively settled. This doesn't mean calcium itself is bad; it means "take it to be safe" has been replaced with "take it only if you actually need it."

### How much calcium does an adult over 40 actually need?

Indian dietary guidelines (ICMR-NIN) suggest roughly 1,000 mg of calcium a day for most adults, rising somewhat after menopause and for older adults generally, though figures vary by source. The practical way to think about it is less about hitting an exact number daily and more about eating calcium-containing foods regularly across the week.

| Food (common Indian serving) | Approximate calcium contribution |
|---|---|
| A glass of milk or a cup of curd | Moderate to high |
| A tablespoon of sesame seeds (til) or til-based chikki | Moderate, often underestimated |
| A cup of cooked ragi (finger millet) porridge or roti | Moderate |
| A cup of cooked leafy greens (methi, amaranth, drumstick leaves) | Low to moderate, but adds up over the week |
| Paneer or tofu | Moderate |

## Who should actually talk to a doctor about a calcium supplement?

**People with diagnosed low bone density, a prior fragility fracture, limited dairy intake, or a known malabsorption condition are the groups where a doctor is more likely to recommend supplementation**, often alongside vitamin D. Anyone taking calcium along with other medicines (for thyroid conditions, certain antibiotics, or iron) should check timing with a doctor, since calcium can interfere with how well some medicines absorb if taken too close together.

**Postmenopausal women and men over 60 are the groups most often discussed in bone health conversations**, but age and sex alone are not a reason to self-prescribe a supplement. A simple blood test and, where indicated, a bone density scan give a doctor far more useful information than guessing.

### What does India's supplement market mean for calcium pills?

India's supplement market is loosely regulated compared to pharmaceutical drugs, so the purity and actual calcium content of over-the-counter products can vary between brands. This is one more reason routine, unsupervised high-dose supplementation isn't advisable: if you do need a supplement, discuss the right form and a trustworthy source with your doctor rather than picking one off a pharmacy shelf based on advertising.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I get enough calcium without dairy if I'm vegan or lactose intolerant?**
Yes, though it takes more deliberate planning. Sesame seeds, ragi, soy products like tofu, and certain leafy greens can meaningfully contribute to calcium intake, and a doctor or dietitian can help assess whether a supplement is still needed on top of these foods.

**Is it true that too much calcium can actually harm your heart?**
Some studies suggest a possible link between high-dose calcium supplements and cardiovascular risk, though evidence is mixed and not definitive. It is one more reason doctors favour getting calcium from food where possible.

**Should I take calcium and vitamin D together?**
Often yes, since vitamin D helps your body absorb calcium effectively, but the right combination and dose depends on your individual levels, which a simple blood test can clarify. Do not combine supplements without checking with your doctor first, especially if you take other regular medication.

**Does calcium supplementation prevent osteoporosis on its own?**
Not reliably by itself. Bone health depends on a combination of adequate calcium and vitamin D, regular weight-bearing and resistance exercise, and, for some people, specific bone-protective medication, so a calcium pill alone is not a complete strategy.

Calcium matters for your bones, but the most useful thing you can do after 40 is find out, with an actual test rather than a guess, whether you have a genuine gap worth supplementing, and build the rest of your bone health around food, movement, and sunlight.
