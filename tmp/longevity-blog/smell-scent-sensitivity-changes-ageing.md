Someone's perfume that never used to bother you now gives you an instant headache. Incense at a temple feels overwhelming rather than calming. A family member's aftershave in a closed car makes you want to open every window. If strong smells seem to affect you more than they used to, you are noticing a real and fairly common shift, and it is not simply about becoming "fussier" with age.

The sense of smell changes in genuinely interesting and sometimes contradictory ways as people get older, and understanding the actual pattern, rather than assuming it is either nothing or something worrying, helps put the experience in perspective.

## Does smell actually get weaker or stronger with age? It seems to be both

**This is a genuinely fair observation, because both things are true, just for different aspects of smell.** The number of functioning smell receptors in the nose does decline gradually with age, and by the 60s and 70s many people have measurably reduced ability to detect faint or subtle odours, something researchers call age-related hyposmia. At the same time, a separate and less discussed phenomenon means that strong, synthetic smells, heavy perfumes, air fresheners, and certain cleaning products, can feel more irritating and overwhelming than they used to, even as overall smell sensitivity is declining.

**The likely explanation is less about the nose itself and more about how the brain filters and interprets sensory information as it ages.** With fewer competing weak signals to process, a strong, concentrated smell may stand out more sharply and trigger a stronger reaction, including headaches, nausea, or a feeling of being overwhelmed, particularly in enclosed spaces like lifts, cars, or crowded rooms where the smell has nowhere to dissipate.

## What role do hormones play in this, particularly for women?

**Hormonal fluctuations around perimenopause and menopause are a well-documented contributor to changed smell sensitivity in women**, with some studies finding both heightened sensitivity to certain smells and, separately, changes in the ability to detect others during this transition. This is one more example of how the hormonal shifts of midlife extend beyond the more commonly discussed symptoms like hot flushes or mood changes into less obvious sensory experiences that can feel confusing without context.

**Reduced saliva production and dry sinuses, both more common with age and with certain medications, can also change how smells and tastes are perceived**, since smell and taste work closely together and a dry nasal passage processes odour molecules differently than a well-hydrated one.

## When is a change in smell just ageing, and when does it need medical attention?

**A gradual decline in detecting faint smells over years, or a mild increase in sensitivity to strong fragrances, fits the pattern of normal ageing and generally does not need investigation on its own.** A sudden, significant loss of smell, however, particularly one that happens over days rather than years, is a different matter and is genuinely worth a doctor's attention, since it can be linked to sinus conditions, certain medications, nutritional deficiencies, or, in some cases, early neurological changes that are worth ruling out.

| Pattern | Likely explanation | Worth a doctor's visit? |
|---|---|---|
| Gradual decline over years in detecting faint smells | Normal age-related change in smell receptors | Usually not, unless it affects appetite or safety (e.g. detecting gas leaks) |
| Increased irritation from strong perfumes or fragrances | Changes in sensory filtering, sometimes hormonal | No, but adjusting fragrance choices around you can help |
| Sudden loss of smell over days | Sinus infection, certain medications, other causes | Yes, worth checking promptly |
| Smell changes alongside memory or balance concerns | Warrants a broader neurological conversation | Yes, discuss with a doctor |

## Why does this matter beyond day-to-day comfort?

**Smell is more closely tied to memory, appetite, and safety than most people realise, which is part of why changes in it deserve some attention rather than being dismissed as trivial.** A reduced sense of smell can blunt appetite in older adults, since much of what we perceive as flavour actually comes from smell rather than taste alone, occasionally contributing to reduced food intake or interest in meals. It also affects the ability to detect warning smells like gas leaks or spoiled food, which is a genuine practical safety consideration worth being mindful of, particularly for people living alone.

**On the other end, heightened sensitivity to strong smells is rarely dangerous but can meaningfully affect quality of life**, contributing to headaches, nausea, or simply avoiding social situations like crowded religious gatherings or family functions where strong incense or perfume is common. Recognising this as a real physiological change, rather than something to feel embarrassed or overly sensitive about, makes it easier to ask for reasonable accommodations.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to suddenly dislike a perfume I used to enjoy wearing myself?**
Yes, this is a common experience as smell sensitivity changes with age and hormones, and many people find their own fragrance preferences shift toward lighter, less concentrated scents over time. It does not indicate anything is wrong.

**Should I be worried if I cannot smell as well as I used to?**
A gradual decline over years is usually a normal part of ageing and not typically concerning on its own. A sudden or significant loss, especially over days, or one accompanied by other symptoms, is worth discussing with a doctor.

**Can medications affect sense of smell?**
Yes, several common medications, including some blood pressure medicines and certain antibiotics, can temporarily or occasionally more lastingly affect smell and taste. If you notice a change after starting a new medication, mention it to your doctor rather than assuming it is unrelated.

**Is there anything that actually helps restore a declining sense of smell?**
There is no guaranteed way to reverse age-related decline, but addressing underlying causes like chronic sinus congestion, ensuring good hydration, and treating any nutritional deficiencies can sometimes improve it. A doctor can help identify whether an underlying, treatable cause is contributing.

Changes in how you experience smell, whether things feel more muted or, paradoxically, more overwhelming, are a genuine and common part of ageing rather than something to dismiss or feel self-conscious about. A little awareness, a few practical adjustments, and a doctor's opinion for anything sudden or significant cover most of what you actually need to manage it well.
