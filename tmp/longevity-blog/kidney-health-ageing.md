Most people can name at least one habit that is good for their heart, and probably one that is good for their brain. Far fewer can say the same for their kidneys, even though these two fist-sized organs quietly filter something like 150 litres of blood a day, regulate blood pressure, and manage the body's fluid and mineral balance.

Kidney function tends to decline gradually with age, and unlike a heart attack or a stroke, kidney damage is often silent until it is fairly advanced. That makes kidney health a genuine, underappreciated piece of the longevity picture, particularly in India, where diabetes and high blood pressure, the two leading causes of kidney disease, are extremely common.

## Why does kidney function decline with age, even without disease?

**Kidney function tends to fall gradually after around age 40**, even in people without any diagnosed kidney disease, as part of normal ageing. The filtering units inside the kidneys, called nephrons, reduce in number and efficiency over decades, and blood flow to the kidneys also decreases somewhat with age. This decline is usually slow and does not, by itself, cause symptoms or require treatment. The concern is when this natural slow decline combines with a condition that accelerates kidney damage, since the two together can push someone toward clinically significant kidney disease well before it would otherwise become a problem.

## What actually damages the kidneys over time?

**Diabetes and high blood pressure are, by a wide margin, the two biggest drivers of kidney damage**, together responsible for the majority of chronic kidney disease cases worldwide, including in India. High blood sugar damages the small blood vessels inside the kidneys' filtering units over years, while high blood pressure forces the kidneys to work under constant excess strain. Both are frequently under-treated or diagnosed late in India, which is part of why kidney disease rates are a genuine public health concern here.

### Common habits that quietly strain the kidneys

A few other everyday habits matter more than most people realise. **Regular, long-term use of certain over-the-counter painkillers, particularly NSAIDs such as ibuprofen or diclofenac, can gradually reduce kidney function**, especially when used frequently for chronic pain rather than occasionally for acute pain. Chronic dehydration also places extra strain on the kidneys over time by concentrating the substances they need to filter. High salt intake, common in much of Indian cooking and heavily processed snack foods, contributes to high blood pressure, which circles back to kidney strain. And excessive, unsupervised use of high-dose protein supplements, increasingly popular among people trying to build muscle after 40, can be a genuine concern for people who already have reduced kidney function, though it is not a risk for someone with healthy kidneys eating a reasonable amount of dietary protein.

## Which tests actually matter, and how often?

Kidney function is checked through simple, inexpensive blood and urine tests, not anything invasive. **A blood test called serum creatinine, used to estimate a number called eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate), and a urine test for a protein called albumin are the two main tests that matter.**

| Test | What it checks | Who should get it, and how often |
|---|---|---|
| Serum creatinine / eGFR | Overall kidney filtering function | All adults over 40 as part of an annual checkup |
| Urine albumin (microalbuminuria) | Early kidney damage before eGFR drops | Anyone with diabetes or high blood pressure, annually |
| Blood pressure check | Major driver of kidney strain | Every checkup, more often if already elevated |
| HbA1c (blood sugar) | Diabetes control, a major kidney risk factor | Annually, more often if diabetic or prediabetic |

## What actually protects kidney health as you age?

**Keeping blood pressure and blood sugar well controlled is the single biggest thing most people can do for long-term kidney health**, and this usually matters more than any specific food or supplement. Beyond that, staying well hydrated, using painkillers only as needed rather than as a routine daily habit, keeping salt intake moderate, and maintaining a healthy body weight all genuinely help. For people who already have reduced kidney function, protein intake needs to be individualised with a doctor's guidance rather than following general muscle-building advice, since very high protein intake can add strain to kidneys that are already compromised.

### Who needs closer monitoring

People with diabetes, high blood pressure, a family history of kidney disease, or who are on long-term medication that affects the kidneys should be tested more often than the general population, typically at least once a year, sometimes more, based on their doctor's advice. Kidney disease in its early stages is often reversible or at least manageable in a way that prevents further decline, which is exactly why catching it early through routine testing matters so much more than trying to treat it once it becomes advanced.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can kidney damage be reversed once it starts?**
Early-stage kidney damage can often be stabilised or even partly improved by treating the underlying cause, such as better blood pressure or blood sugar control. More advanced kidney disease is generally managed to slow further decline rather than reversed, which is why early detection matters so much.

**Is drinking more water always good for the kidneys?**
For most healthy people, staying well hydrated genuinely supports kidney function. However, people with certain existing kidney conditions or heart failure may be advised to limit fluids, so this should be guided by a doctor rather than assumed to be universally true.

**Does eating a high-protein diet damage healthy kidneys?**
For people with normal kidney function, a reasonably high-protein diet, including the increased protein needs common after 40, is not known to cause kidney damage. The concern applies mainly to people who already have reduced kidney function, where protein intake should be discussed with a doctor.

**Are herbal or Ayurvedic remedies safe for kidney health?**
Some traditional Ayurvedic approaches emphasise hydration and dietary balance that can genuinely support general health, but certain herbal preparations, particularly unregulated ones, have actually been linked to kidney harm in some cases. Any Ayurvedic approach to kidney health should be guided by a qualified practitioner working alongside, not instead of, a doctor monitoring kidney function.

Kidney health rarely announces itself early, which is exactly the problem. A simple annual blood and urine test, alongside the same blood pressure and blood sugar habits that protect the heart and brain, does most of the protective work that matters here.
