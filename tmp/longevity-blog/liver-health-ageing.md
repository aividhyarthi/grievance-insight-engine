Ask most people to name the organs they worry about as they age, and the liver rarely makes the list. The heart gets attention, the knees get attention, even the eyes get attention, but the liver quietly does over 500 jobs, filtering toxins, processing every medication you take, storing energy, making the proteins that clot your blood, and regulating cholesterol, without ever really announcing when something is going wrong. That silence is the problem. By the time liver disease causes symptoms, a significant portion of the organ has often already been affected.

This article looks at what actually happens to the liver as we age, why fatty liver disease has become so common among Indians who don't drink at all, and what genuinely protects this organ over the decades.

## Why does the liver matter so much for healthy ageing?

**The liver is your body's processing plant, and everything passes through it.** Every medication, every supplement, most of the alcohol you drink, and much of the fat and sugar you eat gets filtered and processed by this one organ. When the liver is working well, it clears toxins efficiently, keeps blood sugar and cholesterol in a healthy range, and helps your body use nutrients properly. When it's under strain, that filtering slows down, and the effects ripple outward: fatigue, poor blood sugar control, higher cardiovascular risk, and reduced ability to safely process the medications older adults often need for other conditions.

Ageing itself changes the liver somewhat, blood flow to it decreases gradually, and it becomes slightly less efficient at metabolising certain drugs. This is one reason doctors sometimes adjust medication doses for older patients. But most of the serious liver problems seen in midlife and beyond aren't caused by ageing itself, they're caused by decades of accumulated strain that ageing simply reveals.

## What is fatty liver disease, and why is it so common now, even in people who don't drink?

**Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously called non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, happens when fat builds up inside liver cells.** It's strongly linked to excess weight (particularly abdominal fat), insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and high triglycerides, the same cluster of issues that drives heart disease. Indian population studies have found MASLD in a striking proportion of urban adults, including many who are not conventionally "overweight" by international standards but carry excess fat around the abdomen and organs, a pattern researchers sometimes call the Asian phenotype of metabolic risk.

Left unmanaged for years, this fat buildup can progress to inflammation (steatohepatitis), scarring (fibrosis), and eventually cirrhosis, though this progression is slow and, importantly, often reversible in its earlier stages with sustained lifestyle change.

## Does moderate drinking still deserve caution as you age?

**Alcohol remains one of the clearest, most direct causes of liver damage, and the liver's capacity to process it doesn't improve with age.** If anything, body composition changes after 40 (less muscle, relatively more fat) mean the same amount of alcohol tends to affect the body more. This doesn't mean occasional drinking is automatically dangerous for someone with an otherwise healthy liver, but it does mean the margin for "safe" drinking narrows somewhat with age, and combining regular drinking with excess weight or diabetes compounds the risk considerably. If you already have any degree of fatty liver, a doctor will generally recommend cutting alcohol out entirely while the liver recovers.

## What actually protects your liver as you get older?

The good news is that the liver is remarkably capable of healing itself, more so than almost any other organ, provided the underlying strain is reduced. **Weight management, even a modest reduction of 7-10% of body weight, has been shown to meaningfully reduce liver fat in people with MASLD.** Beyond that:

- **Move regularly.** Both aerobic activity and resistance training reduce liver fat independent of weight loss.
- **Cut back on added sugar and refined carbohydrates**, particularly sugary beverages, which are strongly linked to fat accumulation in the liver.
- **Be cautious with supplements and over-the-counter medication.** Several herbal and dietary supplements marketed for weight loss, bodybuilding, or general wellness have been linked to liver injury, and excessive use of common painkillers can also strain the liver. Always mention every supplement you take to your doctor.
- **Manage blood sugar and blood pressure**, since both are closely tied to liver health.
- **Get vaccinated against hepatitis A and B if you haven't been**, particularly relevant for anyone with existing liver risk factors.

## When should you get your liver checked?

Liver function tests are inexpensive, widely available, and often included in routine health checkups, yet many people never have one until something else prompts it. Given how silent liver disease can be, it's worth asking your doctor about liver function tests as part of your regular checkup after 40, especially if you carry excess weight around the abdomen, have diabetes or prediabetes, or drink regularly.

| Risk factor | Why it matters | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Abdominal obesity | Strongly linked to fat accumulation in the liver | Track waist circumference, aim for gradual weight loss |
| Type 2 diabetes or prediabetes | Shares underlying insulin resistance with fatty liver | Get liver function tests alongside blood sugar monitoring |
| Regular alcohol use | Direct toxic effect on liver cells | Discuss safe limits with your doctor; consider periods of abstinence |
| Unsupervised supplement use | Some herbal and weight-loss products cause liver injury | Disclose all supplements to your doctor before starting them |
| Family history of liver disease | May indicate genetic risk (e.g. haemochromatosis) | Mention this during checkups; earlier screening may be advised |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a fatty liver actually reverse itself?**
Yes, in its earlier stages. Fat accumulation in the liver without significant scarring can improve substantially with sustained weight loss, regular exercise, and dietary changes, often within six months to a year. Once significant scarring (fibrosis) has developed, reversal becomes harder, which is why early detection matters.

**Do I need to avoid alcohol completely to protect my liver?**
Not necessarily, unless you already have liver disease, in which case your doctor will likely recommend complete abstinence. For someone without existing liver problems, the concern is more about regular, heavy, or binge drinking rather than occasional moderate consumption, though there's no drinking level that's entirely risk-free for the liver.

**Are turmeric or other Ayurvedic herbs good for the liver?**
Certain traditional herbs have a long history of use for digestive and liver support in Ayurveda, and some have been studied for antioxidant properties. However, self-medicating with concentrated herbal extracts can sometimes stress the liver rather than help it, particularly at high doses. Anyone interested in Ayurvedic approaches to liver health should consult a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner rather than self-prescribing, and always inform their regular doctor about anything they're taking.

**What are the early warning signs of liver trouble?**
Early fatty liver disease typically causes no symptoms at all, which is why blood tests matter more than symptom-watching. When symptoms do appear, they tend to be vague: fatigue, mild discomfort in the upper right abdomen, or unexplained weight loss. Yellowing of the skin or eyes is a later sign that warrants immediate medical attention.

Your liver has been working without complaint for decades, quietly processing everything you've eaten, drunk, and taken. Giving it a little attention now, through movement, a sensible diet, moderate habits, and a periodic blood test, is one of the more overlooked ways to protect your long-term health.
