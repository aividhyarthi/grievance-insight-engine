If you have ever downloaded an app promising to "train your brain" and sharpen your memory in a few minutes a day, you are far from alone. Brain-training games have become a genuine industry over the past two decades, built on an appealing idea: that practising puzzles can slow down the kind of memory lapses many people start noticing after 40. The research tells a more complicated story than the marketing does, and it is worth understanding before you decide how much time and money to put into these apps.

## What do brain-training apps actually claim to do?

**Most commercial brain-training platforms are built around short daily sessions of puzzles targeting memory, attention, processing speed, or problem-solving.** The underlying pitch is intuitive: if you can strengthen a muscle with repeated exercise, surely you can strengthen cognitive abilities the same way. Early studies in the 2000s and 2010s, often funded or run by the companies selling these apps, reported encouraging improvements, which fuelled rapid growth in the category.

**Larger, independently funded trials since then have told a less encouraging story.** A major multi-year study called ACTIVE, along with several independent reviews, found that while participants reliably got better at the trained tasks themselves, this improvement rarely extended to everyday cognitive abilities like remembering where you left your keys, following a complex conversation, or managing multiple tasks at once. This gap between practised-task improvement and real-world benefit is sometimes called narrow transfer, and it is the central weakness in the brain-training industry's broader claims.

## Why does narrow transfer happen?

**Your brain is very good at getting efficient at specific, repeated tasks, which is not the same as becoming generally sharper.** Think of it like practising a specific crossword puzzle format for months. You will likely get much faster and more accurate at that exact puzzle style, but this skill does not automatically make you better at unrelated cognitive challenges, like remembering a new colleague's name or following unfamiliar driving directions. Several brain-training apps have faced regulatory scrutiny in the United States over exaggerated marketing claims, which reflects how far the science has lagged behind the advertising in this space.

## So is there any value in playing them at all?

**Possibly, but for different reasons than the marketing suggests.** If you genuinely enjoy the puzzles, playing them can be a pleasant, low-stakes mental activity, similar to crosswords or sudoku, which some people find satisfying and mildly stimulating. There is nothing wrong with that as a hobby. The concern is treating these apps as a substitute for habits with much stronger evidence behind them, or spending significant money on subscriptions under the belief that they are meaningfully protecting you against future memory decline.

## What this means for how you spend your time

**If a 10-minute daily brain-training session genuinely brings you enjoyment, there is no harm in continuing it as a hobby.** But if the goal is actually protecting your memory and cognitive health as you age, the stronger move is to prioritise regular physical activity, consistent sleep, real social interaction, and taking up something new and genuinely challenging, whether that is a language, an instrument, or a skill you have always wanted to try. These habits do not come packaged as neatly as an app with a subscription fee, but the evidence behind them is considerably more solid.

It is also worth being clear about what brain-training apps are not designed to address. **If you or a family member are noticing memory lapses that are new, progressively worsening, or starting to interfere with daily tasks like managing finances or medications, that needs a doctor's evaluation, not a puzzle app.** Normal age-related forgetfulness, misplacing keys occasionally or momentarily losing a word, is different from a pattern that is clearly getting worse over months, and only a proper medical assessment can tell the difference reliably.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do brain-training apps help prevent dementia?**
There is no good evidence that commercial brain-training apps prevent dementia. Some observational research suggests that generally staying cognitively, physically, and socially active across life is associated with lower dementia risk, but this is quite different from a specific app-based training program causing that protection.

**Are any brain-training apps actually backed by solid research?**
A few academic research platforms, distinct from most commercial consumer apps, have published more rigorous trial data, though results remain mixed and narrow transfer is still a common finding even in these more carefully studied programs. Treat marketing claims of "clinically proven" with caution and look for independently published, peer-reviewed evidence specifically.

**Is it better to do puzzles like sudoku instead of an app?**
Sudoku, crosswords, and brain-training apps likely work similarly, since they all tend to produce narrow improvement in the specific puzzle type rather than broad cognitive benefit. Enjoy whichever format you like, but do not rely on either as your main strategy for protecting memory.

**What is the single best thing I can do for my memory after 40?**
No single habit works alone, but regular aerobic exercise consistently shows some of the strongest, most replicated evidence across large studies, alongside good sleep and genuine social connection. Combining several of these habits appears to matter more than any one in isolation.

Brain-training apps are not harmful, and there is nothing wrong with enjoying them if puzzles are genuinely fun for you. Just do not let a subscription fee stand in for the less flashy habits, movement, sleep, learning, and connection, that the evidence actually supports.
