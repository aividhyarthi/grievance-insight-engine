A cold or flu usually finishes its course in a week or two, cough and all. What catches people off guard after 40 is the cough that outlasts everything else, the one that hangs around for a month after the rest of the illness is gone, or the one that seems to have no obvious trigger at all. It is one of the more common reasons for a doctor's visit in midlife, and it almost always has an identifiable cause.

This article walks through what typically explains a cough that will not quit after 40, how to tell the common causes apart, and the specific warning signs that mean it needs medical attention rather than another round of home remedies.

## Why does a cough linger for weeks after 40?

**A cough that outlasts an infection by weeks is being kept alive by something other than the original illness.** Doctors generally define a cough as chronic once it has lasted more than eight weeks, and as subacute between three and eight weeks, and after 40 the most common drivers shift away from infection and toward four specific conditions: post-nasal drip, acid reflux, asthma, and medication side effects.

**Post-nasal drip is the single most common cause, and it is easy to miss.** Allergies, chronic sinus congestion, or simply drier indoor air from air conditioning can cause mucus to drip down the back of the throat continuously, triggering a reflexive cough that is often worse first thing in the morning or after lying down. It frequently comes with a feeling of needing to clear your throat constantly, even without an obvious runny nose.

### Could it be reflux instead of your lungs?

**Acid reflux causes cough far more often than people realise, sometimes without any heartburn at all.** Stomach acid reaching the lower oesophagus, or even tiny amounts reaching the throat, can trigger a cough reflex as a protective response. This type of cough is often worse at night or after large meals, and it can exist even in people who never feel the classic burning sensation of reflux, which is why it gets missed for months.

## Could a medicine you are already taking be the cause?

**A specific group of blood pressure medications, called ACE inhibitors, causes a persistent dry cough in a meaningful minority of people who take them.** This cough can start anytime from days to months after beginning the medicine, and it typically does not respond to cough syrups because it is not caused by mucus or infection at all. If you started a new blood pressure medicine around the time your cough began, that timing is worth mentioning to your doctor directly, since switching to a different class of medicine usually resolves it.

## How a doctor usually works through it

**Sorting out a chronic cough is more detective work than guesswork, and it follows a fairly standard pattern.** A doctor will typically start with your history, including when the cough started, what makes it better or worse, and your current medicines, before ordering any tests.

| Step | What it checks for | Typical next step |
|---|---|---|
| Review of medicines and timing | ACE inhibitor-related cough | Trial switch to a different blood pressure medicine class |
| Chest X-ray | Infection, lung changes, structural issues | Further imaging or specialist referral if abnormal |
| Trial of allergy or reflux treatment | Post-nasal drip or acid reflux as the cause | Cough typically improves within two to four weeks if that was the cause |
| Breathing test (spirometry) | Adult-onset asthma or airway narrowing | Inhaler therapy if confirmed |
| Further evaluation if cough persists | Less common causes requiring specialist input | Referral to a pulmonologist or ENT specialist |

## When does a cough need urgent attention?

**Most lingering coughs are uncomfortable rather than dangerous, but a few signs change that.** Coughing up blood, even a small streak, always warrants prompt medical evaluation. The same goes for a cough paired with unexplained weight loss, night sweats, a fever that will not resolve after several days, or noticeable breathlessness with ordinary activity. If any of these accompany chest pain, sudden severe breathlessness, or bluish lips, treat it as an emergency and call 112 rather than waiting for a scheduled appointment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long is too long for a cough to last before I should see a doctor?**
A cough lasting more than three weeks, especially one that is not steadily improving, is worth discussing with a doctor. You do not need to wait the full eight weeks that defines a "chronic" cough medically before getting it checked.

**Can air pollution in Indian cities cause a chronic cough on its own?**
Yes, chronic exposure to poor air quality can irritate the airways and contribute to a persistent cough or worsen underlying asthma, particularly during high-pollution months. It is worth mentioning your local air quality and any seasonal pattern to your doctor.

**Why does cough syrup not seem to help my cough at all?**
Most over-the-counter cough syrups target the cough reflex itself rather than its underlying cause. If the real driver is reflux, allergies, or a medication side effect, the cough typically will not improve much until that root cause is addressed directly.

**Is a chronic cough ever just normal ageing with no real cause?**
No. While coughing can become slightly more frequent with changes in swallowing reflexes and lung elasticity with age, a cough lasting weeks is not simply something to accept as normal and almost always has an identifiable, treatable cause.

A cough that has overstayed its welcome is rarely mysterious once someone actually looks for the cause. Working through the short list of usual suspects, rather than reaching for another bottle of syrup, is usually what finally quiets it down.
