Standing up from a low chair and hearing your knees crackle like dry leaves. Climbing stairs and noticing your hips click with each step. For many people, these sounds first show up, or first start getting noticed, somewhere after 40, and the instinctive reaction is often alarm, as if the joint is grinding itself apart. In the vast majority of cases, that fear is misplaced.

This article explains what actually causes joints to crack and pop, called crepitus, why it becomes more noticeable with age, and the specific situations where it is genuinely worth a doctor's attention rather than a shrug.

## What is actually making that sound?

**Most joint cracking comes from one of two harmless mechanisms, not bone grinding on bone.** The first is the collapse of tiny gas bubbles, mostly carbon dioxide, that form in the fluid surrounding a joint when it is stretched or moved a certain way, the same basic mechanism behind the classic knuckle crack. The second is the movement of a tendon or ligament sliding slightly over a bony surface as a joint moves through its range, producing a snapping or clicking sensation that is entirely mechanical and not damaging.

**A third, less common source is actual cartilage roughness**, where a joint surface that has become slightly uneven, often from early osteoarthritis, produces a grating or grinding sensation during movement. This type tends to come with other signs, discussed below, rather than appearing in isolation.

### Why does it seem to happen more after 40?

**Joint fluid composition and the elasticity of surrounding ligaments and tendons change gradually with age**, which appears to make the gas-bubble and tendon-snapping mechanisms both more frequent and more audible. At the same time, people in their 40s and beyond are simply paying closer attention to their bodies and are more likely to notice sounds that may well have been present, just unremarked upon, in their 20s and 30s.

## Is cracking the same thing as arthritis?

**No, and this is the most important distinction to understand.** Crepitus is a sound or sensation. Osteoarthritis is a specific, diagnosable condition involving cartilage breakdown, joint space narrowing, and often pain and stiffness, confirmed through a physical examination and sometimes imaging. Many people with loud, frequent joint cracking have perfectly healthy joints on examination, while some people with significant osteoarthritis have joints that barely make a sound at all. The sound alone tells you very little; it is the accompanying symptoms that matter.

## Does cracking your knuckles or joints on purpose cause arthritis?

**This is one of the most persistent myths about joint cracking, and research has not supported it.** Several studies, including long-running observational research comparing habitual knuckle-crackers against non-crackers, have found no meaningful difference in rates of arthritis between the two groups. Deliberately cracking a joint is generally considered a harmless habit rather than a damaging one, though cracking a joint forcefully or in an unnatural direction to the point of pain is a different matter and not advisable.

## What actually helps, if the sound bothers you?

**Strengthening the muscles around a noisy joint often reduces both the sound and any accompanying mild discomfort**, since well-conditioned muscles and tendons move more smoothly through a joint's range than weak, tight ones. This is particularly relevant for knees, where quadriceps strength has a well-documented relationship with knee comfort and function.

- Regular, gentle mobility work (controlled stretching, not aggressive forcing) keeps tendons gliding smoothly
- Strength training for the muscles supporting the joint, two to three sessions a week, is well supported by evidence
- Maintaining a healthy body weight reduces load on weight-bearing joints like the knees and hips
- Warming up before exercise reduces the abruptness of tendon snapping during activity

## When should you actually see a doctor?

| Sign | What it may suggest | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Painless cracking, no other symptoms | Normal gas bubble or tendon movement | No action needed |
| Mild ache after activity, improves with rest | Possible early overuse or mild wear | Monitor, consider strength work |
| Persistent pain with the cracking | Possible cartilage or ligament issue | See a doctor |
| Visible swelling or warmth | Possible inflammation or injury | See a doctor promptly |
| Joint locking, giving way, or instability | Possible structural joint problem | See a doctor without delay |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does cracking your knuckles cause arthritis?**
No. Multiple studies comparing habitual knuckle-crackers to non-crackers have found no meaningful increase in arthritis rates from the habit itself. It is generally considered harmless, though forcing a joint to crack painfully is not advisable.

**Why do my knees crack more when I squat or climb stairs than when I walk?**
Deep bending positions change the angle and tension on tendons and ligaments around the knee, making the snapping or gas-bubble mechanisms more likely to occur and more noticeable than during the smaller range of motion used in walking.

**Can losing weight reduce joint cracking?**
It can help, particularly for weight-bearing joints like the knees and hips, since reduced load changes how joint surfaces and surrounding tissue interact during movement, though it will not necessarily eliminate cracking caused by gas bubbles, which is unrelated to weight.

**Is it possible to have osteoarthritis without any cracking sound at all?**
Yes. Crepitus and osteoarthritis do not always occur together, and plenty of people with diagnosed osteoarthritis report little or no joint sound, which is why doctors rely on pain, stiffness, and examination findings, not sound alone, to make a diagnosis.

For most people, a cracking knee or a popping shoulder after 40 is simply the sound of a joint being a joint, not a warning sign. Paying attention to the symptoms that actually matter, pain, swelling, and movement that is getting worse rather than just louder, is a far more useful habit than worrying about every click.
