Most people think of cataracts as something that simply happens with age, a switch that flips somewhere in your sixties or seventies. There is truth to that, the lens of the eye does cloud gradually over decades. But the pace at which it clouds is not fixed. A number of daily habits, some backed by strong evidence and some by more modest evidence, genuinely slow the process, and a few habits speed it up considerably.

This is not about a miracle eye drop or supplement. It is about a handful of ordinary choices, sun protection, blood sugar control, diet, and smoking status, that add up to meaningfully different odds over a lifetime. Here is what actually holds up when you look at the research, and what you can start doing today.

## What actually causes a cataract to form?

**The lens of your eye is made mostly of protein**, arranged in a precise, transparent structure. Over decades, oxidative stress, essentially wear and tear from free radicals, causes these proteins to unfold and clump together, scattering light instead of letting it pass through cleanly. That clumping is a cataract. It is a near-universal part of ageing, most people over 70 have some degree of lens clouding, but the speed at which it progresses varies enormously between individuals.

Genetics play a role you cannot control. But a large share of the variation comes down to cumulative exposure to things that accelerate oxidative stress in the lens, and that part is genuinely within your influence.

## Does sun exposure really matter that much?

**Ultraviolet light is one of the best-documented accelerants of cataract formation**, particularly a type called cortical cataract. India's intense sunlight, especially at higher altitudes and closer to the equator, means outdoor workers, farmers, and anyone who spends long hours outside without eye protection face meaningfully higher lifetime exposure.

The fix is simple and cheap: sunglasses that specifically state UV400 or "100% UV protection" on the label, not just dark-tinted glasses, which can actually be worse if they cause your pupils to dilate without blocking UV. A wide-brimmed hat adds further protection, especially for the peak sun hours of late morning to mid-afternoon.

## How much does diabetes and blood sugar control matter?

**Poorly controlled blood sugar is one of the strongest risk factors for early cataracts.** High glucose levels cause a buildup of sorbitol inside the lens, which draws in water and disrupts the delicate protein structure faster than normal ageing would. People with poorly managed type 2 diabetes often develop cataracts five to ten years earlier than those without diabetes, and the cataracts can progress faster once they start.

This is one more reason, alongside heart and kidney health, that steady blood sugar control in your 40s and 50s pays off in ways that go well beyond the number on a glucose meter.

## Does diet actually make a difference?

**Antioxidant-rich foods appear to slow lens ageing**, though no single food or supplement reverses damage already done. The nutrients most consistently linked to eye health, lutein, zeaxanthin, and vitamin C, are found in familiar Indian vegetables: spinach, methi, bottle gourd, capsicum, amla, and citrus fruits.

Eating a variety of these regularly, rather than relying on a single "superfood," is the more evidence-backed approach. There is no need for expensive imported produce or supplement capsules, most of what helps is already common in Indian kitchens.

## When should you actually see an eye doctor?

**Early cataracts often cause no symptoms you would notice on your own.** By the time you notice blurring, glare around headlights at night, or colours looking faded, some clouding has already progressed. This is exactly why an annual dilated eye exam after 40 matters, it catches changes long before they affect daily life, and it also screens for glaucoma and diabetic eye changes at the same visit.

If cataracts do progress to the point of affecting vision, the good news is that surgery to replace the clouded lens is one of the most successful and commonly performed procedures in modern medicine, widely available across India at a range of price points including subsidised options at many government and charitable hospitals.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can cataracts be reversed without surgery?**
No. Once the lens proteins have clumped and clouded, no eye drop, diet change, or supplement reverses that damage. The habits described here slow the pace at which cataracts form and progress, but surgery remains the only way to restore vision once a cataract significantly affects sight.

**Are cataracts only a problem for elderly people?**
Cataracts are most common after 60, but they can start forming, often without symptoms, in your 40s and 50s, especially with risk factors like diabetes, heavy sun exposure, or smoking. Some people are also born with or develop cataracts earlier due to injury, certain medications, or other eye conditions.

**Do reading glasses or screen time affect cataract risk?**
Not directly. Reading glasses correct focusing problems unrelated to lens clouding, and there is no strong evidence that screen use itself causes cataracts. Screen time is more linked to digital eye strain and dry eyes, a separate issue worth managing on its own terms.

**Is cataract surgery safe, and how soon should it be done once diagnosed?**
Cataract surgery is generally very safe and highly effective, with most people experiencing significantly improved vision. Timing is a personal decision made with your doctor based on how much the cataract is affecting your daily activities, such as driving or reading, rather than a fixed rule.

Your eyes do a lot of quiet, uncelebrated work every day, and the habits that protect them, a pair of proper sunglasses, steady blood sugar, a plate with some greens on it, are neither expensive nor complicated. Building them in now is a far easier ask than trying to catch up later.
