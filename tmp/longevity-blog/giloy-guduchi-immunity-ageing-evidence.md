If you kept any houseplants during the pandemic years, there is a good chance one of them was giloy. The heart-shaped leaves and climbing stems of this vine, known in Ayurveda as guduchi, suddenly appeared on balconies and terraces across India, boiled into decoctions, sold as juice in supermarkets, and stocked by pharmacies as capsules and tablets. Few herbs made the jump from classical Ayurvedic texts to mainstream household use quite so fast.

That popularity has outlasted the pandemic itself, and many people over 40 now take giloy as a routine part of staying well through the year, particularly across seasonal changes. This article looks at what the herb traditionally claims to do, what current research actually supports, and an important safety point that genuinely deserves more attention than it usually gets.

## What exactly is giloy, and why did it become so popular?

**Giloy is a climbing vine with heart-shaped leaves**, traditionally used in Ayurveda under the name guduchi or amrita. Classical texts describe it as a rasayana, a category of substances believed to support overall vitality, immunity, and resilience against disease rather than treat one specific condition. The stem is the part most commonly used, either as a fresh decoction boiled at home, a commercially bottled juice, or a dried extract in tablet or capsule form.

Its sudden popularity during the COVID-19 pandemic came from word-of-mouth claims that it could boost immunity and help the body fight viral infections, amplified heavily through social media and family WhatsApp groups. **This surge in use happened largely without matching evidence**, which is a pattern worth noticing: a herb can have a genuinely long traditional history and still have its claims outrun the actual research available on it at any given moment.

### What does Ayurveda traditionally say it does?

In classical Ayurvedic thought, guduchi is considered tridosha-shamaka, meaning it is believed to help balance all three doshas rather than target just one, which is part of why it is framed as a general tonic rather than a remedy for a specific ailment. It has traditionally been used for recurring fevers, general weakness, and to support digestion and joint comfort, always as part of a broader approach that included diet, rest, and other herbs tailored to the individual, not as a single herb taken in isolation for everything.

## What does modern research actually show?

**Laboratory and animal studies have found that extracts of Tinospora cordifolia show measurable immune-modulating and anti-inflammatory activity**, along with some antioxidant effects. This is scientifically interesting and is one reason the herb has drawn research attention, but laboratory results do not automatically translate into proven benefits for a person drinking a cup of giloy juice each morning.

**Human clinical trials remain limited in number, size, and quality.** A few small studies have looked at giloy alongside standard treatment for conditions like dengue fever, with some suggesting a possible supportive role in symptom recovery, but these studies are generally too small and too preliminary to be treated as established medical guidance. No large, well-designed trial currently supports using giloy to prevent or treat COVID-19, dengue, or any other specific infection on its own.

## The liver safety concern worth knowing about

**A genuine and specific safety issue emerged during the pandemic years**: doctors in India published case reports of liver injury, in some instances serious, in people who had been taking giloy or guduchi products, often in combination with other supplements or at higher-than-traditional doses. Some of these cases were later linked to confusion with a toxic look-alike plant sold under the same name, which highlights a real quality-control problem in an unregulated herbal market, not necessarily a universal danger from correctly identified guduchi itself.

**This does not mean giloy is inherently unsafe for everyone**, but it is a strong reason to treat it with the same seriousness as any other substance taken regularly: source it from a reputable supplier, avoid self-prescribed high doses, and stop and see a doctor promptly if you notice yellowing of the eyes or skin, dark urine, unusual fatigue, or abdominal pain while using it.

## How should you think about using it responsibly, if at all?

| Question to ask yourself | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Am I using it as a general wellness habit, or to treat an actual illness? | It should never replace diagnosis or treatment for a specific condition such as dengue, fever of unknown cause, or COVID-19 |
| Where is the product sourced from, and is the plant correctly identified? | Misidentified look-alike plants have been linked to liver injury cases |
| Am I combining it with several other herbal or supplement products at once? | Combining multiple unregulated products raises risk and makes it harder to identify the cause if something goes wrong |
| Do I have any existing liver condition, or take regular medication? | These situations call for a doctor's input before starting, not after a problem appears |
| Has a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner actually reviewed my use, or did I start based on a forwarded message? | Personalised guidance accounts for your specific constitution and health history in a way a generic recommendation cannot |

**If you choose to use giloy as part of a general wellness routine, moderation and a known, trustworthy source matter more than the dose claimed on any package.** Treating it as one small part of an overall healthy routine, alongside sleep, diet, and movement, is a far more reasonable framing than expecting it to meaningfully prevent or cure disease on its own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is giloy safe to take every day?**
Long-term daily use has not been well studied in rigorous human trials, and the liver injury case reports mean caution is genuinely warranted. If you want to take it regularly, discuss it with a doctor or qualified Ayurvedic practitioner first, particularly if you have any existing liver condition.

**Can giloy actually cure or prevent dengue or COVID-19?**
No. Some small studies have looked at it as a possible supportive measure alongside standard medical treatment, but there is no reliable evidence that it prevents these infections or replaces proper medical care, including hospital monitoring when a doctor recommends it.

**How is giloy traditionally different from chyawanprash or ashwagandha?**
Each has a distinct traditional role within Ayurveda. Guduchi is framed primarily around immunity and recurring fevers, ashwagandha around stress and vitality, and chyawanprash as a broader multi-herb rasayana for general strength and immunity. They are not interchangeable, and combining several at once without guidance increases both cost and risk without clear added benefit.

**How can I tell if a giloy product is reliable?**
Buy from an established pharmacy or a recognised Ayurvedic brand rather than an unfamiliar roadside seller or an unverified online listing, and avoid products that make specific disease-curing claims, since genuine Ayurvedic practice does not promise that from a single herb.

Giloy's rapid rise from a classical Ayurvedic herb to a pandemic-era household staple says more about how quickly health claims can spread than about how much has actually been proven. There is real, if early, scientific interest in it, and a genuine traditional history behind it, but neither of those things makes it risk-free or a substitute for proper medical care. Used thoughtfully, from a reliable source, and with your doctor or a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner aware of it, it can be one small part of a sensible routine rather than a cure-all.
