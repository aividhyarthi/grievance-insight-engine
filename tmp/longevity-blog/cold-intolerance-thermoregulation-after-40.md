You reach for a shawl while everyone else in the room seems perfectly comfortable, or a room that used to feel pleasantly cool now feels genuinely chilly within minutes. If this has been happening more often since your 40s or 50s, it is not just in your head, and it is not simply about being "thin-blooded." Your body's ability to regulate its own temperature genuinely changes with age, in ways that are well understood and, in most cases, manageable.

## Is it normal to feel colder as you get older?

**Yes, this is a well-documented and genuinely common part of ageing.** Several systems contribute at once. Muscle tissue generates heat as a byproduct of activity and even at rest, and since muscle mass naturally declines with age unless it is actively maintained through strength training, the body simply produces somewhat less internal heat than it used to. Resting metabolic rate also slows gradually with age, which further reduces the baseline amount of heat the body generates throughout the day. **None of this means something is wrong.** It reflects a combination of ordinary physiological changes that affect most people to some degree, though the extent varies quite a bit from person to person.

### The circulation piece

Alongside reduced heat production, the body also becomes somewhat more conservative about where it sends blood. Blood vessels near the skin's surface, particularly in the hands and feet, tend to constrict more readily with age as part of the body's strategy to protect the core temperature around vital organs. **This is why cold hands and feet are often the first and most noticeable sign**, even when the rest of the body feels reasonably comfortable, and why layering that specifically covers the extremities tends to help more than simply adding another layer over the torso.

## What else can make you feel cold more than it should?

**A few specific, checkable conditions are worth knowing about, since they are common and very treatable.** An underactive thyroid, which becomes considerably more common after 40, particularly in women, slows metabolism and is a classic cause of persistent cold intolerance, often alongside fatigue, weight gain, and dry skin. Anaemia, from iron or vitamin B12 deficiency, reduces the blood's capacity to carry oxygen efficiently and commonly shows up as cold hands and feet along with tiredness. **Certain blood pressure medications, and poor circulation from conditions like diabetes, can also increase cold sensitivity**, which is one more reason a full list of your current medications is useful information to bring to any conversation with your doctor about this.

## What actually helps day to day?

**Layering strategically matters more than simply wearing more clothes.** Since the hands, feet, and head lose heat disproportionately, warm socks, gloves, and a light head covering often do more for comfort than an extra sweater over the torso. Natural fibres like wool and cotton layered loosely trap warm air more effectively than a single thick layer.

**Staying physically active is one of the most reliable ways to improve your baseline warmth.** Regular movement, especially strength training that helps maintain muscle mass, directly supports the body's heat-generating capacity over time, while even a short brisk walk provides an immediate, if temporary, warming effect through increased circulation. **This is one more reason strength training remains valuable well into your 60s and beyond, not just for bone and muscle health, but for genuinely feeling warmer in daily life.**

**Eating regularly also supports temperature regulation.** Skipping meals lowers the fuel available for the body to generate heat, so regular, balanced meals, including enough protein, support a steadier internal temperature throughout the day, alongside the many other reasons regular eating matters after 40.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why do my hands and feet get cold before the rest of my body?**
The body prioritises keeping blood flow to vital organs and the core as we age, which means blood vessels in the hands and feet constrict earlier and more readily than those closer to the torso. This is why gloves and warm socks often make a bigger difference to overall comfort than an extra layer on the chest.

**Could my cold sensitivity be a thyroid problem?**
It could be, particularly if it has come on gradually alongside fatigue, weight gain, dry skin, or hair thinning. An underactive thyroid is common after 40 and is diagnosed with a simple blood test, so it is worth mentioning to your doctor rather than assuming it is only ageing.

**Is it normal for one hand or foot to feel colder than the other?**
No, this is different from the general, even cold sensitivity that comes with ordinary ageing and is worth getting checked, since it can point to a circulation issue in that specific limb that benefits from prompt attention rather than watching and waiting.

**Can exercise really make you feel warmer, or is that just temporary?**
Both are true. A single walk or workout provides an immediate warming effect through increased circulation, while regular strength training over months builds the muscle mass that raises your baseline heat production, making you feel less cold generally, not just during exercise itself.

Feeling the cold more than you used to is one of those quiet, unglamorous shifts of getting older, easy to dismiss and easy to simply work around with another layer. Most of the time that is exactly the right response. But paying attention to when it changes suddenly, or shows up alongside other symptoms, means you catch the checkable causes early, rather than assuming every cold morning is just age doing what age does.
