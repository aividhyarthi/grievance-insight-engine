Somewhere around 40, many people notice their comb picking up more strands than it used to, their parting looking a little wider in the mirror, or their first few genuinely grey hairs turning into a visible patch at the temples. For some, it happens earlier, for others later, but very few people reach their 50s without some degree of hair thinning or greying.

This article looks at what is actually happening to hair as you age, which changes are a normal and harmless part of getting older, and which patterns are worth raising with a doctor because they can point to something treatable, from a deficiency to a thyroid issue.

## Why does hair actually go grey?

**Hair gets its colour from pigment cells called melanocytes that sit inside each hair follicle.** As you age, these cells gradually produce less melanin and eventually stop working altogether in a given follicle, leaving that strand to grow in without pigment, which appears grey or white. This is a genuinely predictable biological process, not a sign of poor health, and the age at which it starts is largely determined by genetics. If your parents went grey early, there is a reasonable chance you will too, regardless of diet or stress levels, though severe prolonged stress is thought to play a modest accelerating role in some people.

**Premature greying, generally meaning before 20 in Indian populations, is a different situation** and more often linked to genetics, certain autoimmune conditions, or nutritional deficiencies, and is worth a doctor's evaluation. Greying that starts gradually in your late 30s or 40s, however, sits well within the normal range.

## Is hair thinning after 40 the same thing as hair loss?

**Not exactly, and the distinction matters.** Thinning usually refers to hair strands becoming finer and the overall density reducing gradually across the scalp, often linked to a slowing hair growth cycle and hormonal changes. Hair loss, especially patchy loss, clumps of shedding, or a receding hairline with visible bald patches, is a more specific pattern that can have distinct causes.

### Common, usually harmless patterns

- **Gradual overall thinning**: A slow reduction in hair density and thickness across the scalp over years, often more noticeable at the crown or parting
- **Male and female pattern hair loss**: A genetically influenced pattern, often running in families, that follows predictable areas of the scalp and tends to progress slowly
- **Texture changes**: Hair that was once straight becoming slightly wavier or coarser, or vice versa, which is a normal follicle-ageing change

### Patterns worth a doctor's opinion

- **Sudden, noticeable shedding**: Handfuls of hair coming out over weeks, sometimes months after an illness, major stress, surgery, or childbirth, a pattern called telogen effluvium
- **Patchy bald spots**: Distinct round or oval patches, which can suggest an autoimmune condition called alopecia areata
- **Hair loss with other symptoms**: Thinning alongside fatigue, weight changes, cold intolerance, or irregular periods can point to thyroid dysfunction
- **Scalp changes**: Itching, redness, scaling, or pain alongside hair loss, which may suggest a scalp condition needing specific treatment

## What nutritional factors actually matter?

**Diet plays a genuine, though limited, role in hair health, mainly through a small number of specific deficiencies that are common in Indian adults.** Iron deficiency, which is widespread among Indian women in particular, is a well-documented contributor to hair thinning and is worth checking with a simple blood test. Vitamin B12 deficiency, more common in people following strictly vegetarian diets without adequate supplementation, and low vitamin D levels have also been linked to hair changes, though the evidence for vitamin D specifically is less conclusive.

**Protein intake matters too**, since hair is largely made of a protein called keratin, and chronically inadequate protein intake can affect hair growth and strength over time. This is one more reason to pay attention to protein intake across the day as you age, something covered in more depth in our piece on changing protein needs after 40.

Beyond addressing a genuine deficiency your doctor has identified, there is no reliable evidence that any specific vitamin or supplement reverses age-related greying or makes hair meaningfully thicker in someone who is not actually deficient. Be wary of products making dramatic claims here, and remember that India's supplement market is loosely regulated, so quality and actual content vary by brand.

## What about colouring your hair and other practical choices?

This is a personal decision with no health requirement either way. **Colouring grey hair is cosmetically safe for most people** when done with reasonable care, though frequent use of certain hair dyes has been associated with mild scalp irritation in some people, and those with sensitive skin or known allergies should do a patch test or consult a dermatologist first. Choosing not to colour it is equally fine and, for many people, becomes a gradual and comfortable adjustment rather than something to fight.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can stress really turn hair grey overnight?**
Not overnight in the literal sense, but severe acute stress has been linked in some research to triggering a temporary shedding phase or accelerating greying that was already in progress. The dramatic "grey overnight" stories are largely exaggerated, but stress management is still worthwhile for overall health.

**Should I get a blood test if my hair is thinning?**
If the thinning feels faster than expected, is patchy, or comes with other symptoms like fatigue, it is reasonable to ask your doctor for basic blood tests, typically including iron studies, thyroid function, and vitamin B12, since these are common and treatable contributors.

**Do hair oils and scalp massages actually help?**
Regular gentle scalp massage may modestly improve local blood flow and is a low-risk, pleasant habit, and traditional oiling practices common in Indian households are generally safe for most people. However, neither reverses genetic greying or significantly changes pattern hair loss on its own.

**Is it normal for hair loss to be worse for women around perimenopause?**
Yes, hormonal shifts during this transition can genuinely affect hair density for many women, and it is worth discussing with a doctor, who can rule out other contributing factors and discuss reasonable options.

Thinning and greying after 40 are, for the overwhelming majority of people, simply part of getting older, not a sign that something has gone wrong. Paying attention to the pattern, rather than the mirror alone, is what tells you whether it is time for a doctor's opinion or simply a new relationship with your reflection.
