A work trip that once meant a quick flight, a full day of meetings, and a flight back without much thought now leaves you counting the days to recover. If you are noticing that conference season or a client trip to another city takes more out of you than it used to, you are not imagining it, and you are certainly not alone among professionals in their 50s and 60s who are still travelling for work, often at a demanding pace.

## Why does a work trip wipe you out more than it used to?

**The honest answer is that several small systems are working harder at once.** Circadian rhythm resets more slowly with age, so even a short time-zone shift takes longer to adjust to than it did in your 30s. Add disrupted sleep in an unfamiliar hotel bed, irregular meals around a conference schedule, and the mental effort of staying sharp through back-to-back sessions or client meetings, and the cumulative load is genuinely higher. **This is not a sign you are less capable at your job.** It reflects real physiological changes in how the ageing body recovers from disruption, and understanding that can help you plan around it instead of pushing through and paying for it later.

### The compounding effect of a packed itinerary

A single disrupted night is easy to recover from at any age. The problem with work travel is that it rarely stays to a single night: an early flight, a late dinner with colleagues, an early start the next day, and a jam-packed agenda in between can stack three or four nights of shortened or broken sleep in a row. **After 50, sleep debt accumulates in a way that is harder to reverse quickly**, which is part of why the third day of a conference often feels far worse than the first, even though the schedule itself has not changed much.

## What actually helps before and during the trip?

**Protecting your sleep window matters more than any other single habit.** Where possible, choose flight times that let you arrive with at least one full night before your first meeting or session, rather than landing and going straight into a full day. If the trip crosses time zones, try to shift your sleep and meal timing by an hour or two in the days before you leave, so the adjustment on arrival is smaller.

**Movement during long stretches of sitting is not optional at this stage.** Whether it is a long flight, a long train journey, or a full day seated in conference sessions, getting up and walking for a few minutes every hour or two supports circulation and genuinely lowers the risk of leg swelling and stiffness, both of which become more relevant with age. Compression socks for long flights are a simple, low-effort addition worth discussing with your doctor if you have any history of circulation issues or varicose veins.

**Medication timing should travel with you, not adjust to the itinerary.** If you take regular medication for blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, or anything else, carry it in your hand luggage with a few extra days' supply, and keep the dosing time as close to your usual schedule as the time zone allows. A pill organiser labelled by day, not just by time of day, makes this far easier to track across a multi-day, multi-city trip.

## Does jet lag actually get worse with age, and what helps?

**Yes, and the direction of travel matters.** Flying eastward, which shortens your day, is generally harder to adjust to at any age, but the gap between how a 30-year-old and a 55-year-old recover from it tends to widen over time. Getting daylight exposure soon after arrival helps reset your internal clock faster, as does resisting the urge to nap for more than 20 to 30 minutes on the first day, even when you are exhausted. If you are on a short trip of two days or less, some frequent business travellers deliberately stay on their home time zone rather than trying to adjust at all, since a partial adjustment can sometimes leave you feeling worse than none.

## How should you handle conference days themselves?

**Pacing through the agenda matters as much as pacing through the travel.** Conference days are often built around back-to-back sessions with little natural break, heavy coffee and biscuits at the refreshment counter, and standing through long networking sessions in the evening. **Choosing selectively which sessions and events genuinely matter to you**, rather than attending everything out of obligation, protects the energy you need for the parts that actually count, whether that is a keynote you need for work or a dinner with a client you have been trying to meet for months.

| Situation | What tends to go wrong | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Early flight after a late work day | Arriving already sleep-deprived | Pack the night before, sleep by a fixed cutoff even if it means an earlier bag-check |
| Full day of back-to-back sessions | Skipped meals, over-reliance on coffee and snacks | Carry a small protein snack, drink water between sessions, eat a proper meal even if it means missing part of a session |
| Evening networking dinner | Late, heavy meal with more alcohol than usual | Eat something before the event so you are not deciding on an empty stomach, set a personal drink limit in advance |
| Return flight the same day as a big meeting | No buffer for delays or exhaustion | Where possible, build in one buffer day before a high-stakes commitment |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to need a full day to recover after a short work trip after 50?**
Yes, this is common and reflects real changes in how the body recovers from disrupted sleep and irregular routine, not a lack of stamina or commitment. Building a lighter day into your schedule after a demanding trip, rather than diving straight back into a full workload, tends to actually protect your output over the following week.

**Should I avoid business travel altogether as I get older?**
Not necessarily. Most people can continue travelling for work well into their 60s and beyond by adjusting how they plan trips, protecting sleep and medication schedules, and being more selective about which trips and events genuinely require their presence. If travel consistently triggers serious fatigue, chest discomfort, or other concerning symptoms, that is worth discussing with your doctor rather than working around it indefinitely.

**Does travel insurance and carrying a medical summary actually matter for work trips?**
Yes, particularly for trips involving flights, unfamiliar cities, or any existing health condition. A brief written summary of your medications, allergies, and any chronic conditions, kept in your phone and your bag, can save valuable time if you need care while travelling, and most professionals underestimate how useful this is until they need it.

**What is the single most useful habit to protect on a work trip?**
For most people, protecting a consistent sleep window matters more than diet, exercise, or anything else during a short trip, since sleep debt is what tends to compound fastest and take longest to recover from after 50.

Work travel does not have to shrink as you get older, but it does benefit from being planned a little more deliberately than it once was. A few protected habits, chosen in advance rather than improvised under a packed agenda, usually make the difference between a trip that leaves you depleted and one you can actually enjoy.
