Somewhere in their 40s, many people notice flat brown or dark patches appearing on their face, the backs of their hands, or their forearms, areas that spend the most time in daylight over a lifetime. These are often called age spots, sun spots, or liver spots, even though they have nothing to do with the liver. For Indian skin tones specifically, pigmentation changes can also show up as broader patchy darkening, particularly around the cheeks and upper lip, a pattern often called melasma.

This article covers why pigmentation changes happen with age, how to tell ordinary age spots apart from something that needs a dermatologist's attention, and what genuinely helps, as opposed to what gets marketed heavily with little evidence behind it.

## Why does pigmentation change after 40?

**Skin pigment comes from melanin, produced by cells called melanocytes, and over decades of sun exposure these cells can cluster or become locally overactive in certain spots, producing the flat brown patches known as solar lentigines, commonly called age spots.** This is a direct, cumulative effect of ultraviolet exposure over a lifetime rather than a sudden event, which is why age spots typically appear on areas that have had the most sun: the face, the backs of the hands, the forearms, and the upper chest.

### Melasma: a different, more Indian-specific pattern

**Melasma shows up as broader, often symmetrical patches of darker pigmentation, most commonly across the cheeks, forehead, nose, and upper lip, and it is especially common in Indian and other darker-skinned populations.** Unlike simple age spots, melasma has a stronger hormonal link, often appearing or worsening during pregnancy, with hormonal contraceptive use, or around perimenopause, and it is made notably worse by sun exposure and sometimes by heat. It tends to be more stubborn to treat than ordinary age spots and often needs a dermatologist's guidance for a realistic treatment plan.

### Post-inflammatory pigmentation

A third common pattern in Indian skin is darkening left behind after acne, an insect bite, a cut, a burn, or any skin inflammation, called post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. This becomes more noticeable with age partly because skin takes longer to turn over and clear pigment as the decades pass, and partly because the cumulative number of minor skin injuries over a lifetime simply adds up.

| Type of pigmentation | Typical pattern | Main driver |
|---|---|---|
| Age spots (solar lentigines) | Flat brown spots on face, hands, forearms | Cumulative sun exposure |
| Melasma | Broader, symmetrical patches, cheeks and upper lip | Hormones plus sun exposure |
| Post-inflammatory pigmentation | Darkening where skin was previously inflamed or injured | Past acne, injury, or irritation |

## What genuinely helps

**Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen, applied every morning regardless of whether it looks sunny, is the single most evidence-backed step for both preventing new pigmentation and keeping existing spots from darkening further.** This matters year-round in most of India, not just during the hottest months, since ultraviolet exposure continues even on cloudy days and indoors near windows. A wide-brimmed hat and seeking shade during peak midday hours add meaningful extra protection, particularly for anyone who spends time outdoors for work or commuting.

**Beyond sunscreen, a dermatologist can offer a range of evidence-based options depending on the type and depth of pigmentation, including certain topical prescriptions, chemical peels, or laser-based treatments.** These work best when matched to the specific type of pigmentation and skin tone involved, which is exactly why a proper in-person assessment tends to produce better, safer results than trying a sequence of over-the-counter creams based on marketing claims alone. A dermatologist consultation in most Indian cities typically costs somewhere in the range of ₹500 to ₹1,500, which is a reasonable investment before committing to any ongoing treatment.

## A genuine safety warning: unregulated skin-lightening creams

**India has a well-documented problem with unregulated and illegally sold skin-lightening creams, some of which contain banned substances such as high-dose steroids, mercury, or hydroquinone at unsafe concentrations, sold without proper labelling in local markets or online.** These products can cause thinning of the skin, visible blood vessels, steroid-induced acne, and in the case of mercury, genuine systemic toxicity with long-term use. If you are considering any pigmentation treatment, buying from a known pharmacy or getting a prescription from a dermatologist is a meaningfully safer path than an unlabelled jar promising fast results.

## When to actually see a doctor, not just treat it cosmetically

**Most age spots and melasma are harmless and purely cosmetic, but certain changes are worth a prompt dermatologist visit rather than being dismissed as "just pigmentation."** A spot that is new and growing quickly, has an irregular or asymmetrical border, contains multiple colours, is larger than roughly the size of a pencil eraser, or has started bleeding, itching, or changing texture should be examined, since these can be signs of a skin cancer rather than ordinary age-related pigmentation. If you are unsure whether a spot fits this description, it is always reasonable to get it looked at rather than guess.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are age spots a sign of an internal health problem?**
Generally no. Ordinary age spots (solar lentigines) are a cosmetic sign of cumulative sun exposure, not an internal disease marker. That said, any new or unusual-looking skin growth is still worth a doctor's opinion to rule out something that needs attention.

**Can home remedies like lemon juice or turmeric paste fade pigmentation?**
There is little reliable evidence that these commonly suggested home remedies meaningfully fade established pigmentation, and lemon juice in particular can irritate skin and occasionally worsen pigmentation through sun sensitisation. Sunscreen and, where needed, dermatologist-guided treatment have far stronger evidence behind them.

**Does darker skin mean I don't need to worry about sun protection?**
No. While darker skin tones do have some natural protection against sunburn and a somewhat lower skin cancer risk than fairer skin, they are still very much prone to pigmentation changes like melasma and age spots, and skin cancer can still occur and is often diagnosed later in darker-skinned individuals. Daily sunscreen is still worthwhile.

**How long does it take to see results from sunscreen alone?**
Sunscreen mainly prevents new pigmentation and stops existing spots from getting darker, rather than actively fading them quickly. Noticeable fading of existing pigmentation, if sunscreen is paired with other dermatologist-recommended treatment, is usually measured in months, not days or weeks.

Pigmentation changes after 40 are extremely common and, in the vast majority of cases, nothing to worry about beyond appearance. A consistent sunscreen habit and a dermatologist's input for anything stubborn or unusual will take you further than any product promising a dramatic overnight fix.
