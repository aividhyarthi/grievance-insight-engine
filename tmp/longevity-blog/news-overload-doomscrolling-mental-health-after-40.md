Walk into many Indian homes after 6pm and you will find a television tuned to a news channel, volume up, running in the background through dinner and well into the night. Add to that a phone buzzing with WhatsApp forwards, family group chats sharing alarming health claims and political outrage in equal measure, and a habit of scrolling news apps right before sleep, and you have a daily information diet that would have been unthinkable a generation ago. After 40, when stress already tends to sit more heavily in the body, it is worth asking honestly what this habit is actually doing to you.

## Is doomscrolling actually a real thing, or just a dramatic word for a habit?

**It is a real, measurable pattern, and it has a fairly consistent physiological signature.** Doomscrolling describes the compulsive consumption of negative news, one distressing headline leading to another, often for far longer than intended, and often late at night. Studies tracking this behaviour have found associations with higher anxiety, lower mood, and poorer sleep quality, and the effect appears to be driven partly by the sheer repetition: seeing variations of the same threatening story dozens of times a day keeps the nervous system's threat-detection system mildly activated well beyond what a single reading of the news would do.

**This is not simply about being informed, which remains genuinely valuable.** The issue is volume and timing, not awareness itself. Reading a newspaper once in the morning and scrolling an endless, algorithmically amplified feed of the most alarming stories available, repeatedly through the day and into the night, are very different experiences for the nervous system, even though both count as "following the news."

## Why might this hit harder after 40 than it did at 25?

**Midlife often comes with a genuinely heavier real-world load already**, ageing parents, career pressures, financial responsibilities, and sometimes early signs of health concerns in yourself or your spouse. Layering a steady stream of external threat, political instability, crime stories, health scares, onto an already full stress bucket leaves less room to absorb it, and the body's baseline stress hormones may already be running somewhat higher than in your twenties, when recovery from a stressful news cycle was simply faster.

**There is also a generational pattern in how older Indians often consume news, worth naming honestly.** Many households have a television tuned to a news channel for hours as background, a habit formed over decades, and this now runs alongside, rather than instead of, newer habits like phone-based scrolling and WhatsApp forwards. The combination means some people in their 40s, 50s, and beyond are absorbing more hours of news-adjacent content daily than almost any generation before them, often without fully registering how much that adds up to.

### What about WhatsApp forwards specifically?

**Family and neighbourhood WhatsApp groups deserve particular attention, since they are a uniquely Indian amplifier of anxiety that general discussions of "social media and mental health" often miss.** Forwards about health scares, often unverified or outright false, travel through these groups rapidly, frequently landing in the hands of people least equipped to fact-check them, and generating real anxiety about personal health or safety based on nothing more than a message forwarded a dozen times. Political content follows a similar pattern. **Being part of several active family groups while also following television news and personal social media can mean encountering the same alarming theme from three or four directions in a single day**, each reinforcing the others.

## What does this actually do to sleep and mood?

**Sleep is one of the most consistently affected areas, and the mechanism is straightforward.** Checking news or scrolling social media in the hour before bed exposes you to emotionally activating content right when your body should be winding down, and the blue light from screens adds a separate, well-documented delay to melatonin release. The combined effect is a double hit: harder to fall asleep, and the sleep you do get is lighter and less restorative because your nervous system has not fully downshifted.

**Mood effects tend to be subtler but cumulative.** Rather than a single dramatic crash, many people describe a persistent low-grade unease, irritability, or a sense that the world is more dangerous and unstable than it was, which can colour everyday interactions and decisions without ever being traced back to its actual source: hours of accumulated news exposure.

## A practical comparison: habits worth keeping versus changing

| Habit | Effect on mood and sleep | Suggested change |
|---|---|---|
| TV news as constant background noise | Keeps mild stress activation running for hours | Set specific viewing windows, switch off outside them |
| Checking news first thing on waking | Starts the day in a threat-alert state | Delay first check by 30-60 minutes, have breakfast or a walk first |
| Scrolling news or WhatsApp in bed | Delays sleep onset, reduces sleep quality | Keep the phone outside the bedroom or on silent and out of reach |
| Forwarding unverified health or political messages | Spreads anxiety to others, often based on false information | Pause and verify before forwarding, especially health claims |
| Following one or two trusted sources rather than many | Reduces repetitive exposure to the same alarming story | Unfollow or mute accounts that mainly amplify outrage |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it bad to stay informed about the news at all?**
No, staying informed is genuinely valuable and this is not an argument for disengaging from the world. The concern is specifically about volume, repetition, and timing, several hours a day across television, social media, and forwarded messages, particularly late at night, rather than a single focused reading of the news once or twice a day.

**Why do WhatsApp forwards cause more anxiety than reading a newspaper?**
Forwards often come from trusted family members, which makes people less likely to question their accuracy, and they frequently contain exaggerated or false health and safety claims designed to provoke a strong reaction so they get shared further. A newspaper or established news source, by contrast, generally has at least some editorial accountability.

**Can doomscrolling actually affect physical health, not just mood?**
Yes, indirectly. The stress response it triggers involves real physiological changes, elevated cortisol and a heightened alert state, and when sustained for hours daily over months or years, chronic low-grade stress of this kind is linked to effects on blood pressure, sleep quality, and overall resilience, the same pathways discussed in relation to chronic stress more broadly.

**What should I do if I feel genuinely anxious most days because of the news?**
If anxiety from news consumption is affecting your daily functioning, sleep, or relationships most days, it is worth speaking with a doctor or a mental health professional rather than assuming it will pass on its own. If you ever feel unable to cope, iCall (9152987821, Monday to Saturday, 8am-10pm) offers free, confidential psychosocial support, and in a genuine emergency, call 112.

The point is not to disconnect from the world after 40, that is neither realistic nor desirable. It is to notice how much of your day is quietly spent absorbing other people's crises through a screen, and to decide deliberately how much of that your mind actually needs, rather than letting the habit decide for you.
