You might be eating roughly the same amount of protein you did at 30, yet finding it harder to maintain muscle and strength in your 40s and beyond. This is not in your head, and it is not simply about doing more push-ups. Your body's ability to use protein to build muscle actually becomes less efficient with age, a shift that has real consequences for strength, metabolism, and independence later in life, and understanding it changes how much protein you should actually be eating.

## What is muscle protein synthesis, in plain terms?

**Muscle protein synthesis is simply the process by which your body builds new muscle protein**, constantly breaking down and rebuilding muscle tissue in response to what you eat and how you move. In a young, healthy adult, eating a moderate amount of protein, especially after strength training, triggers a strong muscle-building response. This process is how muscle is maintained and grown throughout life, and it depends heavily on getting enough protein, particularly the amino acid leucine found in good-quality protein sources, at regular intervals throughout the day.

**Ageing blunts this response**, a phenomenon researchers call anabolic resistance. The same amount of protein that would trigger a robust muscle-building signal in a 25-year-old produces a noticeably smaller response in someone in their 60s or 70s. This does not mean older adults cannot build or maintain muscle, they absolutely can, it means they typically need a larger per-meal protein dose to achieve the same effect, along with resistance exercise to make the most of that protein.

## Why does this matter for long-term health, not just appearance?

**Muscle is far more than an aesthetic concern, it is a metabolic and functional organ.** Muscle mass and strength are strongly linked to independence in later life, the ability to get up from a chair, climb stairs, carry groceries, and recover from illness or injury without prolonged disability. Sarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle mass and strength, begins subtly as early as the 30s and accelerates notably after 60, and it is associated with higher risk of falls, fractures, hospitalisation, and reduced life expectancy in some studies.

**Muscle also plays a significant role in metabolic health.** It is a major site for glucose disposal, meaning more muscle mass generally supports better blood sugar control, which matters given India's high rates of type 2 diabetes. Preserving muscle through midlife is one of the more underrated levers for metabolic and functional longevity, alongside cardiovascular fitness.

## How much protein do you actually need after 40?

**General population guidelines, often around 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight daily, were designed to prevent deficiency, not to optimise muscle maintenance in an ageing body.** Most researchers studying muscle health in older adults now suggest higher targets, commonly in the range of 1 to 1.2 grams per kilogram daily for general healthy ageing, and up to 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram for those actively strength training or recovering from illness or surgery. For a 70 kilogram adult, that works out to roughly 70 to 110 grams of protein spread across the day, depending on activity level, a notably higher target than many people currently eat, particularly in typical Indian dietary patterns that can be relatively carbohydrate-heavy, especially at breakfast (a common pattern of parathas, poha, or idli with lower protein content) and lunch.

**Distribution across the day matters as much as the total.** Research suggests roughly 25 to 30 grams of good-quality protein per meal is a reasonable target to meaningfully trigger muscle protein synthesis in someone over 40, meaning a single large protein-rich dinner is less effective than spreading adequate protein across breakfast, lunch, and dinner.

| Meal | Common lower-protein Indian pattern | A protein-forward adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | Poha, parathas, or idli alone | Add curd, paneer, eggs, or a side of sprouts |
| Lunch | Rice, one vegetable, small dal portion | Larger dal or rajma portion, add curd or a boiled egg |
| Evening snack | Tea and biscuits or namkeen | Roasted chana, peanuts, or a boiled egg |
| Dinner | Roti, sabzi, small dal | Add paneer, chicken, fish, or soya alongside dal |

## What are good protein sources for Indian diets?

**Vegetarian and vegan options are entirely workable with some planning.** Dals and legumes (moong, chana, rajma, urad), paneer, curd and other dairy, soya products like tofu and soya chunks, and combining grains with legumes across the day all contribute meaningfully. For those who eat eggs, fish, or chicken, these are efficient, complete protein sources that make hitting higher targets somewhat easier. Protein powder, used as a convenient top-up rather than a primary source, can help people who struggle to eat enough through food alone, particularly older adults with reduced appetite, though whole foods remain the foundation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can eating too much protein harm my kidneys?**
For people with healthy kidney function, higher protein intake within the ranges discussed here has not been shown to cause kidney damage in research. However, anyone with existing kidney disease should discuss protein intake specifically with their doctor, since recommendations differ in that situation.

**Is plant protein as effective as animal protein for muscle maintenance?**
Plant proteins can absolutely support muscle maintenance, though they are sometimes slightly lower in certain amino acids like leucine per gram. Combining different plant protein sources across the day, and eating slightly larger portions, generally closes this gap effectively for most vegetarian and vegan eaters.

**Do I need protein supplements to hit these targets?**
Most people can meet higher protein targets through food alone with some planning, particularly by adding protein-rich items to breakfast and lunch. Protein powder is a reasonable convenience option for those who struggle with appetite or time, but it is not required for everyone.

**At what age should I start paying more attention to protein intake?**
Muscle loss begins gradually as early as the 30s, so building good protein habits in your 40s, alongside regular strength training, is a genuinely useful preventive step rather than something to postpone until muscle loss becomes noticeable.

Protein needs are one of the more concrete, actionable pieces of the ageing puzzle. Combined with regular strength training, paying deliberate attention to how much protein you eat and when can meaningfully protect the muscle, strength, and independence that make the decades after 40 feel like an expansion rather than a slow retreat.
