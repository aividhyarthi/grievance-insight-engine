You stand up quickly from the sofa, bend down to pick something up and then straighten, or get out of bed in the middle of the night, and for a few seconds the room swims, your vision greys slightly, and you need to hold onto something steady. This brief, standing-related dizziness has a name, orthostatic hypotension, and it becomes noticeably more common after 40, especially for people on blood pressure medication. It is usually harmless on its own, but it is also one of the more overlooked contributors to falls in midlife and beyond.

## What is actually happening in the body during this dizzy spell?

**When you stand up, gravity pulls roughly half a litre of blood down into your legs almost instantly, and your body normally compensates within a few seconds by tightening blood vessels and briefly raising your heart rate to keep blood flow to the brain steady.** This reflex, which works efficiently in younger adults, becomes slightly slower and less responsive with age, for reasons including stiffer blood vessels and a nervous system that reacts a touch more sluggishly to position changes. The brief lag between standing and the body's compensation is what you experience as that dizzy, swimming few seconds.

**A reading is generally considered orthostatic hypotension when blood pressure drops by at least 20 points in the top number, or 10 points in the bottom number, within a few minutes of standing**, though most people never actually measure this and simply experience the sensation.

## Why does this become more common after 40, and especially with certain medications?

### Blood pressure medication

**Medicines prescribed to lower blood pressure, by their very design, can sometimes lower it a little too much on standing, especially when a dose was recently started or increased.** This is one of the single most common and most fixable causes of standing-related dizziness in midlife and older adults, and it is always worth mentioning to the prescribing doctor rather than assuming it is simply unavoidable.

### Dehydration and heavy meals

**Even mild dehydration reduces the total blood volume available to compensate quickly on standing, and a large, carbohydrate-heavy meal diverts blood flow toward digestion, both of which make a standing dizzy spell more likely.** This is why episodes are often noticeably worse on hot days or shortly after a big lunch.

### Prolonged bed rest or sitting

**Extended time in bed, such as during a recovery from illness or surgery, temporarily weakens the body's reflex response to standing, which is why dizziness is common for the first day or two after getting back on your feet following any extended period of rest.**

### Underlying conditions

**Diabetes, Parkinson's disease, and certain nerve conditions can directly affect the autonomic nervous system responsible for this standing reflex**, making orthostatic hypotension a more persistent issue rather than an occasional one. This is one of the reasons a doctor will usually ask about other symptoms rather than treating the dizziness in isolation.

## Why does this matter beyond the momentary discomfort?

**Dizziness on standing is one of the genuinely underappreciated contributors to falls after 40, particularly for nighttime trips to the bathroom, when a person is half asleep, the room is dark, and there is no time to notice early warning signs before losing balance.** A fall from this kind of dizziness is entirely preventable with the simple habit of standing up in stages, which is why it deserves more attention than it typically gets.

**Keeping a small light on for nighttime bathroom trips, and sitting on the edge of the bed for a few seconds before standing, are two of the lowest-effort, highest-impact changes available for reducing this specific fall risk.**

## When does this need a doctor's attention?

**Occasional, brief dizziness on standing that resolves within a few seconds and never leads to an actual fall is common and usually not urgent, though it is worth mentioning at your next routine visit.** It is worth a dedicated appointment, rather than waiting, if episodes are happening frequently, are lasting longer than a few seconds, have ever caused an actual fall, or started or worsened after a new medication.

**Dizziness accompanied by chest pain, a pounding or irregular heartbeat, fainting, slurred speech, or confusion is not routine orthostatic hypotension, it needs emergency evaluation immediately, call 112.**

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is this the same thing as vertigo?**
No. Vertigo, often caused by an inner-ear problem, typically involves a spinning sensation that isn't necessarily tied to standing up. Orthostatic hypotension causes a lightheaded, swimming, or greying-out feeling specifically linked to a change in position, most often standing from sitting or lying down.

**Can this happen even if my blood pressure readings are otherwise normal?**
Yes. Orthostatic hypotension is about the drop that happens on standing, not your baseline reading, so a generally normal or even slightly high blood pressure can still come with a standing-related dip.

**Should I stop my blood pressure medication if it seems to be causing this?**
No, never stop or adjust a prescribed medication on your own. Mention the dizziness to your doctor, who can review the dose or timing, since stopping suddenly can itself be risky.

**Does drinking more water really make a noticeable difference?**
For many people, yes, steady hydration through the day genuinely reduces the frequency and intensity of standing-related dizziness, particularly in hot weather or after sweating heavily.

A brief dizzy spell on standing is one of those small, easy-to-dismiss signals that is actually worth paying attention to, both because it is usually simple to manage and because, left unaddressed, it quietly raises fall risk in a way few people think to connect to it.
