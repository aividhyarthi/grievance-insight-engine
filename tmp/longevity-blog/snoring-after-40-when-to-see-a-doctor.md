It usually starts as a running joke between spouses: elbows in the night, separate blankets, the occasional "you were so loud last night." But snoring that gets louder, more frequent, and harder to ignore after 40 is worth more than a joke. For most people it is simply a mechanical consequence of ageing tissue, but for some, it is the most visible clue to a condition that quietly affects the heart and brain for years before anyone takes it seriously.

## Why does snoring tend to increase with age?

**Snoring happens when air struggles to move smoothly past relaxed tissue in the throat, causing it to vibrate.** As we age, several things make that vibration more likely. Muscle tone in the throat and soft palate gradually decreases, the same way muscle tone decreases elsewhere in the body. The airway itself can narrow slightly due to tissue changes. And weight gain, particularly around the neck and upper body, a common pattern after 40, adds soft tissue that further crowds the airway during sleep.

**Alcohol, certain sedatives, and sleeping on your back all relax throat muscles further, making snoring louder and more frequent on nights when any of these are involved.** This is why snoring often seems worse after a late evening out, or more noticeable in the second half of a long night's sleep as muscles relax further into deep sleep.

### Men, women, and the hormone connection

Snoring has traditionally been thought of as more of a male issue, but this gap narrows with age. Hormonal shifts around and after menopause reduce some of the natural protective tone in women's upper airway muscles, which is part of why snoring and sleep apnoea both become noticeably more common in women in their late 40s and 50s, even among those who rarely snored before.

## When is snoring just noise, and when is it something more?

This is the single most important distinction to make, and it usually requires input from whoever shares your bed, since you are, by definition, asleep when the most telling signs occur.

**Simple, uncomplicated snoring is typically steady, doesn't involve long silent pauses, and the person wakes up feeling reasonably rested.** It can still be disruptive to a partner's sleep and worth addressing for that reason alone, but it isn't usually a medical red flag by itself.

**Snoring that comes with pauses in breathing, choking or gasping sounds, or very restless, thrashing sleep is a different picture entirely**, and is the hallmark description partners give when obstructive sleep apnoea is present. In this condition, the airway doesn't just vibrate, it actually collapses and blocks airflow for seconds at a time, repeatedly through the night, each episode followed by a gasp as the brain briefly wakes the body to reopen the airway.

| Feature | Simple snoring | Possible sleep apnoea |
|---|---|---|
| Sound pattern | Steady, rhythmic | Loud snoring interrupted by silence, then gasping |
| Breathing pauses | Not reported | Reported by a bed partner, sometimes several per hour |
| Daytime tiredness | Usually mild or absent | Often marked, even after a "full" night's sleep |
| Morning headache | Uncommon | Fairly common |
| Associated risks | Mostly social, disrupts a partner's sleep | Linked to high blood pressure, heart strain, and daytime accidents |
| What to do | Lifestyle and positional changes usually help | Needs medical evaluation, often a sleep study |

## What actually helps with ordinary snoring?

**Sleeping on your side rather than your back is one of the simplest, most effective changes**, since lying on the back lets the tongue and soft palate fall backward into the airway more easily. Some people find a simple trick, like sewing a tennis ball or similar object into the back of a nightshirt, enough to discourage rolling onto the back through the night.

**Reducing weight around the neck and midsection, cutting back on alcohol in the evening, and treating nasal congestion from allergies or sinus issues** can all meaningfully reduce snoring volume and frequency. Elevating the head slightly while sleeping can also help some people, similar to advice given for acid reflux.

## Why it's worth taking seriously, even if you feel fine

**Obstructive sleep apnoea, when present and untreated over years, is associated with higher rates of high blood pressure, irregular heart rhythms, and daytime sleepiness that increases accident risk, including while driving.** The frustrating part is that many people with significant sleep apnoea feel only vaguely unrefreshed rather than obviously unwell, which is why a partner's observations often matter more than how you feel yourself. If sleep apnoea is suspected, a doctor can arrange a sleep study, either at a sleep lab or increasingly through simpler home-based devices, to confirm it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can losing weight stop snoring completely?**
For many people, yes, significantly, since excess tissue around the neck is one of the biggest contributors. It may not eliminate snoring entirely for everyone, since airway shape and tissue tone also play a role, but it is consistently one of the most effective single changes.

**Do nasal strips or sprays actually help?**
They can help in specific cases, mainly when congestion or a narrow nasal passage is the main contributor, by improving airflow through the nose. They are less effective when the snoring originates from the throat rather than the nose.

**Is snoring worse in humid or polluted Indian cities?**
It can be, since poor air quality and seasonal allergies both contribute to nasal congestion, which forces more breathing through the mouth and increases throat vibration during sleep.

**Should I get a sleep study even if I don't feel particularly tired?**
If your partner reports witnessed breathing pauses or choking sounds, yes, it is worth discussing with a doctor regardless of how rested you feel, since sleep apnoea symptoms during the day can be subtle or easy to dismiss as general ageing.

Snoring is often dismissed as a harmless, even amusing, part of getting older, and for many people that is a fair description. But when it comes with silence, then a gasp, it stops being noise and starts being information your body is giving you. It is worth listening to, with a doctor's help if needed.
