Getting pointed feedback from a manager fifteen years younger, or having a project you led get picked apart in a review, can feel different at 45 than it did at 25. Some of that is ego, but a genuine amount of it is a real psychological shift, how the brain processes criticism, threat, and self-worth changes with age and experience, and understanding that shift can turn workplace feedback from something to dread into something you can actually use well.

## Does criticism really land differently in your brain with age?

**There is genuine research behind this, not just anecdote.** Studies on emotional regulation across the lifespan have found that older adults, on average, show reduced amygdala reactivity, the brain region heavily involved in fear and threat response, when processing negative information, compared with younger adults. **This does not mean older adults feel nothing when criticised**, it means the intensity and duration of the emotional spike tend to be smaller and shorter for many people, which is sometimes described in research as an age-related "positivity effect" in how information is processed and remembered.

**Experience adds a second, more practical layer on top of this biological shift.** By your 40s or 50s, you have likely survived enough projects, reviews, and setbacks to have direct evidence that a single piece of critical feedback rarely determines your overall competence or career trajectory, a perspective that is much harder to have genuinely internalised at 24, however intellectually you might understand it.

## Why does feedback from a younger manager sometimes feel harder, not easier?

**This is a common and specific pattern worth naming directly.** Feedback from someone significantly younger, especially someone junior to you in age though senior in title, can trigger a status-related discomfort that is different from ordinary criticism processing, tied more to identity and hierarchy than to the substance of the feedback itself. **Recognising that discomfort as a status reaction, not evidence that the feedback is wrong or unfair, is often the fastest way to actually hear what is useful in it.**

**Workplace ageism, subtle or overt, complicates this further and deserves honest acknowledgement.** Feedback that implies someone is "set in their ways" or "not adapting" can sometimes reflect genuine performance concerns, and can sometimes reflect age bias dressed up as feedback, and the two are not always easy to tell apart in the moment. **Asking a trusted colleague for a second read on ambiguous feedback, or asking the person giving it for a specific, concrete example, usually clarifies which it is.**

## Can you actually get better at receiving feedback, or is it fixed?

**It is genuinely learnable, and midlife is, in some ways, a good time to work on it deliberately.** A well-studied practical technique is separating the content of feedback from your interpretation of it in the moment, hearing "this report needed clearer structure" as exactly that sentence, rather than immediately translating it into "I am losing my edge" or "they think I am past my prime". **Writing feedback down before reacting to it, even mentally, and reviewing it again after a few hours, gives the initial emotional spike time to settle before you decide how much weight it deserves.**

**Experience also gives you something younger colleagues often lack: a track record to weigh feedback against.** If you have consistently delivered strong work for years, a single piece of critical feedback is genuinely one data point, not the whole picture, and holding onto that broader context is one of the real advantages of having been in the workforce longer.

## Ways experience changes how criticism lands

| At 25 | At 45-plus |
|---|---|
| A critical review can feel identity-threatening | More often processed as information about the work, not the self |
| Little track record to weigh feedback against | Years of evidence to contextualise a single critique |
| Emotional reactivity tends to be higher | Emotional regulation for negative input tends to be stronger |
| Feedback from authority rarely questioned | More capacity to distinguish genuine feedback from bias |
| Recovery from a harsh review can take longer | Often recovers focus and confidence faster |

## When does workplace feedback stop being manageable stress and become something more?

**Ordinary discomfort after a tough review, that fades within a day or two and does not affect how you show up the next week, is a normal part of working life at any age.** What is worth taking seriously is **persistent anxiety before every review, dread that lingers for days, sleep disruption tied specifically to work feedback, or a pattern of feeling undermined that does not match the actual substance of what is being said**, all of which are reasonable things to bring to a doctor or a mental health professional rather than assuming they are simply part of "getting older at work". This matters particularly if it is affecting sleep, appetite, or your sense of purpose more broadly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it true that people get less sensitive to criticism as they age?**
On average, yes, research on emotional regulation suggests reduced emotional reactivity to negative feedback with age for many people, alongside the practical benefit of having more experience to contextualise a single critique. Individual variation is still large, and workplace stress, personality, and specific circumstances all play a role too.

**Why does feedback from a younger boss sometimes feel worse than from an older one?**
It often triggers a status-related discomfort separate from the actual content of the feedback, tied to hierarchy and identity rather than accuracy. Naming that reaction for what it is usually makes it easier to evaluate the feedback itself more clearly.

**How can I tell if critical feedback at work is really about ageism?**
Feedback that is specific, tied to a concrete piece of work, and something you can act on is more likely genuine. Feedback that is vague, focused on "energy," "fit," or "keeping up" without specifics is worth questioning, and getting a second opinion from a trusted colleague can help clarify which it is.

**Should I bring up feelings of being undervalued at work with a doctor?**
If it is affecting your sleep, mood, or sense of wellbeing beyond ordinary day-to-day stress, yes, this is a legitimate topic for a doctor or mental health professional, not something to be expected to simply tolerate. Persistent workplace-related distress deserves the same attention as any other source of chronic stress.

Feedback rarely stops arriving, no matter your seniority or experience. What genuinely changes with age is your capacity to hear it without it threatening who you are, and that shift, used deliberately, can make you a sharper, calmer colleague than you were twenty years ago.
