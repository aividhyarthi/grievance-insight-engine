It usually starts with a single toenail, often the big toe, that stops looking quite right. A patch of yellow or white discoloration, a slightly thickened edge, or a corner that seems to be lifting away from the nail bed. Many people assume it is a minor cosmetic issue from an ill-fitting shoe or a stubbed toe, and ignore it for months, sometimes years, until it has spread to other nails or become noticeably thicker and harder to manage.

Fungal nail infections, medically called onychomycosis, become distinctly more common after 40, and India's warm, humid climate for much of the year creates genuinely favourable conditions for the fungi responsible. They are rarely dangerous on their own, but they are persistent, often embarrassing, and worth understanding properly rather than hiding inside closed shoes indefinitely.

## Why does this become more common specifically after 40?

**Nail growth genuinely slows with age, and this single change has an outsized effect on fungal infections.** A fungus that infects the nail has to be outgrown and trimmed away as healthy new nail replaces it from the base, which is normally a slow process even in younger adults. As growth slows further with age, fungus has considerably more time to establish itself and spread before it would naturally be pushed out, making infections both more likely to take hold and slower to clear once they do.

**Circulation to the feet can also reduce somewhat with age, particularly in people with diabetes, varicose veins, or other circulatory conditions**, and good blood flow is part of how the body's immune defences reach the extremities effectively. This is one reason diabetes and fungal nail infections are frequently discussed together by doctors, not because diabetes directly causes the infection, but because reduced circulation and sometimes reduced sensation make infections both more likely and more important to treat properly rather than ignore.

### Why does India's climate make this worse?

**Warm, humid conditions for much of the year, combined with closed shoes worn for work or commuting, create an environment fungi genuinely thrive in.** Feet that stay damp with sweat inside shoes for extended periods, particularly during the monsoon months, give fungal spores exactly the warm, moist conditions they need to multiply. Communal wet areas, public swimming pools, shared bathing areas, and gym locker rooms, add another route of exposure that accumulates in risk over years of ordinary life.

## How can you tell if it is actually a fungal infection?

**The most common signs are a nail that becomes thickened, discoloured, usually yellow, white, or brownish, and increasingly brittle or crumbly at the edges.** The nail may also separate slightly from the nail bed, and in some cases develop a faint, unpleasant odour. It typically starts in one nail, most often a toenail rather than a fingernail, and can spread to neighbouring nails if left untreated over time.

**A doctor can usually confirm the diagnosis through examination, sometimes with a simple scraping sent for laboratory testing**, which matters because other conditions, including psoriasis affecting the nails or simple nail trauma, can look similar but require entirely different treatment. Self-diagnosing from internet photographs alone is unreliable enough that an actual confirmation is worth the relatively minor effort of a clinic visit.

## What does treatment actually involve, and why does it take so long?

**Treatment almost always takes longer than people expect, purely because it depends on the infected nail growing out and being replaced by healthy nail, a process measured in months rather than days or weeks.** Toenails in particular grow slowly, often taking the better part of a year to fully replace themselves, which is why a doctor-prescribed course of treatment, whether a topical antifungal applied directly to the nail or an oral medication for more established infections, typically needs sustained, consistent use over several months to actually work.

**Oral antifungal medications tend to be more effective for established infections but require a doctor's assessment first**, since they can occasionally affect the liver and are not automatically suitable for everyone, particularly people already taking multiple other medications. This is a conversation worth having directly with your doctor rather than purchasing anything over the counter based on a pharmacist's general suggestion alone, especially given how variable the quality and appropriateness of unregulated over-the-counter options can be.

**Stopping treatment early because the nail looks slightly better is one of the most common reasons infections return.** Visible improvement at the surface does not necessarily mean the fungus has been fully cleared from deeper within the nail, so completing the full course a doctor recommends matters more than how the nail happens to look partway through.

## How can you actually prevent it in the first place?

**Keeping feet genuinely dry, especially between the toes, after bathing or sweating is one of the simplest and most effective preventive habits.** Choosing breathable footwear where practical, rotating between at least two pairs of regularly worn shoes so each has time to fully dry out, and changing socks if feet become damp during the day all reduce the moist conditions fungi need. Avoiding walking barefoot in communal wet areas like public showers or pool decks, and not sharing nail clippers, socks, or footwear with others, particularly within a household where one person already has an infection, further lowers the risk of both getting and spreading it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a fungal nail infection go away on its own without treatment?**
Rarely. Most fungal nail infections persist and often slowly worsen or spread to other nails without proper treatment, since the fungus is generally not cleared by the body's own defences alone once established within the nail. Seeing a doctor for a confirmed diagnosis and appropriate treatment is the more reliable path than waiting it out.

**Is a fungal nail infection dangerous for someone with diabetes?**
It is not usually dangerous on its own, but it deserves prompt, proper attention in anyone with diabetes, since reduced circulation and sometimes reduced sensation in the feet can allow minor skin breaks near an infected nail to become more serious complications. Regular foot checks and prompt treatment of any nail changes are part of standard diabetes foot care for exactly this reason.

**Can nail polish or pedicures make a fungal infection worse?**
Covering an infected nail with polish can trap moisture and may make the infection harder to treat and monitor, so it is generally better to let a doctor see and treat the nail without polish during active treatment. Pedicure tools that are not properly sterilised between clients at a salon are also a genuine route of infection, worth checking on before visiting any salon regularly.

**How long does it actually take to see improvement?**
Because treatment depends on nail regrowth, visible improvement is often gradual, typically becoming noticeable only after a few months, with full clearance sometimes taking six months to a year for toenails specifically. Patience and completing the full course a doctor prescribes matter more than looking for rapid results.

Fungal nail infections are common, treatable, and nothing to feel embarrassed about, but they do need a doctor's proper diagnosis and a realistic, patient approach to treatment rather than months of hoping a change in nail polish or a pharmacy cream alone will quietly fix it.
